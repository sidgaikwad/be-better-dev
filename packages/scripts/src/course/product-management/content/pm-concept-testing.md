The video's fitness app example screens three ideas down to two, an AI personal trainer and gamified fitness tracking, and tests both. 75% of respondents prefer the AI trainer. Prototype users ask for goal setting, the team adds it with rewards, and the concept moves on. It sounds like strong evidence, but it measures one thing: shown two concepts side by side, three in four people picked the first. If neither is something they would use, one of them still wins 75%.

## From idea to concept

Kotler's textbooks separate a product idea, something the company might offer, from a product concept, the same idea described in the terms a customer would use. Concept testing shows that description to target customers before anything is built. Its written form is a concept statement:

```text
MOVE-IN CHECK
For families whose son or daughter is moving into a Roost room.
On move-in day a Roost verifier meets your child at the room,
checks it against the listing, photographs its condition, and
has the owner sign off on every charge before any money moves.
You get the report on WhatsApp the same day. At move-out, it is
your evidence for the deposit.
Price: ₹999, paid when you book.
```

A concept board adds pictures, here a sample report. A prototype or demo goes further: a report a parent can open and scroll on a phone. The video's advice suits this stage: a Google Form, a designer and a Figma prototype are enough. Test with whoever pays, which for the move-in check is usually a parent, as the buyer and user lesson showed.

## What a survey measures

The usual question is purchase intent on a five-point scale. Suppose 400 families who booked last season answer:

| Answer                     | Share |
| -------------------------- | ----- |
| Definitely would buy       | 18%   |
| Probably would buy         | 32%   |
| Might or might not         | 25%   |
| Probably or definitely not | 25%   |

The tempting headline is "50% would buy". It is weaker than it sounds, for the reasons in the Mom Test lesson: a prediction about your own future costs nothing, and people are kind. Research adds a pattern. Vicki Morwitz, Joel Steckel and Alok Gupta (2007) found that stated intentions track real purchases more closely for existing products than new ones, for specific products than whole categories, and over short horizons than long ones. A new service described in a survey is the weak case on all three. So read the top box, "definitely", and discount even that by a ratio learned from your own past launches.

Focus groups, the video's other tool, are good for why and poor for how many: eight parents in a room tend to follow the loudest one.

## Stated and revealed preference

Economists separate stated preference, what people say, from revealed preference, what their choices show, an idea that goes back to Paul Samuelson in 1938. A concept test can reach revealed preference without a build. Add the move-in check at ₹999 to the Pune checkout for two weeks and have operations write the reports by hand, like the concierge tests in the MVPs without code lesson. The result is a purchase rate at a real price, from the real buyer, at the moment of decision. Set against the survey's top box, it also gives Roost a ratio for reading its next survey.

## Predict, then verify

The two-week test runs: 54 of 600 Pune bookings add the move-in check, 9%. The founder prefers the survey. "Half of families want it. That's 24,000 checks a year out of 48,000 bookings." Which number goes into the business case?

Answer: 9%, which forecasts about 4,320 checks a year, not 24,000. Fifty-four families paid ₹999 at the moment of booking; 200 families ticked a box. Treat even 9% as the high end for now: the best operations staff did the checks, Pune is Roost's most established city, and two weeks is short. The survey still earns its keep: 9% against an 18% top box is a ratio of one half, which Roost can apply to its next concept survey before any test runs. The principle: stated intent sets the ceiling, and behavior at a real price sets the forecast.
