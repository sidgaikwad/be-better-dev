Roost's growth lead reports a clean funnel for July: 40,000 visitors from Instagram ads, 6,000 signups, 1,200 bookings. When the ad budget stops in September, every line goes to zero, because nothing the funnel produced flows back into its top. The video's post-launch step makes the same point: build a system that grows the product on its own, because paid ads alone do not sustain growth. Its examples are Dropbox's referrals, Instagram's shareable content and Slack's usage-based upgrade.

## A loop reinvests its output

Brian Balfour, Casey Winters, Kevin Kwok and Andrew Chen named the alternative in a Reforge essay, "Growth Loops are the New Funnels", in July 2018. A loop is a closed system whose output is reinvested as its next input. They listed three problems with running growth as a funnel:

- It splits strategy into boxes (product, channels, monetization) planned apart.
- It splits teams: marketing optimizes signups, product optimizes retention, and each can win at the other's expense.
- It runs one way, so every cycle needs fresh input bought from outside. Nothing compounds.

Their main example is Pinterest. A user saves pins to boards, Pinterest makes the boards visible to search engines, someone searching Google lands on a board, signs up, and saves pins of their own.

A content loop like that is one family. A viral loop turns users into invitations. A paid loop turns revenue into ads: if a Roost booking earns ₹720 (8% of ₹9,000) and all of it buys ads at ₹900 per booking, each booking buys 0.8 of another. A paid loop compounds only while revenue per customer stays above the cost of acquiring one.

## Roost's review loop

Roost has the raw material for a content loop: verified stays, and the distrust of reviews its early research surfaced.

1. A student books a room through Roost and moves in.
2. Thirty days later, Roost asks for a review with photos, marked as a verified stay.
3. The review appears on the property's page, which search engines index.
4. A student searching "PG near Symbiosis Pune" lands there, reads reviews from real tenants, and signs up.
5. Some of those students book, and the loop starts again.

Suppose Roost's data puts a number on each step. The loop's yield per booking is their product:

```text
reviews per booking          0.20
search visits per review     30 (over the next season)
bookings per visit           0.04
yield = 0.20 x 30 x 0.04   = 0.24 new bookings per booking
Roost's year                 about 48,000 bookings (4,000 a month on average)
loop bookings next season  = 48,000 x 0.24 = 11,520
```

## Where loops break

Because the yield is a product, doubling any step doubles the output, so the question is which step you can move cheaply and actually control, not which has the biggest drop. Roost controls when and how it asks for a review. It barely controls how a search engine ranks a page.

A loop also has a cycle time, set by how often the product is naturally used. Students book once a year, so a review written in September pays off mostly the following June: Roost's loop cannot replace this season's ads, only shrink what next season needs. Loops also decay, as when a search engine changes its ranking. And funnels do not disappear: each step of a loop is a small funnel, measured with the funnels lesson's tools.

## Predict, then verify

Two proposals compete for June. A: a ₹900,000 Instagram campaign expected to bring 1,000 bookings this season. B: two engineers for a month to redesign the review request, lifting the review rate from 20% to 30% and adding nothing this season. Which produces more bookings across this season and the next?

Answer: B, by a wide margin. A adds 1,000 bookings now, and those also feed the loop: 1,000 x 0.24 = 240 next season, 1,240 in total. B raises the yield to 0.30 x 30 x 0.04 = 0.36, so the year's 48,000 bookings produce 48,000 x (0.36 - 0.24) = 5,760 extra bookings next season. A change to a loop step applies to every user who passes through it; a campaign's output is added once. The caveat is timing: if Roost needs this season's bookings to make payroll, A buys survival. Otherwise fund the step that compounds, and measure the new review rate for a month before trusting the 30%.
