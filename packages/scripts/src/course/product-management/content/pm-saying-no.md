A week before the season, four requests arrive. A hostel chain that pays for Featured listings wants deposits hidden from its listings, because "students get scared off". The Bengaluru operations lead wants owner chat, because owners complain about missed calls. A parent writes to support asking for a video call with the owner before paying. The founder, back from a conference, wants video tours of every listing. The team's six weeks are already full.

## Every yes is a no

Capacity is fixed, so a yes to any of these is a no to something already planned, usually one nobody in the room speaks for. Steve Jobs said it at Apple's developer conference in 1997, answering a question about why Apple had killed a promising technology, OpenDoc: "Focusing is about saying no." The job is not to avoid saying it, but to say it in a way others can check.

A no that works has four parts: the goal the work serves, the evidence, what you are doing instead, and what would change your mind. To the operations lead:

```text
Owner chat isn't in this season's plan. The goal is more
requests turning into bookings, and chat scored fourth of
five (1,733): we have no evidence that slow owners reply
faster in chat than on the phone. Availability confirmation
comes first; it should save about 30 bookings a week in July.
If unanswered requests turn out to cost bookings (say over
5% of requests with no reply in two days), chat moves up.
I'll check that number on 15 July and send it to you.
```

A customer's request is evidence about a problem, not a specification: the parent asking for a video call is telling you what the parent page must answer. Sales and operations requests often carry real revenue: score them like anything else. Some requests contradict the strategy outright. Hiding deposits breaks "record every charge", and no score reopens that. Say so plainly, once, and do not dress a strategic no as a capacity problem, or it returns next season.

## The HiPPO

When the request comes from the top, the risk has a name: the HiPPO, the highest-paid person's opinion. Ron Kohavi, Randy Henne and Dan Sommerfield put it in the title of a 2007 paper on online experiments: listen to your customers, not to the HiPPO. The answer is not defiance. Ask what the executive knows that the scores do not. Put the idea through the same inputs as everything else. If they still want it, make it an explicit override with the reason written down. Part 7, The PM in the room, goes further into influence and decision rights.

## Keeping "not now" honest

A "not now" list turns refusals into a record. Each entry holds the item, why not now, the evidence that would reopen it, and a review date, and the list is reviewed every season. An item that has sat on it for three seasons with no evidence moving is a no, and the person who asked deserves to hear that.

The opposite failure is prioritization theater: the decision is made first, then scores are tuned until the sheet agrees. The signs: confidence at 100% with no data behind it, reach counted as every active user, inputs edited right after a meeting. Theater is worse than an open override: it teaches the team that scores are politics, and every later score gets discounted.

## Predict, then verify

The day after the founder mentions video tours, its row in the scoring sheet changes. Reach goes from 6,000 to 60,000, every monthly active student, and confidence from 50% to 100%. With impact 1 and effort 3, the score goes from 6,000 x 1 x 0.5 / 3 = 1,000 to 60,000 x 1 x 1.0 / 3 = 20,000, top of the list. The teammate who edited it says the founder wants it anyway. What do you do?

Answer: Restore the evidence-based inputs and take the choice to the founder as a choice. Sixty thousand is everyone who opens the app, not the students whose booking a video tour would change, and nothing new justifies 100% confidence. If the founder still wants it this season, record an override: video tours go in, availability confirmation moves out, and the sheet says why. The team keeps trusting the scores, and the founder owns a visible trade-off. The principle: an honest override beats a dishonest score, because only one of them keeps the next prioritization meaningful.
