You keep a log of one ordinary week at Roost in March. Here is where 40 hours went:

```text
Chasing status and adjusting the release plan      12 h
Writing and ordering backlog tickets                7 h
Briefing the June social media campaign             6 h
Coordinating the payment gateway's API change       4 h
Meetings that decided nothing                       5 h
Talking to students or owners, reading the data     6 h
Total                                              40 h
```

Six hours went to the part of the job nobody else at Roost can do. The rest is real work, and larger companies hire a separate role for almost every line. Knowing where each role ends tells you what you are carrying and what you could hand off.

## Five roles, five questions

| Role                      | Owns                           | Answers                          |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Product manager           | The problem and the outcome    | Are we building the right thing? |
| Project manager           | Scope, schedule, coordination  | Will it be delivered as planned? |
| Product owner             | The Scrum backlog              | What does the team build next?   |
| Product marketing manager | Positioning, messaging, launch | Will the market understand it?   |
| Technical program manager | Execution across many teams    | Will the pieces fit together?    |

The lines between them:

- **Project manager.** Marty Cagan's 2008 essay "What Product Management Is Not" draws this line as discovery against delivery: the PM finds a product worth building, the project manager gets it built on schedule. The video's instructor says the same from experience: many PMs end up doing a project manager's coordination when the company has none, but timelines and coordination are not the PM's job.
- **Product owner.** In the 2020 Scrum Guide, product owner is not a job title but one of three accountabilities on a Scrum team: maximizing the value of the team's work, including creating and ordering the Product Backlog. The guide insists it is one person, not a committee. In most product companies, the PM holds it.
- **Product marketing manager.** Cagan (2007) splits these cleanly: the PM decides with design and engineering what gets built, and product marketing tells the world about it. Each feeds the other. The PMM knows what the market hears; the PM knows what customers struggled with.
- **Technical program manager.** Common at large tech companies, with no single originator and definitions that vary by employer. A TPM drives programs that span many engineering teams: dependencies, sequencing, technical risk. A TPM usually does not own the why or the what.

## Why the week looks like this

Roost has none of these roles, so you absorb them all, and with five engineers that is normal. The danger is not the absorbing. It is that coordination is urgent and discovery is not. Status requests arrive with a deadline; nobody pings you to go and talk to a student. Left alone, a week fills from the top of that log down.

Two defenses work. Protect a fixed block for the core job, the way engineers protect focus time. And when you get help, split along the line between deciding what to build and delivering it, never through the middle of the what.

## Predict, then verify

The founder offers one hire. Option A is a product owner who writes tickets and orders the backlog while you keep strategy and customers. Option B is a project coordinator who takes the status chasing and the release plan, 12 hours of your week. Which do you take?

Answer: B. Option A cuts through the middle of the what. The engineers would take direction from someone who has not talked to students, so every question about why ("does the price need a breakdown by charge?") travels to the product owner, then to you, then back. Cagan made this argument in 2011: split the jobs and you get one person with customer knowledge but no feel for the technology, another with the reverse, and no clear owner of the result. Option B takes work that is real but separable and returns 12 hours, which triples your discovery time from 6 hours to 18 if you protect it. The principle: hand off delivery work freely, and keep the decisions about what to build in one head.
