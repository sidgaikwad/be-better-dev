Intellipaat's instructor surveyed his own learners about what bothered them: slow support, operational problems, too little practical knowledge, uneven training quality, placements. Asked which need to solve first, many in his class picked support or training quality. He disagreed. What his learners mostly want, he said, is a higher income. If the product raises every learner's earnings, it has succeeded, and support tickets rank far lower.

A customer's complaints are not the value they are buying. Value is the progress they want, and a problem is worth solving in proportion to how much it stands in the way. The jobs to be done section called that progress the job; this lesson ranks what to build against it.

## The video's five steps

The video lays value creation out in five steps:

1. **Identify needs** through interviews, surveys, feedback and observation.
2. **Prioritize needs by value**: must-haves, nice-to-haves, differentiators.
3. **Define the value proposition**: which need you solve, for whom, and how.
4. **Design solutions**, testing prototypes before a full launch.
5. **Learn and iterate**, because needs shift once the first fix lands.

Its example is a fitness app whose users struggle to stay motivated and to vary their workouts. The team sorts its options with the Kano model (tracking is basic, personalized plans performance, gamified rewards excitement) and ships personalized plans first. After launch, analytics show people like the plans but still skip workouts, so a social accountability feature ships next: step 5 at work.

## Ranking by value, worked

Step 2 is the one teams skip, and it needs a yardstick: the customer's goal, stated as an outcome. Ask how far each unsolved need sets it back. For a Roost student and the parent who pays, the goal is to move into a safe room that matches what the family paid for, before term.

| Need                              | If it stays unsolved                                   | Rank |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---- |
| Room matches its listing          | Goal fails: ₹27,000 paid upfront for the wrong room    | 1    |
| Every charge shown before booking | Goal met at a cost: an unlisted charge averages ₹3,500 | 2    |
| Pay in instalments                | Goal delayed for families without ₹27,000 at once      | 3    |
| Faster chat replies               | Goal met, with friction                                | 4    |

Chat speed can be the loudest complaint and still rank last: a slow reply annoys, a wrong room ends the goal.

## Co-creation

The video's last idea is co-creation: bringing customers into building the product and its message. C.K. Prahalad and Venkat Ramaswamy named the shift in "Co-opting Customer Competence" (Harvard Business Review, January 2000): customers had become a source of competence, not only a market. The video lists the gains: features aligned with real needs, customers who feel ownership and spread the word, their own words for your messaging (its skincare brand learns users say "calming" and "refreshing"), trust, and fewer untested assumptions.

LEGO Ideas adds guardrails. Fans post set designs; one that gathers 10,000 supporters goes to LEGO for review, LEGO decides what ships, and a chosen creator earns 1% of the set's net sales. The threshold and review exist because co-creators select themselves. Roost should redesign the charges form with a dozen owners, but volunteers are its most engaged owners, not the 54 a month who abandon the listing form. Co-create to learn the need and the words; keep the ranking yourself.

## Predict, then verify

Roost surveys 180 owners. 46% name slow payouts as their top complaint: Roost pays an owner ₹8,280 (₹9,000 rent minus the ₹720 commission) 7 days after move-in, and a 2-day payout takes one engineer a month. Only 12% mention empty rooms, yet an empty bed costs an owner ₹9,000 / 30 = ₹300 a day. A vacancy alert to students who saved a locality filled beds about 3 days sooner in a pilot, also one engineer-month. Which do you build?

Answer: The vacancy alert. An owner wants what the instructor's learners want: more income. Paying ₹8,280 five days sooner only moves money they already have; at a generous 12% a year that is ₹8,280 x 0.12 x 5 / 365 = about ₹14. Filling a bed 3 days sooner earns 3 x ₹300 = ₹900 they would otherwise never see, about 64 times as much. The principle: rank needs by how much solving them moves the customer's goal, not by how often they are named.
