Two students open Roost on the same June evening. Both are 18, study engineering in Pune, and budget ₹9,000. On a demographic dashboard they are one customer. One books a verified room in three days. The other browses for five weeks and never books.

The first learned that morning that she has no college hostel seat, classes start in twelve days, and she is 700 km away in Nagpur. The second already lives in a Pune PG and is browsing because her roommate is noisy. Same profile, different job.

## Hiring for progress

Clayton Christensen's name for this is the job to be done. _Competing Against Luck_ (2016, with Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David S. Duncan) defines a job as the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. People do not buy products, in this view. They hire them to make that progress, and fire them when they stop helping.

Teams tend to drop the circumstance, yet change it and the job changes, even for the same person. Jobs also have functional, social and emotional sides: a room near campus, parents who agree it was sensible, and not being the student who got cheated in week one.

```text
Profile:  18, first-year, engineering, from Nagpur, budget ₹9,000
Job:      get a safe room near campus fixed before classes start,
          from 700 km away, in a way my parents will sign off on
Fired:    the WhatsApp group (rooms gone by the time she calls)
          and a broker (the quote left out a maintenance charge)
```

The profile says whom to advertise to. The job says what to build and what you are up against, and it outlasts solutions: students needed a room near campus in the era of notice boards and will need one after today's apps are gone.

## The milkshake, told as a parable

The best-known example first appeared in print in "Marketing Malpractice" (HBR, 2005, with Scott Cook and Taddy Hall). A fast-food chain improved its milkshake on customer feedback, and sales did not move. Researchers watched a restaurant for a day: many shakes went early in the morning, to people alone, as their only purchase, carried to the car. Asked what they hired on other mornings, commuters named bananas, bagels and doughnuts. The job was to make a dull drive bearable and stay full until mid-morning, one-handed, and a thicker shake does it better. Afternoon buyers were parents treating children, a job a thinner shake serves better. One product, two jobs, opposite fixes.

Tell it as a parable, because the record is thin:

- The numbers differ. Christensen's 2016 talk, published as an HBR podcast, says about half were sold before 8:30. A 2011 Harvard Business School summary says 40 percent.
- His written accounts never name the chain. In that talk he named McDonald's and claimed a sevenfold sales rise where the changes shipped, but no region, period or data was ever published.
- He credits "one of my colleagues". Alan Klement credits the fieldwork to Bob Moesta and Rick Pedi, consultants whose client wanted to sell more milkshakes.

It illustrates the idea. It does not prove the method pays.

The course's instructor uses Uber, which in the video "solves the job of convenient and affordable transportation". Without a circumstance, that is a category rather than a job. The video's roadmap segment gets closer with "hiring Uber to avoid waiting for a taxi in the rain": the rain is the circumstance. Add one like it, getting home at midnight from a street with no taxi stand, and the job says what matters: a car that comes, at a known fare.

## Predict, then verify

A survey of Roost's 60,000 monthly active students finds 62% want "more listings, including unverified ones, so I can compare". First-year students moving from another city are about 30% of active students but about 58% of the 4,000 monthly bookings. Should search include unverified listings?

Answer: No. First-years are 18,000 students making 2,320 bookings (58% of 4,000), a 12.9% monthly rate. The other 42,000 make 1,680, a 4% rate. The survey counts heads, so it speaks mostly for a different job: students already in the city, able to inspect a room. For the first-year's job an unverified room is worse than none: the room she cannot see is the one she must trust. If the change cost first-years a fifth of their bookings (464), everyone else would need 28% more (464 / 1,680) to break even. As with the thick and thin shake, an average of two jobs serves neither. Segment by job, then build for the one that pays.
