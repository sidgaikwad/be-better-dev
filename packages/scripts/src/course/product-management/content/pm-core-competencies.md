Roost's investor deck has a slide called "Our core competencies" with nine bullets, from "verified listings" and "a strong brand among students" to "1,200 properties" and "data". The term's inventors would send it back. Most bullets are the wrong kind: 1,200 properties is an asset, a brand is an outcome, an app is a product.

## Roots, not leaves

In "The Core Competence of the Corporation" (HBR, May-June 1990), C.K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel define core competencies as "the collective learning in the organization", especially in coordinating skills and blending technologies. They picture a company as a tree. End products are the leaves, business units the branches, core products the trunk, and competencies the roots. Honda's competence in engines and power trains gave it an advantage in cars, motorcycles, lawn mowers and generators.

They offer three tests:

1. It provides potential access to a wide variety of markets.
2. It makes a significant contribution to the benefits customers perceive in the end product.
3. It is difficult for competitors to imitate: a complex blend of skills, not one technology a rival can buy.

Few companies, they add, lead the world in more than five or six; a list of twenty or thirty is probably not a list of core competencies.

The video keeps value and inimitability but swaps the first test for uniqueness, with Amazon's logistics, data analytics and technology as its example. The swap loses the point: breadth is what makes a competence corporate, feeding many products, and separates it from a good feature. Its long list of Indian companies has Roost's slide problem: "strong brand reputation" is a result, not a root.

## Roost's verification, tested

Roost's operations team visits every property before listing, at about ₹600, and after complaints at ₹1,000.

| Test             | Evidence                                                                                   | Verdict     |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------- |
| Customer benefit | "Parents won't pay until someone has seen the room": 9 of 14 interviews                    | Pass        |
| Many markets     | Carries to new cities; flats took twice as long to verify in the young professionals pilot | Partial     |
| Hard to imitate  | A funded rival can visit 1,200 properties for 1,200 × ₹600 = ₹720,000                      | Fail, today |

The visits are an activity, and money buys activities. What money cannot buy quickly is what the visits could teach. Each month about 3,600 students move in and 360 of them raise a dispute within 30 days. Record every verifier's findings, join them to what happened after move-in, and Roost learns which findings predict a dispute: an owner who will not show the electricity meter, a shared bathroom the owner would not open. A checklist refined against three seasons of outcomes blends field judgment, data and support; a rival starting today needs three seasons to match it. Roost could have that competence. It does not yet.

## From competence to moat

Hamilton Helmer's _7 Powers_ (2016) sets a stricter bar. A power needs a benefit, lower cost or a higher price, and a barrier that stops rivals copying it. His process power fits Roost's candidate: ways of working embedded so deeply that rivals match them only after a long commitment, as with Toyota's production system. In his framing, operational excellence alone is not power, because rivals can copy it.

Prahalad and Hamel's sharpest warning is about outsourcing: the skills behind the next product cannot be rented in from suppliers. Chrysler treated engines as one more component, and its outsourced engines rose from 252,000 to 382,000 between 1985 and 1987. Honda kept engines in-house.

## Predict, then verify

An inspection company offers to do Roost's visits for ₹450 instead of ₹600, and it already works in Chennai, the city Roost wants next. Roost makes about 2,000 routine visits a year. The vendor also sells to rival apps. Do you outsource?

Answer: No. The saving is 2,000 × (₹600 - ₹450) = 2,000 × ₹150 = ₹300,000 a year, under 1% of Roost's ₹34,560,000 in yearly commission. What goes with it is the one activity that passes the customer-benefit test, and the only place the learning could grow. Outsourced, the checklist and field judgment belong to a vendor who sells them to every rival. For Chennai, hire and train local verifiers. Outsource what fails the tests: photo editing, hosting, payment processing. The principle: never rent in a candidate core competence to save money; the saving is small, and the loss is why customers choose you.
