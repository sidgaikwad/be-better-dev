Go back to the season before Roost sold Featured listings, the paid placement that puts an owner's property at the top of search results for its area. The product is built, and three people in the planning meeting propose three prices.

The engineering lead has costed it: a fresh verification visit, new photos and some support time come to ₹600 a month. Add a 50% markup and Featured costs ₹900.

The Pune city lead checked the market: Roost's two rivals there charge ₹1,200 and ₹1,800 a month for a top slot, so she proposes ₹1,500.

You have pilot data. The previous June to August, 40 Pune owners got Featured free as a test, and their vacant beds filled 12 days sooner on average than comparable unfeatured beds.

## Three questions, three prices

Your price needs arithmetic, from the owner's side:

```text
Rent per bed:                ₹9,000 a month, about ₹300 a day
Days sooner a bed fills:     12
Value per bed:               12 x ₹300 = ₹3,600
Vacant beds, typical buyer:  5
Value to the owner:          5 x ₹3,600 = ₹18,000 per month of Featured
```

| Method           | Asks                           | Price  |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ | ------ |
| Cost-plus        | What does it cost us?          | ₹900   |
| Competitor-based | What do rivals charge?         | ₹1,500 |
| Value-based      | What is it worth to the owner? | ₹4,000 |

Cost-plus is easy to defend and answers a question no customer asks. Cost is a floor: below ₹600, every sale loses money. Above the floor it says nothing about what an owner would pay, and at ₹900 an owner who gains ₹18,000 keeps ₹17,100 of it.

Competitor-based pricing is better, because owners do compare. But it inherits the rivals' guesses, and it assumes your top slot delivers what theirs does.

Value-based pricing starts from the customer's gain against their next-best alternative. John Forbis and Nitin Mehta formalized it in 1981 as economic value to the customer (EVC): the price of the next-best alternative, called the reference value, plus the worth of everything you do better, the differentiation value. Against the simplest alternative, a free plain listing, EVC is ₹0 plus ₹18,000.

Why ₹4,000 and not ₹15,000? The ₹18,000 is an average, and an owner with one vacant bed gains only ₹3,600. Owners have not seen the pilot and will judge by what they believe. And an owner who keeps most of the gain buys again next June. ₹4,000 leaves the typical buyer ₹14,000 and still earns more than four times the cost-plus price.

## Price is part of the product

Value-based pricing is the hardest of the three because it needs evidence: everything above rests on 12 days, measured on 40 owners in one city. The next lesson measures willingness to pay directly.

It also shapes the product in ways cost-plus never surfaces:

- **Season.** Featured costs Roost the same in November as in July, but is worth far less when almost nobody is searching. Sell it for the June to August peak, not year-round.
- **Scarcity.** A featured owner's faster fill comes partly out of the neighbors' bookings. If half the owners in a locality buy Featured, nobody stands out and the 12 days shrinks toward zero. Capping Featured slots per locality is a pricing decision.
- **What you charge for.** The course's instructor gives Priya's hostel app a commission plus charges for verified or promoted listings. Roost charges only for promotion. Verification is the promise students rely on; if owners could skip it by not paying, the badge would mean nothing anywhere.

## Predict, then verify

The city lead objects: owners can buy a rival's top slot for about ₹1,500. Owners who have used one say it fills a bed about 4 days sooner, since rivals reach far fewer students than Roost's 60,000 a month. She wants Featured cut to ₹1,500. Do you cut it?

Answer: No. Rerun EVC with the rival as the next-best alternative. The reference value is its price, ₹1,500. The differentiation value is the extra 8 days (12 minus 4) on 5 beds at ₹300 a day: 8 x 5 x ₹300 = ₹12,000. EVC is ₹1,500 plus ₹12,000, or ₹13,500, far above ₹4,000, so the typical owner still does better with Roost. A rival matching the 12 days would force a cut: the differentiation value would fall to zero and EVC to ₹1,500. The principle: respond to a competitor's outcome, not its price. Value-based pricing does not ignore rivals; it prices them in as the reference value.
