In March, Roost's operations lead forwards a request repeated in 212 support messages: let students message the owner straight from a listing. The engineers have slack before the admissions rush, and chat takes three weeks. It ships in April.

By late May, students have started 5,100 chats, and still only 5 of every 100 who open a listing book. Worse, a steady share of chats end with a phone number, and a booking agreed on the phone earns Roost nothing. The engineers built what was asked, on time. Nobody asked why students wanted to message owners.

## Roost, the product you will run

Roost is the product this course follows, and in most lessons you are its product manager. These facts hold throughout:

- A marketplace app that helps college students in India find verified hostels and PG (paying guest) rooms near campus. Students book; property owners list.
- Live in Pune, Bengaluru and Hyderabad: about 1,200 properties, 60,000 monthly active students, around 4,000 bookings a month. Most bookings land in June to August, before the academic year.
- Average rent is ₹9,000 a month. Roost earns 8% of the first month's rent on each booking, plus fees from owners for Featured listings.
- The team: you, a designer, five engineers, and an operations team that visits properties to verify them.
- Early research found fake listings, hidden charges, safety worries, reviews nobody trusted, and a wish to pay in instalments. Parents often pay, so the buyer is not always the user.

One number to keep: a booking earns 8% of ₹9,000, or ₹720, so 4,000 bookings bring in ₹2,880,000 a month. Every booking that moved to the phone lowered it.

Roost borrows its premise from the story that opens Intellipaat's free [Product Management Course](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=abA-QZzbon0) (2025), which this course credits throughout. There, a founder named Arjun hires developers for a student hostel app, they ask questions he cannot answer, and he brings in Priya, a product manager, who starts by talking to students and owners.

## The why and the what, not the how

The video's instructor is blunt: a PM does not code, design, market or sell, and is "only paid to think". His explanation is the chat story: without a PM, the business team hands a solution straight to engineering, engineering builds it, and the customer is still suffering.

So a PM owns two things. The why: which problem, for whom, and why now. The what: the outcome a solution must produce. The how, the screens and the code, belongs to the designer and engineers. Here is the work that should have come before chat:

```text
Request:   "Let students message the owner from the listing."
From:      Operations lead, citing 212 support messages
Evidence:  Read 60 of them. The first question in 41:
             "What will I actually pay each month?"   (23)
             "Is the room really still available?"     (18)
Problem:   Students do not trust the price or availability on a
           listing, so they need a person to confirm both.
Outcome:   More listing views become bookings on Roost.
Options:   Chat. An all-inclusive price owners must fill in.
           Availability owners confirm every week.
```

Chat stays on the list, now as one option judged against a stated problem.

## Where the habit goes wrong

Asking why is not a license to interrogate every request. Sometimes the request is the answer, such as a legal requirement or an obvious bug, and the person asking often knows something you do not. Match the digging to the cost of the build: a two-hour change can just ship, while a three-week build earns a day of reading the evidence first.

## Predict, then verify

Three hostel chains with 150 of Roost's 1,200 listings tell the founder they will leave unless listings show the owner's phone number. Their properties produce about 500 bookings a month. What do you do?

Answer: Neither refuse nor build yet. The phone number moves bookings off Roost: if a quarter of those 500 went to the phone, 125 × ₹720 = ₹90,000 of commission is lost each month. Losing the chains costs 500 × ₹720 = ₹360,000 a month, so the threat is serious. Spend two days asking their managers why. If enquiries reach them a day late, the problem is speed, and an instant alert to the manager fixes it without giving the booking away. The principle: a request is evidence of a problem, and the PM finds the problem before choosing the solution.
