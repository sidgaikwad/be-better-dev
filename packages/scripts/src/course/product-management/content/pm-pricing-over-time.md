On 24 July 2019 Netflix launched a new plan in India: ₹199 a month for one phone or tablet, in standard definition. Its cheapest plan until then cost ₹499. The course's instructor cites it as a price built for a market's low willingness to pay. Netflix's shareholder letter that month was more specific: after several months of testing, the plan would bring more Indians to Netflix in a market where pay TV earned under $5 per subscriber. Pay TV, and Hotstar's ₹999-a-year premium tier, set the reference price.

The phone-only limit is a fence in the tiers lesson's sense: it reached phone-first viewers without cutting the price for television viewers. It bought time. On 14 December 2021 Netflix cut all four Indian plans: Mobile from ₹199 to ₹149, Basic from ₹499 to ₹199, Standard from ₹649 to ₹499 and Premium from ₹799 to ₹649. Its next shareholder letter said India "is fairly unique because pay TV pricing is very low." Basic, which includes the television, now cost what the phone-only plan had cost at launch. The customer's reference price won.

## Skimming or penetration

The introduction and growth lesson named both. Here is how to choose.

**Price skimming** launches high, to the buyers who want the product most, then steps down to reach the next segment. Apple sold the first iPhone from 29 June 2007 at $499 for 4 GB and $599 for 8 GB. On 5 September it cut the 8 GB model to $399 and dropped the 4 GB one, and after hundreds of complaints Steve Jobs offered early buyers a $100 store credit. Skimming works when early buyers value the product most and rivals cannot copy it quickly. Its cost: early buyers who feel punished by each cut.

**Penetration pricing** launches low to win share fast. It pays when being big makes the product better or cheaper to run: network effects, economies of scale. Its cost is that the low price becomes the reference, and every later increase is a fight.

For Roost, the answer differs by side. Students pay nothing, and every student makes the app more valuable to owners, so the student side is priced for penetration. Featured is the opposite: it is worth more the fewer owners have it.

## Free is a price too

Three ways to use it:

- **Freemium:** a free tier forever, with paid features on top. Roost's owner side already works this way: listing is free, Featured is paid.
- **Free trial:** the paid product for a limited time, after which the customer pays or leaves.
- **Reverse trial:** every new customer starts on the paid tier for a limited time, then drops to the free tier instead of leaving. Elena Verna described it for Amplitude in 2022 as combining a trial's urgency with freemium's continued use.

Verna's benchmarks for software: about 5% of freemium users convert to paid, about 15% of people who start a trial without entering a card, and 70 to 80% of those who start a trial that requires one, though far fewer start. They are software numbers, not laws, but show a trade: the more a model asks up front, the fewer people start and the more of them pay.

## Predict, then verify

In Featured's first season, 150 of Roost's 1,200 owners bought it at ₹4,000 a month. For the second season, one teammate proposes penetration: cut Featured to ₹1,000 and expect buyers to double to 300. Another proposes keeping ₹4,000 and giving each of the roughly 200 owners who join this year a reverse trial of Featured. Which do you choose?

Answer: The reverse trial. Penetration gives 300 x ₹1,000 = ₹300,000 a month against the first season's 150 x ₹4,000 = ₹600,000. Breaking even needs 600 buyers, half of all owners, and at that density Featured stops standing out, so the product disappears as people buy it. It also makes ₹1,000 the reference, turning any return to ₹4,000 into a 300% increase. The reverse trial keeps the price for the 150 who pay it and lets new owners see their own faster bookings. If they convert like Verna's opt-in trials, 15%, then 30 of 200 pay: 30 x ₹4,000 = ₹120,000 more, for ₹720,000 a month. Run every trial from mid-May to mid-June, whatever month the owner joined: a November trial shows almost no students, and trial slots count against the locality cap. Cut price when volume makes the product better; when it is worth more the fewer people have it, cut the barrier to trying it instead.
