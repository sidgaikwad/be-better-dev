The video's instructor has a rule for which framework to show to whom. With his team, RICE. With a chief product officer, a VP or the CEO, a two-by-two of value against effort, because RICE's inputs are more detail than that meeting wants. The calculations still exist; they sit behind the chart, ready if someone asks.

## Four quadrants

Plot value on the vertical axis and effort on the horizontal, draw a line across each, and every item lands in one of four boxes:

- **Quick wins**: high value, low effort. Do these first.
- **Big bets**: high value, high effort. Worth doing, but only as a deliberate commitment.
- **Fill-ins**: low value, low effort. Do them when capacity is free.
- **Money pits**: low value, high effort. Avoid them.

The video labels the low-value, low-effort box "low-hanging fruit". Most practitioners use that phrase for quick wins, so the label causes confusion in exactly the executive meeting the chart is for; "fill-ins" is clearer. No originator for the matrix can be traced. It is a facilitation device, not a method, and should be judged as one.

Here are Roost's five candidates from the RICE lesson, with value taken as reach x impact x confidence and effort in person-months:

| Feature                   | Value  | Effort | Quadrant      |
| ------------------------- | ------ | ------ | ------------- |
| Availability confirmation | 10,816 | 2      | Quick win     |
| Map pin fix               | 2,600  | 0.5    | Fill-in       |
| "Ask a senior" reviews    | 7,500  | 3      | Big bet       |
| Instalments               | 6,000  | 4      | Big bet       |
| Owner chat                | 5,200  | 3      | Big bet, just |

The lines are drawn at value 5,000 and effort 2.5. Move the value line to 5,500 and owner chat drops from big bet to money pit without a single estimate changing. Where the lines go is a decision, and on a slide it looks like a fact.

## ICE, the quicker cousin

Sean Ellis, who coined "growth hacking", scored growth experiments with ICE: impact, confidence and ease, each from 1 to 10. He and Morgan Brown spread it through _Hacking Growth_ (2017). It is fast, and it was built for many small experiments rather than a few large features.

Its weak spot is that teams combine the three numbers differently. Some average them and some multiply them, and the two methods can disagree:

```text
Idea X: I 9, C 9, E 1   average (9+9+1)/3 = 6.3   product 9x9x1 = 81
Idea Y: I 5, C 5, E 5   average (5+5+5)/3 = 5.0   product 5x5x5 = 125
```

Averaging puts X first; multiplying puts Y first, because one low factor drags a product down far more than an average. Pick one method, write it at the top of the sheet, and never compare scores between teams that chose differently.

## What the chart hides

The matrix folds confidence into value, so a moonshot and a certainty can sit side by side. It shows no dependencies and no order inside a quadrant. And it says nothing about time: a quick win that must ship before June and one that could wait until November sit in the same box. The instructor's split is right. Use the matrix to agree on direction with people who will not read the table, and keep the table for the decisions it supports.

## Predict, then verify

At the season review, the CEO looks at the chart and says quick wins are small thinking. The team has six person-months; put all of it into the two big bets, "Ask a senior" and instalments. They need 3 + 4 = 7, so one would not finish. Your plan is availability confirmation and the pin fix first, 2.5 person-months, then "Ask a senior", 3 more. Who is right?

Answer: Your plan. The quick win and the fill-in cost 2.5 person-months and carry 10,816 + 2,600 = 13,416 of value, more than twice what instalments carries (6,000) for 4 person-months. Your plan delivers 13,416 + 7,500 = 20,916 in 5.5 person-months. The CEO's delivers "Ask a senior" at 7,500 plus a half-built instalments feature that ships nothing this season. Big bets are not wrong, but they are chosen for strategy, not for size: "Ask a senior" attacks the trust problem Roost's strategy names, so it is the bet to make. The principle: take the cheap value first, then place one deliberate bet, rather than letting ambition set the order.
