A board member forwards Facebook's results for December 2012, 618 million daily active users against 1.06 billion monthly, and compares them with Roost's June: 6,750 daily active students against 90,000 monthly. Roost looks eight times less sticky, and the email asks for a plan to close the gap.

The right plan is not to chase that ratio at all.

## The ratio and what it measures

DAU/MAU divides average daily actives by monthly actives. Multiply it by the days in the month and you get the number of days a typical monthly user was active:

```text
Facebook, Dec 2012:  618M / 1,060M = 58%     x 30 days = about 17 days
Roost, June:         6,750 / 90,000 = 7.5%   x 30 days = about 2 days
```

June's 90,000 is above Roost's usual 60,000 because students search before the academic year. Facebook's ratio describes a need that recurs every day. A student's housing need recurs once a year and lasts a few weeks.

Andrew Chen, a growth investor at a16z, said on an a16z podcast in August 2018 that some products have a low DAU/MAU for good reason, a natural cadence: nobody uses a travel product more than a couple of times a year. His 2019 list of product-market fit signals does include DAU/MAU above 50%, tagged as the sign of a daily habit.

## Measure at the natural frequency

A product's natural frequency is how often the underlying need comes back: many times a day for messaging, weekdays for work tools, weekly for groceries. Measure stickiness at that cadence. Roost has two sides with two frequencies:

- **Students** need Roost for a few weeks a year. In season, the useful number is how fast searching students book: the share who book within 21 days of their first search. Across seasons, it is the share of last year's bookers who come back the next May or June.
- **Owners** get inquiries daily in season, so their cadence is weekly: the share of listed owners who answer every inquiry within 24 hours in a given week.

## The power user curve

A ratio averages away the shape. Andrew Chen's July 2018 post "The Power User Curve" plots the distribution: a histogram of monthly actives by how many days each was active. He credits the name L30 to Facebook's growth team, and on the podcast called the 28-day version L28. A social product shows a smile, with one bump at a single day and a second at 20 or more days, the daily core. On the same podcast, Jeff Jordan said Facebook, WhatsApp and Instagram all had one.

Roost's June histogram leans hard left. Add a booking column:

| Days active in June | Students | Booked |
| ------------------- | -------- | ------ |
| 1                   | 52,000   | 3%     |
| 2 to 3              | 25,000   | 12%    |
| 4 to 9              | 11,000   | 35%    |
| 10 or more          | 2,000    | 20%    |

The students with the most days book less often than the middle band. In a search product, a long streak of visits often means the search is failing: nothing in the student's budget near campus, or listings the student does not trust. A campaign that pushed students rightward on this chart would manufacture the stuck group. Roost wants fewer days to a booking, not more days in the app.

## Predict, then verify

A year later, June's DAU/MAU has fallen from 7.5% to 6%. Meanwhile, the median time from first search to booking dropped from 9 days to 6, and the share of searching students who book rose from 10% to 11%. The board member calls the ratio a stickiness crisis and wants a retention squad on it. Is it a crisis?

Answer: no, it is the product working. At 90,000 monthly actives, 7.5% is 6,750 students a day and about 2.25 active days per student; 6% is 5,400 a day and about 1.8 days. Students spend fewer days in Roost because they find a room sooner, and more of them book. A squad paid to restore 7.5% would have to make searches longer again. The right response is to change the report: replace student DAU/MAU with time to book and booking rate in season, the return rate of last year's bookers across seasons, and weekly owner response for the supply side. A stickiness ratio is a goal only when the product's value recurs at its frequency; for a job that ends, falling engagement can be good news.
