OpenAI was founded in December 2015. For almost seven years its models drew mostly researchers and developers. ChatGPT launched on November 30, 2022, and by January 2023, UBS analysts estimated, it had 100 million monthly users, the fastest ramp they could recall for a consumer internet app. The course's instructor reads this as the curve turning steep, and predicts that within five years people will stop using Excel and Word, because a chatbot will do the basic work. Two different curves sit behind that forecast.

## Two S-curves

1. **The technology curve.** Richard Foster, a McKinsey director, described it in _Innovation: The Attacker's Advantage_ (1986): performance plotted against cumulative effort. Early effort buys little, because the basics are not yet understood. Then each unit of effort buys large gains. Near the technology's natural limit, each gain costs more than the last.
2. **The adoption curve.** Everett Rogers, in _Diffusion of Innovations_ (1962), plotted the share of a population that has adopted something over time: slow, then steep, then flat as few non-adopters remain.

The instructor runs the two together, calling the curve innovation performance, then using it to advise investors on when to enter a market. Keep them apart: the first says when a technology will be good enough, the second when a market will be big enough.

## Jumping to the next curve

Foster's argument is about the gap between curves. A new technology starts below the old one's performance, on its own curve. The incumbent keeps improving the old technology near its limit, where gains are expensive; the attacker improves the new one in its steep middle, where they are cheap. In one of Foster's cases, DuPont kept backing nylon tire cord in the 1960s while Celanese, with no nylon business to protect, moved to polyester. The instructor's example runs the same way: personal computers started far weaker than minicomputers and improved fast, and in 1998 Digital Equipment, the minicomputer leader, was bought by Compaq, a PC maker, for $9.6 billion.

Roost's supply runs on one curve today: a verifier visits about four properties a day, at about ₹600 a visit. A second curve is visible. Roost has scored an automated check of photos and documents against its verifiers' visits for three seasons:

```text
Problems a visit found that the automated check also found
Season 1    30%
Season 2    55%
Season 3    75%
Cost per check: visit ₹600, automated ₹25
```

## Where the curve misleads

You cannot see your position on an S-curve from inside it. The early stretch of an S looks exactly like exponential growth, and the bend shows only in hindsight. Extrapolating the steep part bets that the limit is far away, so find the limit instead. Photos can show a room, but not a charge the owner adds at move-in or what the street is like at night. The instructor's five-year forecast is a bet of the same kind. And his advice to enter on the steep part has a price: by then everyone can see the curve, and the field is crowded.

## Predict, then verify

The founder points at the table, 30%, 55%, 75%, so 90% next season, and wants to replace the yearly re-verification visits to all 1,200 properties with automated checks, saving 1,200 × (₹600 - ₹25) = ₹690,000 a year. Re-verification finds a problem at about 10% of properties, and a property averages 40 bookings a year (48,000 across 1,200). Do you switch?

Answer: not for the promise, only for the parts where the new curve is already good enough. At 75%, the check misses a quarter of problems: 10% of 1,200 is 120 problem properties, and a quarter of those is 30, carrying 30 × 40 = 1,200 bookings in rooms Roost says it checked. The saving works out to ₹690,000 / 1,200 = ₹575 per such booking, less than the ₹720 each one earns, before counting the trust it burns. The 90% forecast assumes the steep middle continues, but the problems photos miss sit past the method's limit. Run the check as a first pass before every visit, and monthly on listings that today get no check between visits. Move the promise only when the check matches visits on what parents care about: charges and safety. The principle: start the next curve before the current one flattens, but switch only when the new curve crosses the old one on the customer's measure.
