In one month, Roost made two decisions. One put recently visited listings at the top of search results. It sat behind a flag that could be switched off in an hour, and took three weeks and three meetings. The other promised Chennai's first 150 owners a year without commission. It was agreed on a fifteen-minute call and sent that evening. The careful process went to the decision Roost could undo, and the quick one to a promise it could not take back.

## Type 1 and Type 2

Jeff Bezos named this pattern in Amazon's 2015 letter to shareholders. Some decisions are consequential and irreversible, or nearly so: one-way doors. Once through, you "can't get back to where you were before". These Type 1 decisions deserve slow deliberation. Most decisions are Type 2, two-way doors: choose badly and you walk back through. Those should be made fast, by individuals or small groups. The trap is a growing company using the heavy Type 1 process for everything, which he said leads to "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently".

His 2016 letter added a rule for the two-way kind: decide with about 70% of the information you wish you had, because waiting for 90% is usually too slow. If you are good at correcting course, being wrong costs less than you think, while being slow is expensive for sure.

Three questions sort a decision:

1. What would undoing it cost, in money and in trust?
2. Who would notice the reversal?
3. How soon would we know it was wrong?

| Roost decision                            | Cost to undo                  | Door                 |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Recently visited listings first in search | Flip a flag                   | Two-way              |
| Next season's Featured price              | Re-notify 60 owners           | Two-way, with a cost |
| A year of free commission in Chennai      | Break a promise to a new city | One-way              |
| Listing unvisited rooms to open faster    | Families meet fake listings   | One-way              |

The last row is one-way because Roost's obstacle is trust, and trust spent on a fake listing does not return when you delist it. Reversible in code is not reversible in trust: a flag can take a price off the screen, not out of owners' memories. When a door looks one-way, look for a design that makes it two-way: a pilot in one locality, or an offer written as one season from the start.

## Disagree and commit

Deciding fast means deciding without agreement. The 2016 letter's answer: "disagree and commit": state your view plainly, then back the decision fully. Bezos applied it to himself. He doubted an Amazon Studios original, said why, and when the team still wanted it, wrote that he disagreed and committed and hoped it would be their most watched show.

The idea is older than Amazon, which made it a leadership principle in 2010 or 2011. Scott McNealy was using the phrase at Sun Microsystems by 1991, and Andy Grove's _High Output Management_ (1983) describes the same sequence at Intel: free discussion, a clear decision, full support.

It breaks when leaders invoke it to cut debate short, skipping Grove's first step, or when people claim to commit while quietly working against the decision, skipping his third. And when two teams hold truly different goals, Bezos says escalate early, or the decision goes to whoever has more stamina.

## Predict, then verify

Ninety Bengaluru listings were last visited over a year ago. Last month they had 60 bookings and 9 disputes, a 15% dispute rate against Roost's usual 9%. The operations lead wants all 90 delisted until re-visited, which at ₹600 a visit costs ₹54,000 and about six weeks. The city lead says delisting will drive owners away. What do you decide, and how fast?

Answer: Decide this week, without delisting. Delisting is close to one-way: the parent page launch already had 11 owners asking to leave, and a delisted owner may not return. Leaving them as they are keeps families facing 15%. So make the door two-way: keep the 90 listed with their visit date shown, rank them below recently visited rooms behind a flag, and re-visit them in order of disputes. The flag risks at most part of 60 x ₹720 = ₹43,200 a month and reverses in an hour. If the operations lead still disagrees, the lead disagrees and commits, with a shared check on bookings and disputes in two weeks. The principle: match decision speed to reversibility, and when a door looks one-way, look for the design that makes it two-way.
