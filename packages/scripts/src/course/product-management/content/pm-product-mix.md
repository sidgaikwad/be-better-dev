The course's instructor opens this topic with Marico, the Mumbai company behind Parachute coconut oil. Its brands span hair care (Parachute, Nihar Naturals, Livon), edible oils and healthy foods (Saffola), men's grooming (Set Wet, Beardo) and fabric care (Revive). A hair-oil company that also sells cooking oil, oats and fabric starch sounds unfocused. Four measurements describe a mix like this one, and one of them explains it.

## Four dimensions

The vocabulary comes from Philip Kotler's marketing textbooks: _Marketing Management_, first published in 1967, and _Principles of Marketing_, written with Gary Armstrong. A company's product mix is everything it sells, measured four ways:

- **Width:** the number of product lines. A line is a group of closely related products sold to similar customers through similar channels.
- **Length:** the total number of items across all lines. Divided by width, it gives the average length of a line.
- **Depth:** the number of versions of each product, such as sizes, colors and formulas.
- **Consistency:** how closely the lines relate in end use, production and distribution.

The video's Apple example, counted on the iPhones Apple sold between September 2024 and February 2025:

```text
Width, the video's lines     iPhone, Mac, iPad, wearables       4
Length of the iPhone line    16 Pro, 16 Pro Max, 16, 16 Plus,
                             15, 15 Plus, 14, 14 Plus, SE       9 models
Depth of the iPhone 16       5 colors × 3 storage sizes        15 versions
```

The video defines length as the items inside one line, which Kotler calls that line's length; the mix's length adds up every line. Keep the two apart when comparing companies. Two long lines and ten short lines can give the same mix length and describe very different strategies. Apple's consistency is high: its lines share software, customers and stores.

## Why lines, not single products

The instructor gives three reasons to build lines:

- **Coverage:** more lines reach more segments.
- **Risk spread:** a bad year in one line is offset by another. His example is Tata Motors, whose commercial vehicles can carry a year in which passenger cars struggle.
- **Brand leverage:** a trusted name lowers the cost of launching the next line.

The four dimensions describe a mix without judging it, and for software even counting items is fuzzy: is Featured a product or a setting? Consistency is the dimension that carries decisions, and it cuts both ways. High consistency lets the brand, the channels and the skills carry over, and that is the case for Marico's mix: hair oil, cooking oil and fabric starch all sell through the same grocery shops, so the expensive part, getting onto shelves across India, is already paid for. But consistent lines also share risks. Two lines that sell to the same people in the same months fail together.

That is Roost's exposure. It runs one line on each side, bookings for students and listings with paid Featured placement for owners, and both earn most of their money from June to August. A second line with a different calendar would spread that risk. One with the same calendar would stack it.

## Predict, then verify

Roost can fund one new line next year:

- **Owner software:** rent reminders and tenant records at ₹299 a month, the new-uses lever from the maturity lesson. The forecast is 300 of 1,200 owners: 300 × ₹299 × 12 = ₹1,076,400 a year, spread evenly across the months.
- **Furniture rental for students:** 2,000 rentals a season at ₹1,000 margin each, ₹2,000,000, nearly all of it from June to August, before paying for a warehouse, vans and repairs.

The founder picks furniture, for nearly twice the money. Which do you pick?

Answer: Owner software. Score each on consistency. Owner software shares Roost's customers (owners its team already visits), its distribution (the app and those visits) and its production (software, which is what Roost's five engineers make). Furniture shares only the customer. Storage, delivery and repair are skills Roost does not have, and every cost the ₹2,000,000 leaves out is a new one. It also lands in the same three months as bookings, so a weak admissions season hits both lines at once, while ₹89,700 a month (300 × ₹299) keeps arriving through the quiet months. The principle: a new line should borrow the company's strengths and spread its risks. A line that needs new capabilities and shares the old risks is a second company.
