In 1999, Nick Swinmurn wanted to know whether people would buy shoes online. He did not build a warehouse. By his own account, he went to Footwear Etc., a shoe store in Sunnyvale, and offered a deal: he would photograph its shoes and put them online, and when someone ordered a pair, he would buy it from the store at full price. The site became Zappos, which Amazon bought in 2009.

Paying full retail meant the test could not show whether selling shoes online would make money, only whether people would buy them, the question that mattered first. Every MVP type proves one thing and is blind to others.

## Five ways to test without code

The video calls Zappos a Wizard of Oz MVP: the customer sees what looks like a working product while people do the work behind it. The others:

- **Explainer video.** Drew Houston's slides record Dropbox's MVP as a three-minute screencast on Hacker News in April 2007, then a beta launch video in March 2008 that took the waiting list from 5,000 to 75,000 in one day. Sync was the expensive part, and the videos measured demand for it first.
- **Landing page with prices.** Joel Gascoigne tested Buffer in late 2010 with a page describing the product, then put a pricing page before the email signup, so a click on a plan measured interest at a price. Buffer had its first paying customer four days after its November 30 launch.
- **Fake door.** A button for a feature that does not exist yet, leading to an honest "not ready yet, want early access?" page. It measures demand from current users, in context.
- **Concierge.** The customer knows a person does the work. In _The Lean Startup_, Ries tells how Manuel Rosso of Food on the Table served his first customer by hand, building her weekly meal plan around local grocery sales, before writing software.

The video adds no-code tools such as Bubble, Airtable and Zapier. They build a working product quickly, but that is still a build.

| Type                     | Can show                             | Cannot show                  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| Explainer video          | Interest from an audience            | Use or payment               |
| Landing page with prices | Clicks at a price                    | Actual payment               |
| Fake door                | Demand in context                    | Payment, or that it works    |
| Concierge                | The outcome is worth paying for      | That software can deliver it |
| Wizard of Oz             | Behavior with a real-looking product | Costs at scale               |

## Climb to the behavior you need

The video's warning is the Mom Test lesson's: people say nice things to be polite, but behavior tells the truth. Behavior comes in grades: a view, a click, a phone number, a payment, a second payment. Pick the type that reaches the grade your assumption is about. If the assumption is that owners will pay every month, no number of clicks can settle it.

Two cautions. A fake door and a Wizard of Oz both mislead people for a while, so tell the truth once someone acts, never charge for what will not be delivered, and deliver what the Wizard promised: Zappos shipped real shoes. And a concierge has a blind spot. The customer is served by a person, and part of what they pay for may be the person.

## Predict, then verify

The previous lesson's Plan B has run: operations sent rent reminders by hand for 100 owners, and 58 paid ₹299 for a second month, far above the 25 needed to pass. But in interviews, 31 of the 58 mention the same thing: an operations associate phoning tenants who were late. The founder wants the engineers to start building now. Do you agree?

Answer: Not yet. The test proved that owners will pay to have rent arrive on time, not that they will pay for software, which will not phone anyone. Run a Wizard of Oz with 50 new owners until their second payment falls due: templated WhatsApp reminders from a Roost number, no calls, still sent by operations behind a simple form. If second-month payment holds above 25%, build. If it falls toward 10%, the value was the calls, and Roost has found a collections service with very different costs, not a software line. The principle: a concierge proves the outcome is worth paying for, and only a test that looks like the real product proves the product is.
