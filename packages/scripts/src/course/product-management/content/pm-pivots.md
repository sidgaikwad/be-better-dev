In 2010, Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger were building Burbn, a mobile web app for checking in at places, making plans, earning points and posting photos. By Systrom's account on Quora in 2011, the iPhone version felt cluttered, so they cut everything except photos, comments and likes. What remained was Instagram, which launched on October 6, 2010 and, he wrote, became the top free photography app within hours. The video's instructor tells it as a data story, users touching only the photos; Systrom's version is about focus. Either way, they did not start over: they kept one part and cut the rest.

## A pivot changes one hypothesis

Eric Ries, in _The Lean Startup_ (2011), defines a pivot as a structured change of course that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, the strategy or the engine of growth. The alternative is to persevere: keep the hypothesis and keep tuning. His catalog names ten types by what changes. Name what stays, too:

| Pivot            | What changes                                    | What stays           |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Zoom-in          | One feature becomes the product                 | That feature's users |
| Zoom-out         | The product becomes one feature of a larger one | The part that worked |
| Customer segment | Who it is for                                   | The product          |
| Customer need    | The problem it solves                           | The customer         |
| Platform         | An app becomes a platform, or the reverse       | The core technology  |

The other five are business architecture (high margin and low volume, or the reverse), value capture (how you charge), engine of growth (viral, sticky or paid), channel, and technology. Burbn to Instagram is a zoom-in.

Slack fits less neatly. Stewart Butterfield's company, Tiny Speck, launched an online game called Glitch in September 2011 and shut it down in December 2012. While building it, the team had made a chat tool for itself on top of IRC. They turned that tool into Slack and launched a preview in August 2013; Butterfield later said 8,000 people requested invitations on the first day. Customer and problem both changed, which by Ries's catalog is closer to a restart. What survived was the team and a tool its builders had used every day, Slack's first retention evidence.

## When to pivot

Ries measures runway not in months of cash but in pivots left, so when you decide matters as much as what. He recommends a regular pivot-or-persevere meeting, so the call is made on evidence rather than in a bad week. This section's tools supply the signals:

- Cohort curves keep decaying toward zero after several rounds of changes.
- No segment you can find approaches 40% on Ellis's question.
- Each round of tuning moves the numbers less than the one before.
- One part of the product, or one group of users, outperforms the whole, as Burbn's photos did.

Keep whatever retained. A pivot that changes segment, need, channel and price at once teaches nothing, because the next result cannot be traced to any one change. Change one hypothesis and carry the rest over.

## Predict, then verify

Suppose Roost launched a rent-collection add-on nine months ago: owners pay ₹500 a month and Roost collects each resident's rent by UPI. After three rounds of changes:

```text
Month-3 retention, cohort after each round   31%, 34%, 35%

Latest cohort, 100 owners
  Large hostels (60+ beds)     14 / 20 = 70%
  Small PGs                    21 / 80 = 26%

Very disappointed, current users
  Large hostels                24 / 45 = 53%
  Small PGs                    10 / 70 = 14%
```

The founder wants to kill it. Kill, persevere with a fourth round for everyone, or pivot?

Answer: pivot to large hostels, a customer segment pivot. The second round bought 3 points and the third only 1: tuning for everyone has stalled. But one group passes both tests: 70% of large hostels still use it at month 3, and 24 of 45 would be very disappointed without it, enough answers to read direction. A small PG with a handful of residents can chase rent itself. Killing it discards the part that retained; a fourth round spends runway on a point. Keep the product, change only who it is for, and set the next test: what large hostels will pay, whether their curve flattens by month 6, and whether the three cities hold enough large hostels to be a market worth having. Pivot when tuning stalls and the evidence already points somewhere specific.
