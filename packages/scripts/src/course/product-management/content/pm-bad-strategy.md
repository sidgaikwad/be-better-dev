Before the kernel in the previous lesson existed, Roost's strategy was a slide from the founder's board deck:

```text
Roost strategy, 2026-27 (draft)
Mission   Be India's most student-centric housing ecosystem
Goal      10 cities and 20,000 bookings a month by 2028
Priorities
  1. Leverage technology to delight students and parents
  2. Grow supply aggressively in every city
  3. Launch roommate matching, a food tie-up and laundry
  4. Be the most affordable option for students
  5. Build a world-class brand
  6. Reach profitability
```

Every line is defensible, and that is the problem. Nothing in it tells the team what to stop doing, and nothing mentions why families do not book.

## Four hallmarks

Rumelt listed four hallmarks of bad strategy in the McKinsey Quarterly (2011), drawn from his book:

- **Fluff**: a restatement of the obvious, dressed in buzzwords that pass for insight. His example is a retail bank whose strategy was "customer-centric intermediation", which, unpacked, says the bank takes deposits and lends them out: it is a bank. "Student-centric housing ecosystem" says Roost houses students.
- **Failure to face the challenge**: no diagnosis, so no way to judge whether an action touches the problem. International Harvester's 1979 plan aimed to lift market share from 16% to 20% and said nothing about its inefficient plants or its notoriously poor labor relations. The slide never mentions trust.
- **Mistaking goals for strategy**: a CEO Rumelt calls Chad Logan wanted a "20/20 plan", 20% annual growth at a 20% margin, and the plan named no point of leverage. "10 cities and 20,000 bookings" has the same hole.
- **Bad strategic objectives**: a long list that nobody chose between, or objectives as hard as the original problem. A Pacific Northwest city's plan, which Rumelt describes, had 47 strategies and 178 action items; item 122 was "create a strategic plan". Priority 3 is three unrelated launches, and priorities 4 and 5 restate the challenge instead of meeting it.

## The rewrite

Line by line, the slide becomes the kernel:

- **Mission line**: delete it. It commits to nothing.
- **Missing diagnosis**: add the trust diagnosis, with its evidence.
- **Goal**: move it below the strategy, as a target the strategy must explain.
- **Priorities**: keep only actions that serve the guiding policy (visits, charges recorded, the parent page, verified reviews, cities paced by verification). The three launches go to a parking list. "Most affordable" is dropped, since it contradicts spending on visits. "World-class brand" becomes a result of keeping promises, not an action.

One question separates the two versions: what does this strategy tell us not to do? The kernel answers: no unverified listings, no cashback war, no city before its visits, no food or laundry this year. The slide says no to nothing.

## Why bad strategy is so common

Rumelt gives two causes. The first is that choosing hurts, because someone's project loses. At Digital Equipment Corporation in 1992, he watched three executives argue for three incompatible directions; leadership wanted consensus, and the compromise everyone could sign was fluff. The second is template planning: fill in vision, mission, values and strategies, and you get statements that sound decisive and decide nothing. The founder's slide is that template, filled in.

## Predict, then verify

The founder accepts the rewrite but wants the headline back for investors: "20,000 bookings a month by 2028" as the strategy, with the kernel in an appendix. Roost books 4,000 a month today, and 2028 is about two years away. Do you agree?

Answer: keep the number, but as a goal under the kernel, and not at that size without a second policy. Going from 4,000 to 20,000 is 5x in two years, about 2.24x a year (2.24 × 2.24 = 5), or 124% annual growth. The kernel's best-evidenced lever, lifting request-to-booking from 50% to 60%, adds 800 bookings a month: 20%. Nearly all of the gap would have to come from new cities, which the guiding policy paces by verification. As written, the number is a blue-sky objective with no named source of leverage. Show investors the number the kernel explains, 4,800 a month in today's cities plus what one verified new city adds, and the evidence that would justify expanding faster. The principle: a goal tests a strategy; it cannot stand in for one.
