Roost's quarterly survey is back: Net Promoter Score +30, down from +35. The founder wants a task force. First, do the arithmetic.

## Net Promoter Score, worked

Fred Reichheld of Bain & Company introduced NPS in "The One Number You Need to Grow" (Harvard Business Review, December 2003). It asks one question: how likely is it that you would recommend the company to a friend or colleague, on a scale from 0 to 10. Scores of 9 or 10 are promoters, 7 or 8 passives, 0 to 6 detractors. NPS is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors, a whole number from -100 to +100. The video describes a 1 to 10 scale; use Reichheld's 0 to 10, which is what published benchmarks use.

Roost's two quarters, 200 responses each:

```text
Last quarter:  110 promoters, 50 passives, 40 detractors
               110 / 200 = 55%   40 / 200 = 20%   NPS = +35
This quarter:  104 promoters, 52 passives, 44 detractors
               104 / 200 = 52%   44 / 200 = 22%   NPS = +30
```

Passives count in the denominator. Leave them out and last quarter becomes 110 / 150 - 40 / 150 = 73.3% - 26.7% = +46.7, a common and wrong figure.

Now the noise. Score each answer as +1, 0 or -1. The variance of one answer is 0.55 + 0.20 - 0.35 x 0.35 = 0.6275, so the standard error over 200 responses is the square root of 0.6275 / 200, or 0.056, and the 95% margin is 1.96 x 5.6 = about 11 points. Last quarter's +35 could be anywhere from +24 to +46.

## What the number cannot say

The course's instructor says NPS never helped him: a score of 30 or 40 tells him people are unhappy, not why. Reichheld's system pairs the question with a follow-up asking the main reason for the score, and the reasons are where the work is. A team that reports the number and skips the reasons keeps the half it cannot act on.

The bigger claim, that this one number predicts growth better than others, has not held up. Timothy Keiningham and colleagues (Journal of Marketing, July 2007) tested it on 21 Norwegian firms and more than 15,500 interviews and could not replicate NPS's advantage over other satisfaction measures. Collapsing 11 points into three buckets also treats a 0 and a 6 alike.

## CSAT and CES answer other questions

- **CSAT**, customer satisfaction, asks how satisfied someone was with a specific moment, usually on a 1 to 5 scale, reported as the share answering 4 or 5. If 320 of 400 move-in surveys score 4 or 5, CSAT is 80%. It judges one touchpoint.
- **CES**, customer effort score, comes from Matthew Dixon, Karen Freeman and Nicholas Toman, "Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers" (HBR, July 2010), a study of more than 75,000 people who had contacted customer service. The question: how much effort did you personally have to put forth to handle your request, from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high). In service interactions, effort predicted repurchase better than satisfaction or NPS: 94% of low-effort customers intended to buy again. The widely quoted claim that 96% of high-effort customers become disloyal is not in the article.

So NPS tracks a relationship, CSAT a touchpoint, CES a task or support flow. For Roost, CES belongs on disputes and CSAT on move-in day. None replaces rebooking and dispute rates, which record what people did, not what they said.

## Predict, then verify

The task force would pause the instalments feature for a quarter to "fix NPS". Read for the 44 detractors, the reason field splits: 19 mention a charge at move-in, 11 slow dispute replies, the rest scattered. Unlisted-charge move-ins have held at 3% for three quarters. Do you form the task force?

Answer: No. Each quarter's score carries a margin of about 11 points, so a 5-point drop does not show that anything changed, and the behavioral measure, unlisted-charge move-ins, has not moved. The reasons still help: 19 of 44 detractors name surprise charges, which confirms the existing charges work rather than calling for a new program. Keep instalments on track and feed the reasons into the charges work. The principle: a score is a symptom measured with error; act on the reasons and on behavior, not on a change smaller than the noise.
