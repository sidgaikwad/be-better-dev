In March 2016 the startup Juicero launched a Wi-Fi connected juice press at $699. It had raised about $120 million, and the machine pressed sealed packs of chopped produce sold on subscription. In January 2017 the price fell to $399. In April 2017 Bloomberg reporters squeezed the packs by hand and got juice nearly indistinguishable, in quantity and quality, from the machine's. Juicero stopped selling on 1 September 2017.

The press worked. What it lacked was a problem: the packs already delivered fresh juice without effort, and the $699 machine added almost nothing that two hands could not.

## The video's definition

The course's instructor defines a product as anything that solves a real problem for someone and gives value in return: time saved, effort reduced, joy, comfort. His test: "If there's no clear problem, you probably don't have a product." He sorts products into four broad types:

1. **Physical**: things you touch, like a laptop or headphones.
2. **Digital**: apps and software, like WhatsApp, Netflix or Spotify.
3. **Service**: work done for you, like consulting, an Uber ride or a Zomato delivery.
4. **Experience**: something you live through, like an Airbnb stay or a guided city tour.

The type decides what one more unit costs. Another copy of an app costs almost nothing; another delivery needs another rider; another laptop needs parts and stock. Most products mix types, and the mix shows where the value sits. Roost is a digital product wrapped around a service: the app lists rooms, but what a student relies on is that someone from the operations team visited the room. That visit is a service, and it scales with people, not servers.

Before any feature list, write the problem down in a form someone else can check:

```text
Product:   Roost
For:       a first-year student moving to a new city, and the
           parent who often pays
Problem:   must commit ₹27,000 (first month plus a two-month
           deposit) for a room they cannot see first
Today:     the college WhatsApp group, a broker, a senior's advice
Value:     the room and rent on the listing are the ones found
           on move-in day
Test:      remove Roost. What gets harder, and for whom?
```

If the last line has no good answer, as it had none for the Juicero press, there is no product yet, only a thing.

## More than the features

Theodore Levitt made the same argument from the marketing side in a Harvard Business Review article in January 1980. Customers, he wrote, buy a total product, which he drew as rings:

- **Generic**: the basic thing. For Roost, a list of rooms near campus.
- **Expected**: what the buyer assumes comes with it. Accurate photos, the real rent, an owner who replies.
- **Augmented**: more than the buyer expected. A verification visit, reviews only from students who booked.
- **Potential**: what could still be added. Instalments, roommate matching.

So UX, support, pricing and trust are part of the product. A student whose support message goes unanswered the night before move-in has used a worse product, whatever the feature list says.

The rings also move. An augmented feature that rivals copy becomes expected: its absence then costs you, and its presence earns nothing. So ask each year which ring every feature now sits in.

One sharpening: what counts is the problem a product solves that the customer's current option does not. Juicero's packs solved a real problem; the press added nothing to them.

## Predict, then verify

The founder wants five new cities before June. The operations team can verify about 400 properties in one new city in that time, so the founder proposes owner self-verification: upload photos and sign a declaration. That would put about 2,000 listings live across five cities. In Pune, the operations team turned away one property in five at the visit. What do you recommend?

Answer: One city, verified. Self-verification would put about 2,000 ÷ 5 = 400 listings live that a visit would have rejected, and students cannot tell which 400. Without the visit, Roost there is a listings app with nicer photos than the WhatsApp group, and the first fake room booked through it teaches students everywhere that the badge means nothing. The visit is not overhead on the product; it is the part that does the solving. The principle: find the part of your offering that solves the problem before cutting it for speed or cost. If visits are the bottleneck, speed them up.
