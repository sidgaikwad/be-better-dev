Roost's finance lead brings a proposal. An insurance partner will sell a ₹299 "move-in protection" plan at checkout, covering a lost deposit if the owner refuses to return it. Roost keeps ₹120 of each sale. The partner's data from other platforms says 12% of buyers add a plan when they tick a box themselves, and 70% keep it when the box arrives already ticked. At 4,000 bookings a month:

```text
Opt-in:      4,000 x 12% = 480 plans   x ₹120 = ₹57,600 a month
Pre-ticked:  4,000 x 70% = 2,800 plans x ₹120 = ₹336,000 a month
```

The same feature, with one default changed, earns almost six times as much. The question is what the extra ₹278,400 is made of.

## Who a feature is for

The course's instructor splits features by whose problem they solve. Customer-focused features address the user's need directly; his example is one-click checkout, which removes friction from buying. Business-focused features serve the company's goals: revenue, retention, differentiation. His example of the second kind is buying motor insurance online and finding an extra personal cover already ticked, placed there in the hope that most buyers assume it is required. He calls this "dark UX".

The instructor's framing makes business features sound suspect. They are not. Every product needs them, because the product has to pay for itself. Roost's own list:

| Feature                                      | Who it serves                         | Verdict          |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| The verification visit                       | Students and parents                  | Customer-focused |
| Featured listings, labelled "Featured"       | Owners, and Roost's revenue           | Business, fair   |
| A renewal reminder to Featured owners        | Roost's revenue                       | Business, fair   |
| Featured listings ranked first with no label | Roost's revenue, at students' expense | Over the line    |
| The pre-ticked protection plan               | Roost's revenue, at students' expense | Over the line    |

The line is not whether the business gains. It is whether the gain depends on the user not understanding what is happening.

## Three questions

A business feature is legitimate when it passes three questions:

1. **Informed:** would the user know it is happening? A labelled Featured slot passes; an unlabelled one fails.
2. **Chosen:** did the user make the choice, or did a default make it for them? A box the student ticks passes; a box they must notice and untick fails.
3. **Endorsed:** if the user later saw exactly what happened, would they be glad or angry? A renewal reminder passes. A ₹299 charge a parent discovers on a bank statement fails.

Features that fail these questions have a name: dark patterns, a term the designer Harry Brignull coined in 2010. Regulators have reached the pre-ticked box already. The EU's Consumer Rights Directive of 2011 requires a customer's express consent to any extra payment, and entitles them to a refund if consent was inferred from a default they had to reject. India's Central Consumer Protection Authority listed "basket sneaking", adding items without the user's consent, among 13 dark patterns in its November 2023 guidelines.

For Roost there is a sharper problem. Its strategy is that the rent you see is the rent you pay, and early research found hidden charges were one of the reasons students distrusted the market. A pre-ticked ₹299 at checkout is a hidden charge, sold by the company whose promise is having none.

## Predict, then verify

Finance models the pre-ticked plan's first season. Of the 2,800 buyers a month, 15% notice and ask for a refund: 420 disputes, each costing about ₹1,000 to handle, plus the refunded ₹120 each. The opt-in version raises about 20 disputes a month. Which version ships?

Answer: Opt-in, and the numbers agree with the principle. Pre-ticked: ₹336,000 minus 420 x ₹120 = ₹50,400 in refunds, minus 420 x ₹1,000 = ₹420,000 in handling, leaves minus ₹134,400 a month. Opt-in: ₹57,600 minus ₹2,400 in refunds and ₹20,000 in handling leaves ₹35,200. Even if disputes came in at half the estimate, pre-ticked would net only about ₹100,800 (₹336,000 minus ₹25,200 minus ₹210,000), and the buyers who never noticed, 2,380 a month in the base case, would still have paid for something they did not choose. For a product whose differentiator is trust, that is the most expensive cost and the one no spreadsheet shows. The principle: a business feature earns its revenue from users who choose it; revenue that depends on users not noticing is borrowed against trust.
