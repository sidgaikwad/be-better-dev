After the pin investigation, three people write the problem into the planning doc:

- The founder: "We need address search in the listing form."
- The designer: "Students don't trust Roost."
- The operations lead: "Owners are careless with pins."

The first is a solution, which leaves the designer nothing to design. The second is true and too big to act on. The third names a culprit. None says who suffers, when, or what it costs, so the team will build whatever the loudest one implies.

## Four parts, no solution

A problem statement names who struggles, with what, in what circumstance, and at what cost, with the evidence. It leaves out the fix:

```text
Who:           First-year students booking a room in a
               city they do not know yet
Struggle:      cannot trust the distance to campus that
               a listing shows
Circumstance:  they book from the listing, often before
               visiting; owners who list from the app get
               the location where they stood, not the
               building's
Cost:          110 cancellations in July cited "farther
               than shown", up from about 40; each loses
               ₹720 of commission and sends a student
               back to search in peak season
Evidence:      44 of 60 listings checked were 1.5 to 8 km
               off; 39 of those were created in the app
```

It is not the artifact of an earlier lesson. A job statement describes the job a student is trying to get done; an opportunity on the opportunity solution tree is a need in the student's own words. A problem statement is the team's agreement on which obstacle to remove, for whom, and what removing it is worth.

Two checks before it goes out. First, can the team name three different fixes that fit? Address search, a map the owner must confirm, and operations correcting the location on each visit all fit this one. If only one fix fits, a solution has leaked in; the usual tell is "lack of", as in "students lack a map view", whose only fix is a map. Second, will you know when it is solved? The cost line sets the measure: distance cancellations back under 40 a month.

## How might we

A statement becomes a brief when it ends in a question. The phrase "how might we" comes from Min Basadur at Procter & Gamble in the early 1970s. P&G was losing to Colgate-Palmolive's Irish Spring, a green-striped soap sold on refreshment, and had tested half a dozen green-stripe copies that all lost. Its team kept asking how to make a better green-stripe bar. Basadur moved it to "How might we create a more refreshing soap of our own?", and that question led to Coast (as Warren Berger tells it). IDEO and others later made the phrase a staple of design thinking.

The soap story shows both ways to miss:

- **Too narrow:** "How might we add address search to the listing form?" The answer is inside the question, as it was in the better green-stripe bar.
- **Too broad:** "How might we make students trust Roost?" Everything fits, so nothing is ruled out.
- **About right:** "How might we make the location on every listing match the building?" Several fixes fit, and success is countable.

## Predict, then verify

The next cycle is four weeks. The designer proposes "How might we help first-year students judge the daily commute before they book?", which covers the 59 commute requests, from the map view to street photos, and the travel time shipped in July. You propose "How might we make the location on every listing match the building?", which covers only the 110 cancellations. She says hers holds the bigger opportunity, and she is right about that. Which goes on the cycle?

Answer: The location question, this cycle. The commute question is real and belongs on the opportunity solution tree, but most fixes under it, the map, distance sorting and the travel time already live, are computed from the listing's location, and the investigation found that location wrong on 44 of 60 cancelled listings. Build on it now and every commute feature inherits the error. The location question also fits the time box: several fixes answer it, it has a baseline of 110 cancellations a month and a target under 40, and four weeks can reach it. The principle: scope a problem statement to the evidence and the time box, broad enough to hold more than one solution, narrow enough that you can tell when it is solved.
