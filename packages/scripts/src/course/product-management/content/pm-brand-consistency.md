The course's instructor asks you to imagine a phone priced at ₹150,000 and sold as premium and innovative. You walk into the store and it is dirty. The box has dirt on it and its edges are tearing. Opening it is a struggle, and the phone has a scratch on one corner. Whatever the advertising said, you now believe the box. The positioning claimed premium, and every physical thing you touched contradicted it.

His summary line is this lesson's idea: positioning informs branding, and branding reinforces positioning. Positioning decides how the product should be perceived. Branding is everything that makes that perception tangible. When the two disagree, customers believe the thing they touched.

## Brand, derived from the position

The video lists four elements of a brand: identity (name, logo, tagline, visuals), voice and personality, values, and emotional appeal. The common mistake is designing these first and finding a position afterwards. Each should follow from the positioning statement. Roost's statement promises that the room and the rent on the listing are the ones you get. So:

- **Identity**: a "Verified by Roost" mark on every listing, with the date of the visit and the name of the person who made it. Photos taken on that visit, not supplied by the owner.
- **Voice**: specific and a little dry. "Rent ₹9,000. Electricity metered, about ₹600 a month in summer. Deposit ₹18,000, returned within 15 days of move-out." Never "Luxurious living awaits."
- **Values**: say the unflattering thing. "Fourth floor, no lift" belongs on the listing, because a brand built on no surprises cannot afford one.
- **Emotional appeal**: relief, for the student and for the parent who is paying.

The video's Apple example works the same way: a premium position carried through the store, the packaging, and a laptop that resumes where you left off when you open the lid. None of those is an advertisement. All of them are branding.

## The touchpoint audit

Consistency means every point where a customer meets the product tells the same story, including the ones marketing does not own. The video's premium luggage brand must show durability and elegance in its packaging and customer service, not only its website, and the improving value delivery lesson already met the video's American Express example: support agents empowered to solve a problem on the first call. For Roost, the audit looks like this:

| Touchpoint   | What the position requires              | What happens now            |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Listing page | Every charge shown, visit dated         | Done                        |
| Move-in day  | Room and charges match the listing      | 3% meet an unlisted charge  |
| Support      | Dispute settled before the student pays | Median reply 3 days in June |

The weakest row defines the brand, and in a marketplace the weakest row often belongs to someone else. Roost does not collect the unlisted charge; the owner does, at the door. So brand consistency for Roost is an operations and policy problem: re-verify owners who draw complaints, delist repeat offenders, and staff support for June rather than for February. A new logo would change nothing a student experiences on move-in day.

## Predict, then verify

Roost has ₹600,000 for the coming season. Option A is an Instagram campaign, "Verified means verified", projected to add 600 bookings. Option B re-verifies the 240 properties that drew any complaint last season (240 full re-inspections, which take longer than a routine ₹600 visit, at ₹1,000 each = ₹240,000) and funds a move-in guarantee: Roost refunds any charge that was not on the listing (₹3,60,000, enough for 360 refunds at an average of ₹1,000). Last season, 3% of about 26,000 June to August bookings, roughly 780 students, met an unlisted charge. The team expects B to cut that to 1%. Which do you fund?

Answer: B. The campaign's 600 bookings would earn 600 × ₹720 = ₹432,000 in commission, but it would broadcast the exact promise that 780 students saw broken, and those students are next year's seniors in the college WhatsApp groups where first-years ask which app to trust. B cuts incidents from 780 to 0.01 × 26,000 = 260, which is 520 fewer broken promises, and the guarantee's 360 refunds cover all 260 that remain, turning each one into a story of Roost keeping its word. Branding can only reinforce a position the experience already delivers. Fix the touchpoint this season, then run the campaign next season with a number that proves the claim.
