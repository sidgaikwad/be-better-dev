In its first season, Featured sold at one price, ₹4,000 a month, to very different owners. A 60-bed hostel in Pune started the season with 20 vacant beds; a 6-bed PG nearby had 2. At the pilot's 12 days sooner and ₹300 a bed per day:

```text
Hostel: 20 beds x 12 days x ₹300 = ₹72,000 gained, ₹4,000 paid (6% of the gain)
PG:      2 beds x 12 days x ₹300 =  ₹7,200 gained, ₹4,000 paid (56% of the gain)
```

One price takes a sliver of the hostel's gain and over half of the PG's: the PG hesitates, and the hostel pays far below what it would. Vacant beds drive willingness to pay here, and tiers let one product sell at several prices without negotiating each sale.

## Good, better, best

The usual template has three tiers: a cheap entry, the one you expect most customers to pick, and a premium one.

| Tier         | Per month | Includes                                     |
| ------------ | --------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Boost        | ₹1,500    | Highlighted card; properties up to 10 beds   |
| Featured     | ₹4,000    | Top-three slot in the locality               |
| Featured Pro | ₹9,000    | Top slot, photo shoot, weekly enquiry report |

What goes where follows _Monetizing Innovation_'s leaders, fillers and killers. Leaders are what people pay for, so every tier needs one: here, placement, at a different strength per tier. Fillers are nice to have and lift the top tier, like the photo shoot. Killers, features few customers value (the book's line is under about 20%), make the rest reject the package and stay out of every tier: here, an auto-reply bot most owners said they distrusted.

## Anchors and decoys

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described anchoring in 1974: people estimate by adjusting from a starting number, and adjust too little. On a pricing page the top tier is that number, and next to ₹9,000, ₹4,000 looks moderate.

Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto showed the decoy effect in 1982: adding an option clearly worse than an existing one raises the share of the option that beats it. Dan Ariely made it famous in _Predictably Irrational_ (2008): offered _The Economist_ at $59 for web, $125 for print, or $125 for print and web, 100 MIT Sloan students split 16, 0 and 84. Another 100, shown no print-only option, split 68 for web and 32 for both:

```text
With the decoy:  16 x $59 + 84 x $125 = $944 + $10,500 = $11,444
Without it:      68 x $59 + 32 x $125 = $4,012 + $4,000 = $8,012
Lift:            $11,444 / $8,012 = 1.43, about 43% more revenue
```

Treat it as an illustration: a classroom choice, no money spent. Shane Frederick, Leonard Lee and Ernest Baskin (2014) found the effect often disappears or reverses when options are described like real products rather than by a few numbers; Huber and his coauthors replied that it holds when the dominance is easy to see. Either way, a tier that exists only as bait is fragile. Make the top tier something some customers want; the anchoring comes free.

## Fences

A fence is a rule that ties a price to a condition, so each customer lands in the tier matching their willingness to pay. Fences limit usage (beds, seats), features, time (peak season) or who can buy (a student ID). Boost's fence is the 10-bed limit, and it holds because Roost's operations team counts beds on every verification visit. A fence works only when it tracks what drives willingness to pay and costs more to dodge than to obey.

## Predict, then verify

Suppose Boost had launched with a top-three slot and no bed limit. Before it, 150 owners bought Featured at ₹4,000. After it, 100 of them move to Boost, 50 stay, and 60 small PGs buy Boost for the first time. What happens to revenue, and what do you change?

Answer: Revenue falls. Before: 150 x ₹4,000 = ₹600,000 a month. After: 50 x ₹4,000 = ₹200,000, plus 160 owners on Boost (100 movers, 60 new) x ₹1,500 = ₹240,000, ₹440,000 in all: ₹160,000 less, though buyers grew from 150 to 210. Fence it twice: 10 beds or fewer, and a highlighted card instead of a top slot. If the 100 larger owners then stay on Featured, revenue is 150 x ₹4,000 + 60 x ₹1,500 = ₹600,000 + ₹90,000 = ₹690,000. A cheaper tier is a price cut for everyone it does not fence out.
