The video's roadmap chapter tells aspiring PMs to learn design thinking and the double diamond, in one sentence, and moves on. Roost's founder has gone further: he has booked two days and a wall of sticky notes for twelve people from operations, marketing and engineering, to "reimagine booking from scratch". Know what the model says before you attend.

## The double diamond

The UK Design Council published the double diamond in 2005, from work its team began in 2003 to describe how designers actually proceed. Four phases sit in two diamonds, and each diamond widens, then narrows: diverge to explore, converge to decide.

1. **Discover.** Understand the issue instead of assuming it: research, observation, talking to the people affected.
2. **Define.** Make sense of what you found and commit to a specific problem to solve.
3. **Develop.** Generate several answers to that problem and try them.
4. **Deliver.** Test at small scale, reject what fails, improve what works, and ship.

The first diamond is about the problem, the second about the solution. The model's most useful claim is that the first exists: teams habitually start at Develop with an answer in hand. In this course, the discovery lessons were the first diamond; this section's sketches and tests are the second.

In 2019 the Design Council placed the diamond inside a wider Framework for Innovation, adding four principles: be people-centered, communicate visually and inclusively, collaborate and co-create, and iterate.

Design thinking is the broader label, popularized by IDEO. Its chief executive, Tim Brown, described it in the Harvard Business Review (June 2008) as meeting people's needs in a way that is technologically feasible and strategically viable: the ground Cagan's four risks cover, with usability folded into desirability.

## The request form, through both diamonds

This section's work, laid out as the model draws it:

1. **Discover.** 4,800 of 12,800 students leave the form each month. Gather 600 exit-poll answers, 20 session recordings, 10 calls with students who left, and a heuristic review. The problem space widens: price shock, parents, a broken upload, silent owners.
2. **Define.** Converge on one statement: "Students who have chosen a room leave the request form because the ₹27,000 due at booking appears only at the end, and they cannot commit before a parent has seen the room." Target: raise the send rate from 62.5%.
3. **Develop.** Sketch three structures, show the total on the listing, add a way to send the room to a parent. Test three rounds of five.
4. **Deliver.** Ship the winner in Pune first, and judge it by sent requests and bookings.

The diagram is a line, but the work loops. Round one of testing showed four of five students believed "Send request" would charge them. That is a new fact about the problem, fear of paying, so it goes back to Define and changes the statement.

## Where it becomes theater

Design thinking goes wrong in recognizable ways. The workshop skips Discover and brainstorms from opinion. It diverges and never converges, ending with a hundred sticky notes and no decision. Nobody critiques ideas against evidence. Natasha Jen, a partner at the design firm Pentagram, made this case in a 2017 talk at the 99U conference: design thinking had shrunk to a ritual of Post-it notes, missing the critique and proof that real design work depends on.

None of that is the model's fault. The PM's job is to insist on its two convergence points: an evidenced problem statement, and a decision about what ships.

## Predict, then verify

The founder's workshop costs 12 people two days each, 24 person-days, and no research has been shared with the attendees. The trio could instead spend that week on the 10 calls with leavers and the first round of five tests, 15 person-days across the three of them. Operations, who know which owners never reply, want to attend. What do you propose?

Answer: cancel the two days, keep the people. Run the calls and the first test round first, then hold a half-day session where the twelve see the exit poll, the recordings and the test findings, agree on the problem statement, and sketch solutions to it. That is 6 person-days of workshop on top of the research instead of 24 without it, and operations' knowledge about owners lands in Define, where it changes the problem, rather than in a brainstorm. The principle: diverge only after you have evidence, and never leave a session without converging.
