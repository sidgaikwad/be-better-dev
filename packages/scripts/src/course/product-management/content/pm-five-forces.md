From 1992 to 2006, the average US industry earned a 14.9% return on invested capital. Soft drinks and packaged software each earned 37.6%. Airlines, which millions rely on, earned 5.9%. Michael Porter's explanation: profits depend less on how useful a product is than on who else can claim a share of the value.

## Five forces, not one

Porter set this out in "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" (HBR, 1979), built it into _Competitive Strategy_ (1980), and restated it in HBR in 2008, the source of those numbers. The five:

- **New entrants**: how easily a newcomer can start competing. Easy entry caps prices.
- **Supplier power**: how much those who provide your inputs can charge or withhold.
- **Buyer power**: how hard customers can push prices down. Buyers are strong when switching costs nothing and offers look alike.
- **Substitutes**: something that does the same job by a different means. Substitutes put a ceiling on price.
- **Rivalry**: how hard existing players compete, and above all whether on price.

Airlines lose on all five: price wars, fickle travelers, powerful plane makers and unions, a stream of new carriers, and trains and cars as substitutes. In Porter's words, "the strongest competitive force or forces determine the profitability of an industry."

## Student housing, run

The industry: apps that match students with PGs and hostels. Roost is the subject.

| Force                    | What drives it                                            | Strength  |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| New entrants             | An app takes months; visited supply takes a season        | Medium    |
| Owners (suppliers)       | Many small owners; the best PGs fill themselves in June   | Medium    |
| Ad platforms (suppliers) | ₹900 of Instagram ads per ₹720 booking                    | High      |
| Students and parents     | Pay nothing, switch freely, search every channel          | High      |
| Substitutes              | Brokers, WhatsApp groups, relatives, managed residences   | Very high |
| Rivalry                  | Fixed costs all year; 54% of commission lands in 13 weeks | Medium    |

The substitutes row decides the verdict. In the real competition lesson, 96% of Pune first-years who searched Roost but did not book went to a broker, a WhatsApp group, relatives or similar, and 4% to another app. Managed residence operators such as Stanza Living, founded in 2017, do the same job by a different means: they run their own branded buildings. Substitutes and free-switching buyers cap Roost's prices; rival apps, the investor deck's worry, are among the weakest forces.

## Where it breaks

A marketplace does not fit Porter's chain of supplier, firm and buyer. Owners supply the rooms and also pay the fees, while students are buyers who pay nothing. Run each side separately, as the table does, or the analysis blurs.

In 2008 Porter also warned against mistaking visible features for structure: growth, technology, government and complements act through the five forces and are not forces themselves. A growing market with easy entry mostly attracts entrants.

The PM's question: which force caps our profit, and what would weaken it? Porter's advice: take the position where the forces are weakest. For Roost that is first-years moving to a new city, who have no seniors to ask and no broker they trust. Substitutes are weakest for them, and it shows: they are 30% of monthly active students but 58% of bookings.

## Predict, then verify

A new app enters Pune offering owners 0% commission for two seasons. Roost earns about 15,000 × ₹720 = ₹10,800,000 a year from Pune. Listing is free on both apps, and each is paid only for bookings it delivers. The sales lead wants to match 0% before owners leave. What do you do?

Answer: Do not match. Owners need not leave Roost to use the newcomer; a sensible owner lists on both. The offer changes where rooms appear, not where students book, and each booking pays the app the student used. Matching hands owners 2 × ₹10,800,000 = ₹21,600,000 over the two seasons, the transfer from industry to customers that Porter says price rivalry causes, and wins nothing on the side that decides. The entrant's real fight is for students, against brokers and WhatsApp groups, where verification is Roost's edge. Watch Pune's share of bookings, not owner counts. The answer changes if the entrant demands exclusivity: then the best-located PGs, which fill without help, hold the power, and only they are worth a special deal. The principle: find the side whose choice decides the outcome, and compete there.
