Roost's weekly dashboard has grown to 41 numbers, and in Monday's meeting nobody can say which of them to worry about. Dave McClure, the investor who later founded 500 Startups, offered a way out in a 2007 talk called "Startup Metrics for Pirates": group the numbers by where the customer is in their life with the product, and watch few of them. Five stages, whose initials read like a pirate's growl: AARRR.

## The five stages

McClure's slides give each stage a one-line definition, paraphrased here:

1. **Acquisition**: people arrive from some channel.
2. **Activation**: their first visit is a happy one.
3. **Retention**: they come back, more than once.
4. **Referral**: they like it enough to bring others.
5. **Revenue**: they do something that makes money.

Translating these generic lines is the real work. A student books a room about once a year, so "coming back" happens within one search season, not over months. Revenue arrives at booking, when Roost earns 8% of the first month's rent; referral usually comes later, after a good stay. So Roost's order is acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, referral. McClure's order is a convention, not a law.

Here are May's new students followed through the season, with the team's definition for each stage:

| Stage       | Roost's definition                              | Students | Step rate |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------- | --------- |
| Acquisition | First visit this month                          | 30,000   |           |
| Activation  | Shortlists 2 or more verified rooms in week one | 9,000    | 30%       |
| Retention   | Returns in at least two separate weeks          | 5,400    | 60%       |
| Revenue     | Books and pays (₹720 commission each)           | 2,160    | 40%       |
| Referral    | Invites a friend who signs up                   | 324      | 15%       |

The activation line is a working definition: whether shortlisting two verified rooms predicts booking is a hypothesis to test against who actually books.

## The biggest drop is not the target

Read as percentages lost, the stages rank: referral loses 85%, activation 70%, revenue 60%, retention 40%. A team that attacks the biggest drop spends its quarter on referral, which is backwards. Students book once a year, so most have no one to refer in May, and a low referral rate is the product's natural shape rather than a leak.

A drop measures a stage, not an opportunity. Three questions decide where to work. Is the loss real, or were those people never going to buy? Many first visitors are parents browsing or students already housed, so part of the activation drop is who marketing brought, not what the product did. Is the cause known? And what does it cost to move? Because the stages multiply, the same relative gain is worth the same at the end: activation from 30% to 33% gives 30,000 x 0.33 x 0.60 x 0.40 = 2,376 bookings, and revenue conversion from 40% to 44% gives 30,000 x 0.30 x 0.60 x 0.44 = 2,376. Equal prizes, so the cheaper and surer fix wins.

AARRR has limits. It is drawn as a straight line, so it hides that referral feeds acquisition. It describes one side of a market, and Roost needs a second set of five for owners. And it says nothing about which number matters most: it organizes the dashboard, while the north star ranks it.

## Predict, then verify

Using May's numbers, three leads bid for next quarter. Growth wants acquisition, because more at the top lifts everything. Design wants activation, the biggest drop that is not referral. Operations brings evidence: exit interviews show that of the 3,240 retained students who did not book, about a third gave up because the owner never answered their request. Where does the quarter go?

Answer: to the revenue stage, owner response. Operations has the only diagnosed cause, and it is a loss among students who had already chosen a room, the most expensive students to lose. If faster owner replies recover half of the third who gave up, that is 3,240 / 3 = 1,080 students, and half of that is 540 more bookings, worth 540 x ₹720 = about ₹390,000, from reminders and calls operations already knows how to make. The activation drop may be larger, but its cause is unknown and partly a marketing mix problem. The principle: work where the loss is understood and cheap to fix per student, not where the percentage is biggest.
