In June 2023 the US Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon over Prime. Joining took one or two clicks. Leaving, the complaint said, meant a flow Amazon had internally code-named "Iliad", after Homer's poem about a ten-year war: four pages, six clicks and fifteen options, with offers along the way to stay at a discount, to switch off auto-renewal instead, or to keep Prime after all. By clicks alone, six against two or one, leaving was three to six times harder than joining. The flow's purpose, the FTC charged, was to stop subscribers cancelling.

On 25 September 2025 Amazon settled for $2.5 billion: a $1 billion civil penalty, a record for an FTC rule violation, and $1.5 billion in refunds to about 35 million customers.

## Four patterns with names

The business features lesson drew the line with Roost's pre-ticked ₹299 protection plan: a business feature is fair when the user is informed, made the choice, and would endorse it afterwards. The designer Harry Brignull named the features that fail it in 2010, dark patterns, and keeps a public catalogue, now at deceptive.design, where some patterns have new names. Four a PM meets constantly:

| Brignull's name                   | What it does                       | A Roost version                            |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Sneak into basket                 | Adds an item the user did not pick | The ₹299 plan added at checkout            |
| Preselection                      | Lets a default do the choosing     | "Send me partner offers" ticked at signup  |
| Confirmshaming                    | Puts guilt on the decline button   | "No thanks, I don't care about my deposit" |
| Roach motel, now "hard to cancel" | Easy in, hard out                  | Featured can be cancelled only by phone    |

India's Central Consumer Protection Authority listed 13 dark patterns in its November 2023 guidelines, and basket sneaking, confirm shaming and subscription trap are three of them. They apply to Roost.

One test catches all four. Imagine every user understood the screen completely. If the design's extra revenue would fall to zero, it earns from a mistake, and it is deceptive.

## What they inflate, and what they burn

Every dark pattern is aimed at a metric, and usually moves it, which is why it ships: a harder cancellation flow wins almost any A/B test judged on retention.

The damage is to what the metric means. An owner who tried to cancel and gave up counts as retained, so retention now mixes customers who value the product with customers who could not find the exit. A price rise justified by "strong retention" rests on a number that is partly false.

The cost lands in the guardrails the metric trees lesson asked for: refunds, chargebacks, complaints, and in a marketplace, supply that quietly leaves. For Roost it also lands on trust, which is the product.

Persuasion is not deception. A retention offer shown once, with a one-tap decline, is fair. Three checks: leaving takes no more effort than joining, every label says what the button does, and defaults match what most users would pick if asked.

US law is unsettled, not absent. The Eighth Circuit vacated the FTC's 2024 click-to-cancel rule on procedural grounds on 8 July 2025; the Amazon settlement came eleven weeks later, under existing law.

## Predict, then verify

Featured has 300 owners paying ₹840,000 a month, an average of ₹2,800 each. Each September, as the season ends, owners cancel; last September 45 did. The growth lead proposes cancellation by phone call to an account manager, weekdays only, and estimates 40% of cancelling owners give up: 18 owners x ₹2,800 = ₹50,400 a month kept, and September churn reported at 9% instead of 15%. The designer proposes one-tap cancellation with a single offer: pause Featured free until May. Which ships?

Answer: One tap with the pause. The ₹50,400 is real for a month or two, but it is made of 18 owners paying for a slot they tried to stop. They will find it on a statement, dispute it, and some will delist, and owners are half the marketplace. The 9% would be false, and pricing would read it as loyalty. Subscription trap is also a named pattern in India. The pause is honest retention: it answers the real reason owners leave, the season, and every owner who takes it chose to. The principle: a retention number is worth only what it measures, so count the customers who chose to stay, not the ones who could not leave.
