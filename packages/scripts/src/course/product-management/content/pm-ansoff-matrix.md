Roost's founder brings four growth ideas to the annual plan, one slide each: win more of Pune, launch in Chennai, let families pay the ₹27,000 upfront cost in instalments, and list flats for young professionals. Each promises a few thousand more bookings, and none says how sure that is.

## The grid

Igor Ansoff, then a planner at Lockheed, published the idea in "Strategies for Diversification" (Harvard Business Review, 1957). The version taught today crosses products, existing or new, with markets, existing or new. The course's instructor illustrates each:

1. **Market penetration:** the existing product in the existing market. Win rivals' customers or raise use, as Coca-Cola does with promotions where it already dominates.
2. **Product development:** a new product for existing customers, like each year's iPhone.
3. **Market development:** the existing product in a new market: a country, a demographic, an industry. Netflix switched on in India on January 6, 2016, one of 130 countries added that day.
4. **Diversification:** a new product in a new market. Related diversification reuses something you know, as Tesla's Powerwall home battery (2015) reused its car-battery work. Unrelated reuses nothing, as Roost renting office space to startups would.

The instructor's unrelated example is Amazon buying Whole Foods (June 2017, about $13.7 billion). That label is disputable: Amazon had delivered groceries through AmazonFresh since 2007, so it bought stores for a product it already sold.

Most versions, the video's included, rank risk from penetration, lowest, through the two developments to diversification. Roost's slides, placed on the grid:

| Option                           | Cell                                 | What is new                     |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Pune share from 30% to 35%       | Penetration                          | Nothing                         |
| Instalments for the upfront cost | Product development                  | A lending partner, repayments   |
| Chennai                          | Market development                   | Owners, verifiers, channels     |
| Flats for young professionals    | Market development, says the founder | Customer, product, lease checks |

The last row is where labels slip. Roost's Bengaluru pilot listed 60 flats: 2,000 professionals used it and 30 booked, at ₹22,000 average rent. A leased flat is not a PG room, so the product changed along with the customer: by the grid's own rules, related diversification.

## What Ansoff actually crossed

Ansoff did not cross products with markets. He paired a product line with its missions: the job the product is meant to perform, such as Lockheed's passenger transport or airborne early warning. Market development meant adapting the product to a new mission, such as a passenger plane refitted for cargo. Penetration meant more sales within the present product and mission, including to new customers. And he drew no risk ladder. He wrote that a well-run business pursues the first three together, and that diversification stands apart because it needs new skills and facilities. The ladder is a later teaching gloss.

The mission view re-sorts Roost's slides. Chennai serves the same job, a first-year from another city needing a verified room before term, with the same checks and worried parents. In Ansoff's terms that is new customers for the present product: close to penetration. Young professionals bring a new job, a flat near an office, rented all year and paid for by the renter, on top of a new product.

The rule: before trusting a cell's risk, count what is actually new: the job, the customer, the product, the capability you must build.

## Predict, then verify

Next season's growth money funds one of two plans. Pune: five more points of share, 2,500 bookings. Coaching partners and ambassadors are at capacity, so the extra bookings come from Instagram ads at ₹900 each. Chennai: 1,500 bookings at ₹8,500 average rent, with ₹805,000 of verifiers, travel, support and channels. The founder says penetration is the lowest-risk cell, so Pune goes first. Which do you fund?

Answer: Chennai. Pune's extra share costs 2,500 × ₹900 = ₹2,250,000 and earns 2,500 × ₹720 = ₹1,800,000: a loss of ₹450,000, or ₹180 a booking. Chennai earns 1,500 × ₹680 (8% of ₹8,500) = ₹1,020,000 against ₹805,000, a contribution of ₹215,000. Nor does risk rescue Pune. By Ansoff's mission test, Chennai is the present product doing its present job for new customers, and its new parts, local owners and verifiers, are work Roost already did in Bengaluru and Hyderabad. At 30% share, the students Pune has left mostly chose brokers on purpose. The principle: rank growth options by what is actually new and by what the next booking costs, not by the name of the cell.
