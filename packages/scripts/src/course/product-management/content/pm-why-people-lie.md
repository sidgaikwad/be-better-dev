In the story that opens Intellipaat's course, Priya validates the hostel app by speaking to over 100 students and asking: would you trust this listing, would you feel safe booking here, and is EMI payment something you would actually use? Suppose 80 of them say yes to all three. What has she learned?

Almost nothing. Each question asks a stranger to predict their own behavior, about an idea the interviewer plainly loves, and saying yes costs nothing. The same video later warns that "would you use this?" invites polite lies, and names the book that fixes it.

## The Mom Test

Rob Fitzpatrick's _The Mom Test_ (2013) starts from a plain observation: your mother will lie to you about your business idea, kindly, because she loves you, and so will most polite strangers. The fix is not to find more honest people. It is to ask questions even your mother could not lie in answering, because they are about her life, not your idea.

His three rules:

1. Talk about their life, not your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the past, not generalities or opinions about the future.
3. Talk less and listen more.

Some answers feel like evidence and are not. Fitzpatrick names three kinds.

- **Compliments.** "That sounds really useful." Deflect it and go back to their life.
- **Fluff.** Generic claims ("I usually check reviews"), future promises ("I would definitely pay") and hypotheticals ("I might use that"). Anchor each one to a real event: "When did you last check reviews?"
- **Ideas and feature requests.** "You should add a map view." Write it down, then dig for the motive: "What would a map let you do?"

## One Roost interview, twice

Roost's founder wants to sell live video tours of properties, run by the operations team. The first interview, with a first-year who moved from Nagpur to Pune:

```text
PM:       We're building live video tours of hostels. Would you use that?
Student:  Oh, definitely. That's a great idea.
PM:       Would you pay ₹199 for one?
Student:  Yeah, for sure. Safety is really important to me.
```

Two compliments and a future promise. Now the same student, with the idea never mentioned:

```text
PM:       How did you find the room you have now?
Student:  My cousin in Pune visited three PGs and sent photos.
          We picked one. On day one the owner asked for ₹4,000
          "maintenance" that wasn't in the listing.
PM:       What did you do?
Student:  Paid it. My father was angry for a week.
```

Her family already checks whether a room is real and safe, for free, by sending a cousin. What cost real money was the price, which no tour would have shown.

## What counts instead

Nobody in the first transcript is malicious. People forecast their own behavior badly, and they want to be kind to someone visibly invested. The lie lives in the question, not the person.

Fitzpatrick's substitute for opinions is commitment. Once you do make an offer, the other person should give something up: time (a scheduled next step), reputation (an introduction), or money (a deposit or pre-order). Saying a tour is worth ₹199 tells you nothing. Paying ₹199 to book one for Saturday tells you a lot.

The method has a limit: it teaches you about problems people already have. A behavior that does not exist yet has no past to ask about, so it needs a test of what people do, which comes later in this part. And where the video adds asking users what they "wish existed", Fitzpatrick would file the answer as a feature request, useful only for its motive.

## Predict, then verify

You run 10 interviews about the video tour. 8 students say they would pay ₹199. 3 describe a relative visiting properties for them last season. None has ever paid anyone to see a room. The founder says 80% want it and asks for a three-week build. What do you tell him?

Answer: The 8 yeses are compliments and future promises, so they count for nothing. The evidence is behavior: 3 families solved the problem by sending a relative, and nobody has paid for it, which points against a paid tour. Instead of building, offer the tour for real on 20 listings for a week at ₹199. If almost nobody pays, you saved three weeks. The principle: count what people have done and what they commit to now, not what they predict.
