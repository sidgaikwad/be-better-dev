Every month about 12,800 students open Roost's request form, the four steps that ask an owner to hold a room, and 8,000 send it. The other 4,800, 37.5% of those who started, leave. The founder has seen a rival's app and thinks he knows why: "Our form looks dated. Give the designer three weeks to make it look modern."

He may be right about the look and wrong about the problem. Telling the two apart takes a distinction the video draws near the end of its MVP chapter.

## Two words for two scopes

The user interface is what is on the screen: layout, type, color, icons, buttons, the controls a person touches. User experience is broader. Don Norman coined the term in 1993, when he joined Apple with the title User Experience Architect, because "human interface" and "usability" felt too narrow to him. Nielsen Norman Group, the firm he later co-founded with Jakob Nielsen, defines UX as every aspect of a person's interaction with a company, its services and its products.

The video's instructor puts it with a car. UI is the paint job and the dashboard design. UX is the whole drive: how smooth it is, how comfortable, whether you arrive. The analogy goes further than the video takes it: the drive also includes the fuel station, the service center and the road. For Roost, the experience of booking includes the owner who takes three days to reply, the verifier's visit and the parent who has to approve the room. None of it is on a screen; all of it is UX.

## Sorting the 4,800

Before the designer touches a pixel, ask the people who left. A one-question exit poll on the form drew 600 answers:

| Why they left                               | Answers | Share |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------- | ----- |
| Did not expect ₹27,000 due at booking       | 216     | 36%   |
| Need to check with parents first            | 150     | 25%   |
| College ID upload failed, or too many steps | 108     | 18%   |
| Not sure the owner will reply               | 78      | 13%   |
| Other                                       | 48      | 8%    |

Only the third row is mostly a UI problem, and even that is partly a bug. The first is about when Roost tells the truth: the first month's rent plus a two-month deposit appears only on the form's last step. The second is about a person not holding the phone. The fourth is about owner behavior, which operations can change and a color palette cannot. A visual refresh aims at 18% of the reasons.

Treat these as leads, not a ranking: what people say is not what they did, as the lesson on where feedback lives warned. They still show the problem is bigger than the paint.

## What the PM owns in design

The four risks lesson gave the split: the designer owns usability, the PM value and viability. The PM brings four things to design work:

1. **The problem and the evidence**: the 4,800, the exit poll, the recordings.
2. **The outcome**: more sent requests that become bookings, not "a modern form".
3. **The facts and constraints**: what the ₹27,000 is made of, which charges owners may add, what the lending partner requires.
4. **The parts off the screen**: owner reply times, the parent's approval, the verification promise.

The designer owns the flow, the interaction and the visuals. In a review, name the problem ("three of five students missed the total"), not your fix ("make it bold and red"). A PM who dictates pixels hands over a solution in disguise, and the designer stops thinking.

## Predict, then verify

Instead of the refresh, the team moves the ₹27,000 total onto the listing page, above the "Request this room" button. A month later, 10,900 students open the form instead of 12,800, and 8,200 send it instead of 8,000. The founder sees a 15% fall in form opens and wants the change reverted. Was the change a win?

Answer: yes. The form now converts 8,200 of 10,900, 75.2%, up from 62.5%, and it produces 200 more requests a month. The 1,900 who no longer open the form saw the price on the listing and left before typing anything, which costs them less than leaving at step four. The founder is judging one screen's traffic; an experience is judged by the outcome of the whole journey. The principle: UX is end to end, so measure it at the end.
