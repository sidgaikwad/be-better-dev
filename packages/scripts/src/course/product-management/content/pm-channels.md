The video's hostel app launched through four channels, and none was chosen for being cheap or fashionable. Its PM, Priya, ran campaigns in college student communities, tie-ups with student YouTubers and Instagram creators, SEO articles (hostel-hunting tips, safety checklists, city guides), and partnerships with coaching centers that already had reach. A student is already inside each of the four before hearing of the app. She did not build an audience; she went to four that existed.

## Start from the customer's week, not the channel list

A channel is the path along which a customer first meets you and comes to buy. Teams tend to pick the channels they know how to run. Pick them from the ideal customer profile instead, walking through what the customer does before buying:

1. The admission is confirmed, and the student asks seniors in the college's WhatsApp and Telegram groups where to live. Channel: student ambassadors inside those groups.
2. They watch videos about the college and the city. Channel: student creators.
3. A parent searches "PG near" plus the college's name. Channel: search pages, one per college and locality.
4. They still follow the coaching center where they prepared for entrance exams. Channel: coaching partnerships.

Each step names a place, a moment and a person. A billboard matches none of them; it might still work, but you have no reason to expect it to.

## Channel-product fit

Brian Balfour's 2017 essay on product-channel fit puts it plainly: products are built to fit channels, and channels do not bend to products. For Roost this turns channels into product work. Search only works if the product generates an indexable page for each college and locality listing its verified rooms, which is an engineering ticket, not a marketing budget. Creators only work if there is something worth filming, like a verified-room walkthrough. Balfour's companion idea, channel-model fit, is the budget rule: at ₹720 of revenue per booking, a channel where each booking costs ₹1,000 loses money however much it grows.

## Test cheaply, then pick one

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares's _Traction_ (2014) gives the procedure, which they call the Bullseye: brainstorm a way to use each of nineteen channels, rank them, run small tests on the most promising few at once, then focus on the one that works. A Roost test sets its pass line before spending anything:

```text
Channel tests, Pune, four weeks in May
Pass line: cost per booking under ₹400, leaving ₹320 of the ₹720
  Ambassadors, 20 colleges   ₹40,000    110 bookings   ₹364 each    pass
  Creator reels, 2 videos    ₹60,000     25 bookings   ₹2,400 each  fail
  Coaching partners          ₹300 fee    70 bookings   ₹300 each    pass
```

Two cautions. Test in April and May, not October: Roost's demand lands between June and August, so an off-season test measures an empty market. And do not judge search pages in four weeks; they take months to rank, so judge them on search position first and bookings over a season.

Why narrow to one? Peter Thiel argued in _Zero to One_ (2014) that one working distribution channel is enough for a great business, and Balfour observes that a company with product-channel fit gets 70% or more of its growth from one channel at a time. Channels reward practice: the one you work on gets cheaper, while a budget spread across six keeps all six expensive.

## Predict, then verify

Pune's paid channels must deliver 2,000 bookings this season on ₹800,000. Roost has 35 coaching partners, each good for about 20 bookings, a ceiling of 700. Ambassadors cost ₹364 a booking for the first 800, then about ₹500 as they reach less-connected colleges. The marketing lead wants ₹400,000 for creators, because their reels reached 9,000 students against the partners' 900. How do you allocate?

Answer: coaching partners to their ceiling, ambassadors for the rest, and creators only as a small retest. Partners cost 700 x ₹300 = ₹210,000. Ambassadors cost 800 x ₹364 = ₹291,000, then 500 x ₹500 = ₹250,000. That is 2,000 bookings for ₹751,000, leaving ₹49,000 to retest creators in a new format, such as verified-room walkthroughs. ₹400,000 in creators at ₹2,400 a booking would buy about 167 bookings and lose ₹1,680 on each. Reach does not pay; bookings do. The principle is channel-model fit: fund channels in order of cost per booking, each up to its ceiling, and make a channel that loses money on every booking earn a bigger budget in a test first.
