In December 1975 Steve Sasson, a young Kodak engineer, got a self-contained digital camera working. It weighed 8 pounds, recorded a 100 by 100 pixel black-and-white image, and took 23 seconds to write it to a cassette tape. Sasson told The New York Times in 2008 that management's reaction was "that's cute, but don't tell anyone about it." Kodak filed for bankruptcy in January 2012.

The usual moral is that Kodak buried the future. The record says otherwise. In 2005, by IDC's figures in Kodak's annual report, Kodak kept its number one US position in digital still cameras, and digital revenue passed traditional revenue for the first time. In that year's third quarter, worldwide film capture sales fell 30%. Kodak did not miss digital. It could not make digital earn what film had: film paid on every roll, and a digital camera sold once. That is the innovator's dilemma.

## How good allocation starves the new business

Christensen's claim: the process that makes a company good, funding whatever earns most from customers it knows, starves a disruptive business. Run it on Roost. Suppose Roost answers the disruption theory lesson's rival B with its own cheap-room product, using ₹25 automated checks instead of ₹600 visits, and it meets a rebuilt search in the planning meeting:

```text
Yearly commission          48,000 × ₹720 = ₹34,560,000
Growth target, 20%         ₹6,912,000 of new revenue
Search rebuild, +8%        3,840 × ₹720  = ₹2,764,800  (40% of target)
Cheap rooms, year one      1,500 × ₹320  = ₹480,000    (7% of target)
```

A rational team funds the search rebuild, and it should. But the same arithmetic wins every later meeting too, while the rival improves. The video lists five barriers: resistance to change, resource allocation, risk aversion, culture and structure, and customers' expectations (the instructor: Swiggy users want better delivery, not a new business). They are one force seen from five sides.

## Separate, then connect

Christensen's answer, restated in the 2015 HBR article: keep improving the core, and put the disruptive business in a separate division protected by senior leadership. Charles O'Reilly and Michael Tushman called this the ambidextrous organization (HBR, 2004): exploratory units with their own processes and culture, tied to the core at the top. Of 35 breakthrough attempts they studied, more than 90% of those run this way met their goals, though the sample was theirs to choose.

The video's success story, Microsoft's Azure, fits the pattern. It began as Red Dog, the first project out of Ray Ozzie's advanced development labs, led by Dave Cutler and Amitabh Srivastava. It was announced in October 2008 and sold from February 2010. By February 2011 it sat in the Server and Tools business, which Satya Nadella was promoted to run; he became CEO in 2014. Separate while small, joined to the core once it had paying customers.

The video also cites Google's 20% time, which Google's 2004 IPO letter credited with AdSense for content and Google News. That produces ideas. Kodak was never short of ideas, only of a budget that did not have to beat film. Even Christensen's team concedes the separate unit sometimes works and sometimes does not.

The instructor closes with six dimensions for judging a disruption's impact: market, competitive, economic, technology, organizational, and societal and regulatory. He admits they fit any idea, which is their weakness: a list that fits everything ranks nothing. For a disruption the economic row decides.

## Predict, then verify

The founder wants the cheap-room product ranked by RICE against the search rebuild. The alternative is a separate unit: one of the five engineers and two operations staff, under a different name, with its own target (bookings of rooms under ₹5,000) and a two-season budget. Which do you choose?

Answer: The separate unit. Inside the roadmap the cheap-room product loses every time, 7% of the growth target against 40%. Nor can it carry Roost's name: Roost promises a visit to every room, and this product's cost advantage is not visiting. Its economics are different, not bad: 5 bookings × ₹320 = ₹1,600 per property, less a ₹25 check, leaves ₹1,575, about half the core's ₹3,000 and more than the nothing Roost earns from cheap rooms today. Give it a kill rule (fewer than 1,000 bookings by the end of the second season) and a line to the founder instead of a slot in the core's planning. The principle: a business that earns less per customer than the core needs its own budget and measures, or the core's rational process will starve it.
