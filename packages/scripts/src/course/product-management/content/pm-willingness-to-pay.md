In 2016 Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke of the pricing consultancy Simon-Kucher published _Monetizing Innovation_. Their firm's survey found 72% of new products launched in the previous five years had failed. Many, they argue, were doomed before launch because the price came after the build. They name four failure types:

- **Feature shock:** too many features, a price to match, and a product nobody can explain.
- **Minivation:** the right product priced too low, like Featured at the last lesson's cost-plus ₹900.
- **Hidden gem:** a good idea never brought to market, often because it sits outside the core business.
- **Undead:** a product that launches although nobody wants it.

The remedy is the book's subtitle: design the product around the price, asking about willingness to pay before you build.

## The conversation, before the build

The book's questions, each followed by "why?", adapted for Roost owners in the season before Featured launched:

```text
A paid option would put your property at the top of search results
for your area from June to August.
1. What would be an acceptable monthly price for that?   Why?
2. What would be an expensive price?                      Why?
3. What price would be too expensive to consider?        Why?
4. Would you buy it at ₹4,000 a month?                    Why, or why not?
```

The numbers give a range; the reasons show what drives it. An owner who says "only if I am the first result" has told you placement is what sells. Segment owners by these answers, not by who they are.

The Mom Test lesson warned that questions about the future collect compliments. Price questions are about the future too, which is why each needs its "why" and the last names a number. Treat the answers as a range to test, not a forecast.

## The price sensitivity meter

At survey scale, the standard tool is Peter van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter, presented at the ESOMAR congress in 1976. It asks for four prices: so cheap you would doubt the quality, a bargain, getting expensive but still worth considering, and too expensive to consider. Each cell is the share of 200 Roost owners who would describe that price that way:

| Price  | Too cheap | Cheap | Expensive | Too expensive |
| ------ | --------- | ----- | --------- | ------------- |
| ₹2,000 | 35%       | 75%   | 15%       | 5%            |
| ₹3,000 | 20%       | 55%   | 30%       | 10%           |
| ₹4,000 | 10%       | 35%   | 50%       | 20%           |
| ₹5,000 | 5%        | 20%   | 70%       | 40%           |

Find each crossing on a straight line between neighboring rows:

```text
Optimal price: "too cheap" meets "too expensive"
  ₹3,000: 20 - 10 = +10     ₹4,000: 10 - 20 = -10
  ₹3,000 + ₹1,000 x (10 / 20) = ₹3,500
Lower bound: "too cheap" meets "expensive"
  ₹2,000: 35 - 15 = +20     ₹3,000: 20 - 30 = -10
  ₹2,000 + ₹1,000 x (20 / 30) = ₹2,667
Upper bound: "too expensive" meets "cheap"
  ₹4,000: 20 - 35 = -15     ₹5,000: 40 - 20 = +20
  ₹4,000 + ₹1,000 x (15 / 35) = ₹4,429
```

Owners accept roughly ₹2,700 to ₹4,400, centered near ₹3,500. Tools differ on which curves mark the bounds, so expect small shifts.

## Where it breaks

The meter measures stated perception, not purchases. Its questions name no alternative, so the rival's ₹1,500 slot is absent unless you add it, and it cannot say how many owners buy at each price. Use it for the range; test real prices with real payments.

## Predict, then verify

The survey tops out near ₹4,400; the pilot says a typical owner gains ₹18,000. The founder wants ₹8,000, citing the value. Finance wants ₹3,500, the survey's optimum. What do you launch at?

Answer: ₹4,000, inside the range, and not ₹8,000 yet. The ₹18,000 to ₹4,400 gap is one of perception: owners answered before seeing a single faster booking, and ₹8,000 asks them to pay for value they cannot see. The optimum is where two complaints balance, not a revenue figure. At ₹4,000, 20% of owners call the price too expensive; at ₹3,500, halfway, about 15%. Even if everyone else bought, ₹4,000 x 80 = ₹320,000 per 100 owners beats ₹3,500 x 85 = ₹297,500. Then close the gap with product work: show featured owners their days to fill against unfeatured neighbors, and survey again next season. Stated willingness to pay sets today's ceiling; value customers can see is what raises it.
