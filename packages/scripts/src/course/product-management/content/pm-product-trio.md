Here are two ways Roost's founder could hand the team its work for the season:

```text
Brief A:  Build an availability calendar that owners update daily,
          shown on every listing. Due May 15.

Brief B:  Students keep reaching rooms that are already full, and
          they stop trusting us. Cut the share of booking requests
          that hit a full room from 13% to under 5% by July 1.
```

Under Brief A, the team ships the calendar on time. Owners, many of whom run their hostels from a phone and a notebook, stop updating it within weeks, and the calendar starts to lie. Under Brief B, the designer joins you in owner interviews and hears that nobody will maintain a calendar, but most will answer a yes-or-no WhatsApp message. The tech lead proposes sending that message automatically every Monday and marking any listing that stays silent as unconfirmed. The best idea came from the people who were given the problem, not the answer.

## Feature teams and empowered teams

Marty Cagan names the difference. In "Product vs. Feature Teams" (2019), expanded in _Empowered_ (2020, with Chris Jones), a feature team is cross-functional but is handed features from a roadmap and measured on output: did it ship? The stakeholder who asked for the feature effectively owns whether it creates value, yet the team gets the blame when it does not, and the PM spends most of the week on project management. An empowered product team is handed problems to solve, judged on outcomes, and trusted to find the solution. Brief A makes a feature team. Brief B makes an empowered one.

## The trio

Teresa Torres, in _Continuous Discovery Habits_ (2021), gives the core of an empowered team a name: the product trio, a product manager, a designer and a software engineer, which she calls the minimum set of roles needed to build good digital products. The trio does discovery together, interviewing customers, choosing which problem to chase, generating solutions and testing them, instead of a relay in which the PM writes requirements, the designer draws them and the engineers code them.

Each member brings what the four risks lesson assigned them:

- **PM:** the customer, the data and the business constraints, and the call on value and viability.
- **Designer:** how the experience feels to use, and the call on usability.
- **Tech lead:** what is possible and cheap, including what new technology has just made possible, and the call on feasibility.

That split also settles deadlocks. The trio should argue freely, but when it is stuck, the person who owns the risk in dispute makes the call. The PM gets no tie-breaking vote on screens or architecture and must not take one. What the PM must not give away is the other half: answering for whether the result works for students and for Roost.

## Where it breaks

A trio inside a company that still runs on feature roadmaps is a feature team with better meetings. Empowerment is granted by leadership, which has to hand out problems and outcomes instead of features and dates; a PM can only make the case for it. And a trio is not a committee. Three people who must agree on everything move slower than one who decides, which is why the ownership rule matters. How the trio keeps talking to customers every week comes later in the course.

## Predict, then verify

The founder asks why the tech lead spends 4 hours a week in interviews and discovery sessions instead of writing code. "Give the engineers a spec and let them build." Is the time worth it?

Answer: Yes, and the arithmetic is lopsided. Four hours of a 40-hour week is 10% of one engineer, or 2% of a five-engineer team. Over a 12-week season that is 48 hours, about 1.2 engineer-weeks. One wrong feature at Roost's scale, three weeks of work for all five engineers, costs 15 engineer-weeks. If the tech lead's presence prevents one wrong build a season, or catches one feasibility surprise before it is designed around, the time pays for itself more than ten times over. The principle: the how often changes the what, so the people who own the how belong in the room where the what is decided.
