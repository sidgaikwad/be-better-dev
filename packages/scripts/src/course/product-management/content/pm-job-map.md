Roost's funnel dashboard tracks five steps: search, listing view, shortlist, deposit, booking. But a first-year's job starts weeks before her first search, when her family argues about budget, and ends ten months after the booking, when she tries to get her deposit back. The dashboard cannot see either end, so no one at Roost does.

## The video's version

The course's instructor breaks buying a t-shirt online into jobs: search and discovery; checking the details (fabric, size, price, quality); entering name and delivery address; entering payment details; and delivery. For each, the instructor asks what the current solution is and how to improve it. That habit is sound.

The steps themselves are the problem. They are the stages of one shop's checkout, not of the customer's job. "Enter payment details" describes how a particular solution works. And the list stops at delivery, so it never reaches the shirt that does not fit and has to go back, which for clothes bought unseen is part of the job.

## The universal job map

Lance Bettencourt and Tony Ulwick published the more rigorous version in "The Customer-Centered Innovation Map" (HBR, May 2008). They argue that every job is a process, that every job has the same structure, and that the job is separate from any solution. The eight steps:

1. **Define**: set goals and plan the approach.
2. **Locate**: gather the inputs the job needs.
3. **Prepare**: set up the inputs and the environment.
4. **Confirm**: check that everything is ready before committing.
5. **Execute**: carry out the job.
6. **Monitor**: check that it is going well.
7. **Modify**: adjust when it is not.
8. **Conclude**: finish, or get ready to do it again.

Their test for a valid step is that it states what the customer is trying to accomplish, not an action with a particular tool: "ascertain patient vital signs", not "check the monitor". Their example of what a map reveals: in washing clothes, the check for stains happens after the clothes are dried and folded, too late to fix anything. A machine that spotted stains during the cycle would move Confirm to where it could still help.

## Mapping Roost's job

For a first-year from Nagpur, the job is to secure a place to live near campus before term starts. Against today's WhatsApp group and broker:

1. **Define**: agree a budget and area with parents. Nobody in the family knows what rooms in Pune cost.
2. **Locate**: find rooms that fit. The group posts dozens a day; the broker shows what he has.
3. **Prepare**: arrange money, documents and a visit.
4. **Confirm**: check the room is real, safe, and fully priced. From 700 km away this is guesswork.
5. **Execute**: pay and commit, often in cash to the broker.
6. **Monitor**: make sure the room is held and ready.
7. **Modify**: change rooms if it is not as promised, usually losing the deposit.
8. **Conclude**: move out and recover the deposit.

Roost's verification does what the washing machine should: it moves Confirm ahead of Execute. Its screens cover Locate, Confirm and Execute. Define, Modify and Conclude belong to nobody, which makes them candidates, not conclusions, for the opportunity solution tree.

## Predict, then verify

In May, 12,000 first-year search sessions in Pune start on Roost. In 3,600 of them (30%) the family sets a maximum rent under ₹7,000, most see no verified results, and 1% book, against 9% for everyone else. An engineer proposes listing unverified rooms under ₹7,000 so results are never empty. Support notes show that when a caller tells a family what verified rooms near their college cost, about a third raise their budget. What do you build?

Answer: a Define-step fix, not a Locate-step patch. Build a page showing what verified rooms near each college cost and what the rent includes, made to share with parents and shown before the first search. Today the low-budget sessions yield 3,600 × 1% = 36 bookings. If a third (1,200) reset their budget and book like everyone else, that is 1,200 × 9% = 108, plus 2,400 × 1% = 24, for 132: nearly four times as many. Unverified listings would fill Locate's empty page by breaking Confirm, the step Roost is hired for. The principle: failures surface downstream of their cause. The empty page is at Locate, but the problem began at Define, and only a map of the job, not of your screens, shows that.
