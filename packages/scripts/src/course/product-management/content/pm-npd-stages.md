The course's instructor walks one hypothetical product from idea to shelf: a smart fitness tracker from a wearables company. Customer requests for longer battery life, and rivals' weak phone apps, supply the idea. Three concepts are drawn up, and the lightweight general-purpose one is prototyped and tested. A business analysis puts break-even at 50,000 units against 100,000 forecast for the first year. A pilot in a few markets sells 500 units in two months with 90% positive reviews, and the full launch sells 10,000 in its first month.

Every step in that story was a chance to stop the tracker, and that is what the process is for. Greg Stevens and James Burley pooled data on industrial innovation in 1997 and found roughly 3,000 raw ideas behind each commercial success: about 300 written up, 125 small projects, 4 major developments, 1.7 launches and 1 winner. A process for new products is mostly a process for killing ideas while they are cheap.

## The eight stages

The standard list comes from Philip Kotler's marketing textbooks, met in the product mix lesson:

1. **Idea generation**: a deliberate search for ideas.
2. **Idea screening**: dropping ideas that do not fit or cannot work.
3. **Concept development and testing**: describing the idea as a customer would see it, and asking target customers.
4. **Marketing strategy development**: the target market, positioning, price, channels and first-year goals.
5. **Business analysis**: projected sales, costs and profit.
6. **Product development**: building it, with prototypes and technical tests.
7. **Test marketing**: selling it in a limited, realistic setting.
8. **Commercialization**: the full launch.

The video folds marketing strategy into business analysis and adds a final review of sales, feedback and competitors' responses after launch. Keep it: in the video's example, post-launch feedback drives the tracker's app and firmware updates.

## Stages and gates

A list of stages says what work to do. It does not say who decides to stop. Robert G. Cooper, a McMaster University researcher who studied why new product projects succeed or fail, supplied that part. His book _Winning at New Products_ appeared in 1986, the name Stage-Gate first appeared in print in 1988, and a 1990 _Business Horizons_ article set the system out.

In Stage-Gate, each stage is a block of work that gathers evidence, and before each stage sits a gate: a meeting where the senior people who control the money judge the evidence against written criteria and decide go, kill, hold or recycle. Spending rises stage by stage, so the gates put the cheap decisions first. A gate that never kills anything has become a status meeting.

## What software compresses

The stages were written for factories and shelves, and in software some shrink:

- **Test marketing** becomes a beta, or a release to a few percent of users, in days rather than months.
- **Concept testing and development overlap**: a clickable prototype is both the concept and a first version.
- **Commercialization** needs no factory or stock; distribution is an app update.

In 2016 Cooper and a co-author described an Agile-Stage-Gate hybrid: sprints inside the stages, gates kept.

What software does not shrink is the cost of building the wrong thing. For Roost that cost is the five engineers' weeks and, since bookings arrive once a year, possibly a season. Screening, a concept test and a business case take days, and they can stop the build.

## Predict, then verify

It is January. The founder wants summer stays, two-month rooms for students on internships, live in Pune, Bengaluru and Hyderabad by May. The engineers estimate eight weeks for all five of them. There is a one-page idea and no evidence. "We're software," he says. "The launch is our test market." Which stages do you keep?

Answer: Keep the screen, a concept test and a business case, finished in three weeks: a few of the PM's and designer's days and a survey, against 40 engineer-weeks of build. Then merge development and test marketing: launch in one city in May with a kill line written in advance, such as a minimum number of paid stays, before the other two. The founder is right that a software launch can be a test market, but only for questions about the product. Whether owners will hold rooms for two months, and whether interns will pay, can be answered for far less than the build. The principle: software makes the late stages cheap, not the mistakes, so keep the gates that come before the expensive work.
