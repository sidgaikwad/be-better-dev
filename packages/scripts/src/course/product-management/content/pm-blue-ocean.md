In 1984, street performers in Quebec started a circus in a declining industry. Audiences were drifting elsewhere, children to video games, and animal-rights groups were campaigning against animal acts. In under twenty years, Kim and Mauborgne later reported, Cirque du Soleil reached revenues that Ringling Bros. and Barnum & Bailey took more than a century to reach. It did not beat other circuses at what circuses did. It stopped doing most of it.

## Red oceans and blue oceans

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne of INSEAD named the idea in Harvard Business Review in 2004, then in a 2005 book, _Blue Ocean Strategy_. Red oceans are today's industries, with known boundaries, where companies fight over existing demand. Blue oceans are market space not yet in existence, where demand is created rather than fought over. The way in is value innovation: raising value for buyers while cutting your own costs, rather than choosing between differentiation and low cost as in the generic strategies lesson.

The course's instructor describes a blue ocean as a market with no competitor, one that does not exist until you create it. The authors are more modest: most blue oceans, they say, come from within red oceans, by stretching an industry's boundaries.

## The four actions

Their tool is four questions about the factors an industry competes on:

- **Eliminate:** which factors the industry takes for granted should go?
- **Reduce:** which should fall well below the industry's standard?
- **Raise:** which should rise well above it?
- **Create:** what should be offered that the industry never has?

Cirque eliminated star performers and animal shows, reduced thrill and danger, raised the venue, and created a theme with artistic music and dance, borrowed from theater. It sold to adults and corporate clients at several times a circus ticket's price. The eliminations paid for the creations: stars and animals were among a circus's heaviest costs.

Roost's grid, against brokers and WhatsApp groups:

| Action    | Roost                                               |
| --------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Eliminate | The broker's fee, about ₹2,000                      |
| Reduce    | Choice: only rooms the team has visited             |
| Raise     | Checked photos, and every charge before the deposit |
| Create    | A shortlist a parent in another city can approve    |

A grid with nothing under eliminate and reduce describes a more expensive product, not a new curve.

## Why the ocean does not stay blue

- **Uncontested depends on where you draw the line.** Cirque had no circus rival for its audience, but it competed with theater and every other night out for adults' money. No app matches Roost's curve either, yet it still fights brokers, seniors and relatives for every booking.
- **New space fills up.** A 2010 study by Andrew Burke, André van Stel and Roy Thurik tested it on 41 Dutch shop types, 1982 to 2000. New market space did pay: firm profits and the number of firms rose together. But the spaces filled with rivals as they grew, and competition eroded the innovators' profits over about 15 years.
- **The cases are chosen after the fact.** The book's examples are winners read backward; companies that eliminated and created, then failed, are absent.

Use the grid to design a curve that is costly to copy, and plan on being copied anyway.

## Predict, then verify

A rival app in Bengaluru offers students ₹1,000 cashback per booking; the founder wants to match it. The alternative is a move-in guarantee: if a room does not match its listing, Roost pays for a week in another verified room, about ₹2,100 at ₹9,000 a month, and finds a replacement. About 3 in 100 Roost bookings end in that kind of dispute; for an app that does not visit its rooms, assume 15 in 100. Which do you ship?

Answer: the guarantee. Matching the cashback costs ₹1,000 against ₹720 of commission, a ₹280 loss per booking, and the rival can raise its offer next week: price is the factor anyone can copy. The guarantee costs Roost 3% × ₹2,100 = ₹63 a booking. A rival that copies the promise without visiting rooms pays 15% × ₹2,100 = ₹315, five times as much. It creates a factor the industry never offered, and ties its cost to the one thing Roost does that others skip. It is not a blue ocean, since brokers and the rival remain. The principle: compete on a factor you created and can deliver more cheaply than anyone copying it, not on price.
