The course's opening story gives its product manager, Priya, a first step it calls finding product-market fit: before anything is built, she interviews students, owners and wardens and hears about fake listings and hidden charges. That is good discovery. It is not finding fit in the original sense, because fit is something a product shows in a market once people can use it. The video uses the phrase for that pre-build check; this section uses the original.

## Andreessen's definition

Marc Andreessen, co-founder of Netscape and later of the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz, published "The only thing that matters" on his blog on June 25, 2007. He defined product/market fit as "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market."

Both halves carry weight. He built on what he called Rachleff's law, after the investor Andy Rachleff: the market is the biggest single driver of a startup's success. A great team in a bad market loses; a weak team in a great market can still win. A good market has many reachable people with the problem who will pay, or whose parents will. A product that satisfies it is one they choose over what they did before: the broker at the college gate, the WhatsApp group, a cousin's spare room.

Before fit, he wrote, getting to fit is the only job: rewrite the product, change the team, move markets if that is what it takes. After fit, the job is scaling. A team that scales first pays to amplify a product nobody is pulling.

## What it looks like from inside

Andreessen described both states in terms a team can feel. Without fit, word of mouth does not spread, usage grows slowly and deals drag. With fit, customers buy as fast as you can deliver and you hire as fast as you can. For Roost:

- **Without fit:** bookings arrive only when you pay for them, owners do not list again, and students still ask the broker.
- **With fit:** students arrive because a friend sent them, owners ask to be listed, and the bottleneck is operations' verification visits, not demand.

## Fit belongs to a segment

"Does Roost have product-market fit?" has no single answer. Split an average month by the segment chosen in the lesson on picking who to serve first, first-year students moving to a new city:

```text
Monthly active students            60,000
  First-year movers, 30%           18,000
  Other students                   42,000
Bookings                            4,000
  First-year movers, 58%            2,320
  Other students                    1,680

Bookings per 100 active students
  First-year movers   2,320 / 18,000 = 12.9
  Other students      1,680 / 42,000 =  4.0
  Blended             4,000 / 60,000 =  6.7
```

Same app, same month, more than three times the booking rate. Roost fits students who arrive in a strange city and cannot inspect a room before paying ₹27,000 upfront. It barely fits the rest, mostly returning students who know the city and have friends to ask. The lifecycle lesson's Hyderabad season showed the same split by city: 85% of Pune's owners would list again, 55% of Hyderabad's.

Fit is not permanent either. Brian Balfour argued in a December 2013 essay that markets move, and that each new segment a company enters must be earned again. So say it with its scope: "we have fit with first-year movers in Pune, not yet in Hyderabad, and not with returning students."

## Predict, then verify

Next season's growth budget of ₹1,800,000 can go one of two ways. Plan A, the growth lead's: a campaign aimed at the 42,000 other students, "our biggest under-served group". Lifting them from 4.0 to 5.0 bookings per 100 adds 420 bookings a month. Plan B: tie-ups with admissions offices at 30 more colleges in Pune and Bengaluru, expected to bring 3,000 more first-year movers a month into the app; at 12.9 per 100, that is 387 bookings. A is bigger on paper. Which do you fund?

Answer: B. Its 12.9 is measured; A's 5.0 is a hope, and the arithmetic hides why. The 42,000 are already active on Roost: they found the app, searched it, and did not book. A campaign changes who arrives, not what the product does for them once inside. The gap between arriving and booking is a product question, and its next step is discovery with those students, not advertising. This is Andreessen's order of operations applied per segment: scale where fit is proven, and treat a segment without fit as a product problem, not a marketing one.
