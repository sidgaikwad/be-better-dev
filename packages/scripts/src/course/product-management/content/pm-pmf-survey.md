In the summer of 2017, Superhuman, an email client, had been in development for two years, had 14 people, and had not launched. Its founder, Rahul Vohra, was under pressure to launch and had no way to say whether the product was ready. Andreessen's signs were no help: customers buying faster than you can deliver is a lagging indicator, visible only after launch. Vohra needed a number he could move before it, and described what he built in First Round Review in November 2018.

## Ellis's question

The number came from Sean Ellis, who ran early growth at Dropbox, LogMeIn and Eventbrite. He asks users one question: how would you feel if you could no longer use the product? The answers are very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed, and "I no longer use it". Comparing nearly a hundred startups, Ellis found that those where at least 40% answered "very disappointed" tended to grow sustainably, and those below it tended to struggle.

It is one practitioner's benchmark from his own sample, not a law, and it measures something narrower than Andreessen's definition: how much today's users would miss the product. Ask only recent users of its core. Superhuman surveyed users who had used it at least twice in the previous two weeks. It had 100 to 200 users to poll; Vohra reports that answers become directionally correct at around 40 responses.

## Superhuman's engine

Vohra's first score was 22%. What he did next is the part to copy:

1. **Segment.** Two more questions asked what type of person would benefit most and what the main benefit was. The "very disappointed" answers clustered among founders, managers, executives and business development people. Scoring only those roles lifted the result to about 33%, with no product change.
2. **Learn why lovers love it.** Their main benefit was speed, helped by keyboard shortcuts.
3. **Choose whose complaints count.** Ignore the "not disappointed"; Vohra calls them essentially a lost cause. Among the "somewhat disappointed", listen only to those whose main benefit was also speed. Their blockers included a mobile app, integrations, attachment handling and calendaring.
4. **Split the roadmap.** Half on what lovers already love, such as faster responses and more shortcuts, half on the blockers.
5. **Track it** weekly, monthly and quarterly.

Within three quarters the score reached 58%.

## Roost's version

Roost's students use it a few weeks a year, so "twice in two weeks" becomes "moved in through Roost in the last 60 days". The survey, with Vohra's three follow-ups, goes to 400 such students. 150 answer:

```text
Very disappointed, all        51 / 150 = 34%
  First-year movers           35 /  70 = 50%
  Other students              16 /  80 = 20%
```

Below 40% overall, 50% among first-year movers, the segment the previous lesson found booking at three times the others' rate. Survey and booking data point at the same students.

## Where the number misleads

- **Who answers.** Only 150 of 400 replied, and people who love a product reply more, so the true share is probably below 34%.
- **Narrowing until it passes.** Any score clears 40% if the segment shrinks far enough. Tristan Kromer wrote in 2014 that his own startup scored above 40% without having fit: respondents were rating the promise of a solution, and the market was too small. The segment must still be a good market. First-year movers, 18,000 active students a month and 58% of bookings, are.
- **Words, not behavior.** A survey records what people say they would feel. The next lesson checks what they do.

## Predict, then verify

The improvement question produces two leading requests. A roommate finder is asked for by 30 respondents, 24 of them "not disappointed" returning students. Itemized charges shown before booking are asked for by 18, of whom 14 are "somewhat disappointed" first-year movers whose main benefit is knowing the room was real. The team can build one this quarter. Which?

Answer: itemized charges. The count is the wrong weight. Of the roommate finder's 30 votes, 24 come from people Vohra would politely ignore: so far from loving the product that one feature will not change their answer. The 14 are near-lovers who value exactly what the lovers value and are held back by something specific, and hidden charges are one of the problems Roost exists to solve. Converting them is how the 50% segment score climbs. Weight feedback by who gives it, not by how often it is given.
