The course's instructor builds a competitive landscape matrix by picking any two parameters, such as price and customer satisfaction, and plotting every competitor. His slide's quadrants (leaders, contenders, high performers, niche) match those of the review site G2's Grid, which plots software on user satisfaction against market presence. That suits a buyer shortlisting vendors. A PM needs to know where customers see a gap, and "any two parameters" breaks that, because the axes decide the answer. Roost's investor deck plots verified listings against app rating, with Roost alone in the top right, where every startup sits on axes it chose.

## Axes from buyers, positions from buyers

Perceptual maps entered marketing around 1970, when researchers led by Paul Green of Wharton applied multidimensional scaling, a technique from psychology, to consumers' ratings of brands. The statistics are optional. The discipline is not: axes and positions both come from customers.

1. Find the attributes buyers weigh, from research, not from your pitch.
2. Have buyers rate each alternative on the top two.
3. Plot the averages; look for clusters and empty space.
4. Ask why each empty space is empty before moving in.

Roost asked 300 parents of admitted first-years in Pune what mattered most: 58% chose knowing the room is as shown before paying, and 24% the total cost of moving in. They then rated five alternatives from 1 to 5 on both:

| Alternative                | Sure it is as shown | Cheap to move in |
| -------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------- |
| Managed residences         | 4.3                 | 1.8              |
| Roost                      | 4.0                 | 2.9              |
| WhatsApp group or a senior | 3.4                 | 3.8              |
| Broker                     | 3.1                 | 2.0              |
| Rental portals             | 1.9                 | 3.2              |

With certainty up the page and cheapness to the right, the map reads:

1. Top left: managed residences, with Roost just below and to the right.
2. Middle right: the WhatsApp group.
3. Middle left: the broker.
4. Bottom: rental portals.
5. Top right: nobody.

Parents place Roost nearest managed residences, not other apps: that is the comparison Roost must win. And the top right is empty. Of the ₹27,000 it costs to move in, ₹18,000 is the two-month deposit, which owners set and no platform has touched. Roost can work on that, through the instalments feature, so the corner is a hypothesis worth testing.

## Why feature tables mislead

A comparison grid on a pricing page is a map with forty axes and no customers. The author picks the rows, so it is built to win. A tick flattens quality: "verified listings" can mean a visit with dated photos or an owner uploading an ID, and both earn a tick. It counts features where buyers weigh outcomes. And matching a rival row for row buys sameness, not a reason to choose you.

The video's example, a project management startup, sums up its rivals in a line each: Asana strong at team collaboration, Trello simple but limited for complex work, Monday.com rich in features but costly for small teams. It then claims the gap: simple, powerful and affordable for small teams. Those lines are the team's opinions, not buyer ratings, and simple-and-powerful is the corner nearly every product claims. It looks empty because the two pull against each other.

## Predict, then verify

The founder redraws the map with monthly rent and amenities as the axes. One corner is empty: rooms under ₹6,000 with meals and air conditioning. In the survey, 34% of parents say they would choose one. He wants 200 such rooms listed before June. An owner's monthly cost per bed is about ₹3,000 for two meals a day, ₹800 for air conditioning, and ₹3,000 for the bed's share of lease, staff and upkeep. Is the corner an opportunity?

Answer: no. Cost per bed is ₹3,000 + ₹800 + ₹3,000 = ₹6,800, so every bed rented at ₹6,000 loses ₹800 a month. Nobody can supply the corner at a profit, and the axes are nearly one: amenities are what rent pays for. Owners who advertise it recover the gap through unlisted charges at move-in, averaging ₹3,500, so the 200 listings would import the hidden charges Roost exists to remove. The 34% is a wish, not a market. The principle: before moving into white space, name why it is empty. If nobody wants it, or nobody can supply it profitably, it is a wall. Only a reason you can remove, like the deposit on the parents' map, makes it a gap.
