Roost's quarterly review opens on a green roadmap. The team shipped eleven things in thirteen weeks, including photo reviews, owner chat and a cost breakdown on every listing, all on time. Then the founder puts up the chart she cares about: bookings are within 2% of the same quarter last year, and the share of bookings that end in a complaint about hidden charges has not moved. Nothing is visibly different.

## Three words for three different things

Josh Seiden's book _Outcomes Over Output_ (2019) gives that meeting its vocabulary. He borrows a chain from the logic models that foundations such as the Kellogg Foundation use to judge whether a program worked:

- **Output**: what the team makes. Features, releases, documents.
- **Outcome**: in Seiden's words, "a change in human behavior that drives business results". Someone does something differently because of what you made.
- **Impact**: the business result itself, such as revenue, profit or market share.

His test is one question: what will people do differently if this succeeds? For a better inbox search, he said on Intercom's podcast, the answer is that people find what they want on the first search and move on.

Here is the chain for one of Roost's eleven outputs. Early research found students surprised by electricity, food and maintenance charges after moving in.

```text
Output   Every listing shows rent, deposit, electricity, food and
         maintenance before a student can book.
Outcome  Students stop disputing charges after move-in.
         Bookings with a charges dispute within 30 days: 9% falls to 4%.
Impact   Fewer cancellations, less refunded commission.
         4,000 bookings x 9% = 360 disputes a month
         4,000 bookings x 4% = 160 disputes a month
         30% of disputes end in cancellation: 108 falls to 48, so 60 fewer
         60 x ₹720 commission (8% of ₹9,000) = ₹43,200 a month kept
```

Owners must change behavior too: a breakdown filled with zeros looks clean and still surprises the student, which may be why the dispute rate in that review never moved. In a marketplace, an output usually needs an outcome on each side.

## Why teams drift to output

Output is fully under the team's control, so a release date can be promised and met. Behavior belongs to students and owners, so an outcome goal feels like one you might miss. That is the reason to write it: only that kind of goal says whether the work mattered. A team measured on features shipped will ship many features and quietly stop asking whether any were used.

The course's instructor gives an example of the gap. A fitness app launches an AI coach, and three months later only 20% of users use it. Shipping the coach was the output. The 20% was the first outcome measure, and it said the output had not done its job.

Impact is the opposite trap: it moves slowly and has many causes outside the team. Roost's bookings swing with the admissions calendar more than with any feature, so "raise bookings" cannot tell a team in March whether this week's work helps. Outcomes sit in between: they move within weeks, the team can influence them, and each is a bet on impact that you check. The OKR lesson warned against key results that are tasks. That warning is this chain: a task is an output dressed as a result.

## Predict, then verify

Next quarter goes to instalment payments, the most requested feature in Roost's research. Each month 8,000 students reach the payment step and 4,000 finish, a 50% completion rate. The engineers propose "ship instalments in all three cities by 1 June", because they control it. The founder proposes "bookings up 10% this quarter", because investors see it. The PM proposes "payment-step completion from 50% to 58%". Which should the team own?

Answer: the PM's. Completion at the payment step is the behavior instalments exist to change, it moves within weeks, and the team's work is what moves it. Priced: 8,000 x 58% = 4,640 bookings, 640 more a month, worth 640 x ₹720 = about ₹460,000 in commission. The ship date is a milestone, not the goal, because it would score perfectly even if nobody paid in instalments. Bookings stay on the dashboard as impact, not as the target, because the June rush can move them more than 10% on its own. The principle: set a team's goal at the outcome, where behavior changes, and report output and impact around it.
