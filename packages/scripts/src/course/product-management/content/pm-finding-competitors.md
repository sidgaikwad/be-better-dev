The founder wants a competitor spreadsheet before Friday's investor call. His draft lists three student-housing apps down the side and thirty features across the top, each cell a tick or a cross. The real competition lesson showed what is wrong with it: when Roost asked 1,000 first-years in Pune where they went instead, every app and website combined took 4%. The format is fine. The rows and columns are wrong.

## Direct, indirect, future

The course's instructor sorts competitors into three kinds. The sort is common practice, not one author's framework:

- **Direct**: the same kind of product for the same customer. For Roost, other housing apps and portals, and Stanza Living, founded in 2017, which leases buildings and runs them as managed residences for students and young professionals in cities including Pune, Bengaluru and Hyderabad. Its model differs, but a family weighing the two is choosing between ways to get a room it can trust.
- **Indirect**: a different means to the same job, what the five forces lesson called a substitute. For Roost: the broker, the WhatsApp group, a senior's old room, the notice board at the college gate. In business software the classic indirect competitor is a spreadsheet: free, already open, and nobody has to approve it.
- **Future**: a company that does not compete today but already holds part of what the job needs.

## The sheet, rebuilt

One row per alternative, not per company, and columns that lead to decisions rather than features:

| Alternative                                   | Kind     | Share of lost first-years | What it does better            |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Broker                                        | Indirect | 38%                       | The family sees the room first |
| WhatsApp group or a senior                    | Indirect | 27%                       | Someone they trust vouches     |
| Relatives, a late hostel seat, walking around | Indirect | 31%                       | Free, or seen in person        |
| Other apps, Stanza included                   | Direct   | 4%                        | Varies by rival                |
| A rental portal that starts verifying         | Future   | none yet                  | Already has owners and traffic |
| The college's own housing list                | Future   | none yet                  | Reaches students at admission  |

The working sheet adds two columns: the dated evidence behind each row, and what would make it a bigger threat. A row whose evidence is a year old is a guess.

## Finding the ones that are not there yet

A survey of lost customers cannot find a future competitor, because nobody has left for it yet. Work from the job instead: list what doing it takes, then ask who already holds a piece.

1. **The customer at the moment of need.** The college knows every admitted student weeks before Roost does.
2. **The supply.** A national rental portal already has thousands of owners; a managed-living operator already has beds.
3. **The capability Roost is built on.** Anyone with a field team that visits properties, or a lender that could offer rent instalments directly.

A company holding two of the three is one decision away from competing.

Each sheet covers one segment and one side. Owners hire Roost to fill rooms, so their sheet lists a "to let" board, the broker again, and simply waiting. Review each one quarterly, and again before admissions open.

## Predict, then verify

A rival app announces that from next June it will visit and verify every listing in Pune: Roost's one unique attribute. The founder points at the sheet: apps took 4% of lost first-years, so this rival deserves no more attention than before. Last June, 5,000 first-years in Pune searched Roost without booking, and about 1,500 booked. Is he right?

Answer: no. The 4% measures where lost students went while no rival verified; it cannot measure a rival that does not yet do the thing. Apps took 5,000 × 4% = 200 of the students Roost lost. This rival threatens the 1,500 Roost won. If it takes one in five, that is 300 bookings, or 300 × ₹720 = ₹216,000 in commission in June alone. So it moves to the top of the direct rows with a named trigger: each month, count how many of Roost's Pune properties it has visited, since verifying at scale needs the same owners. The principle: size indirect alternatives by where lost customers go, and size direct and future rivals by how much of your own base they could take if they copied what makes you different.
