In one June week in Pune, 900 first-year students opened the deposit screen on a verified Roost listing, and 540 closed it without paying. Each had searched, shortlisted and messaged an owner. On exit calls most said a version of this: I liked the room, I needed a room, and I was not going to send money to someone I had never met for a room I had never seen. Many took a broker's room the next day.

## Four forces on every switch

Bob Moesta (whom Alan Klement credits with the milkshake fieldwork) and Chris Spiek drew every switch as four forces:

1. **Push of the situation**: what is wrong with how things are now.
2. **Pull of the new solution**: the better life the customer pictures with it.
3. **Anxiety of the new solution**: what might go wrong after switching.
4. **Habit of the present**: the comfort of what they already do, even when it is bad.

Push and pull drive a switch; anxiety and habit hold it back, and the switch happens only when the first pair outweighs the second. For a first-year moving from the WhatsApp group and a broker to Roost:

```text
Push:    rooms posted in the group are gone by the time she calls;
         the broker's quote left out a maintenance charge
Pull:    someone from Roost has visited the room;
         the full monthly cost is on the listing
Anxiety: paying online to an owner she has never met;
         the room might not match its photos
Habit:   seniors vouch for the broker they used;
         her father has always seen a room before paying for it
```

The 540 had strong push and pull, or they would never have reached the payment screen. Anxiety stopped them, and habit caught them the next morning.

## The switch interview

Moesta finds the forces with the switch interview: talk to people who recently switched, or nearly did, and rebuild the decision like a documentary. The timeline runs:

1. **First thought**: the old way stops being good enough (the hostel list without her name).
2. **Passive looking**: noticing options without acting (reading the WhatsApp group).
3. **Active looking**: comparing seriously against a deadline, Roost against the broker.
4. **Deciding**: trading off, because no option does everything.
5. **First use, then ongoing use**: move-in day, and whether the room matched.

Keep asking what happened that made them realize that. As the lesson on questions about the past argued, a specific past decision is evidence and a hypothetical one is not. Interview the ones who stalled too; the 540 are the richest source.

## Where teams put their effort

Moesta observes that companies pour effort into pull (features, benefits, advertising) and neglect anxiety and habit, which are often cheaper to move. His best-known case: a Detroit-area builder selling condos to downsizers saw visitors like the units and leave without buying. Interviews with people who had bought traced the blocker to the dining table where a family had gathered for decades. The builder added moving help and storage, and sales rose; retellings disagree on how much. The condos did not change. The anxiety did.

Where the model breaks: the forces are not quantities. Scoring each from 1 to 5 and adding them up is arithmetic on opinions. Use the forces to sort what interviewees said and to choose which one to work on.

## Predict, then verify

Roost can fund one project before July. A, video tours of every listing, adds pull: a test suggests 10% more students would reach the deposit screen, with the same 40% paying. B holds the deposit until the student checks in and confirms the room matches, refunding it in full if not. Of the 540 who left, 320 named the payment worry, and a one-college pilot suggests a third of such students pay when the money is held. Which ships?

Answer: B. The baseline is 900 × 40% = 360 bookings a week. A brings 990 to the screen, and 990 × 40% = 396, a gain of 36. B converts a third of 320, about 107 more, for 467: three times A's gain. A adds pull to people who were already pulled; they had reached the payment screen. B costs something (owners are paid days later, and Roost carries refunds), but verification keeps mismatches rare. The principle: find the binding force. When people reach the edge of a switch and stop, reduce anxiety, because more pull is wasted on them.
