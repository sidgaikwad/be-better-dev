The course's instructor tells quick commerce as a story about adapting: Swiggy had a food delivery roadmap, Zepto arrived promising 10-minute delivery, and Swiggy launched Instamart and moved people onto it. The lesson holds; the order does not.

Swiggy launched Instamart first, in August 2020 in Gurugram, promising groceries in 30 to 45 minutes. Zepto launched in Mumbai in 2021 with a 10-minute promise. Grofers, founded in 2013, introduced 10-minute delivery in August 2021 and renamed itself Blinkit on 13 December 2021. Swiggy's move came on 2 December 2021, when it committed $700 million to Instamart and set a goal of 15-minute deliveries by January 2022. So Swiggy did not invent a response. When customers' idea of fast moved from 45 minutes to 10, it re-planned a bet it already had, which is what adapting usually looks like. Flipkart launched its 10-minute service only in August 2024, and Amazon in 2025.

## Why incumbents move late, or move wrong

The instructor blames inertia: companies fix a yearly roadmap and execute it whatever the market does, and bigger companies find change harder. Donald Sull, in "Why Good Companies Go Bad" (HBR, 1999), sharpened this. The usual failure, he argued, is not paralysis but active inertia: doing what worked before, harder.

His main case, Firestone, saw radial tires coming through its European business. After Ford announced in 1972 that its new cars would have radials, it quickly invested nearly $400 million in radial production. But it ran the new business with its old processes and relationships, and its CEO kept assuming ever-growing demand, although radials last twice as long as the old tires. Firestone lost much of its US market and was bought by Bridgestone in 1988. It acted fast and still failed.

Sull names four things that harden: frames into blinders, processes into routines, relationships into shackles, values into dogmas. Before asking "What should we do?", he says, ask "What hinders us?" In a product team it is usually the roadmap: written for last season's market, defended because it was promised.

## Signal, test, re-plan, tell

The video's steps: analyze the insight, assess the current strategy, define adjustments, validate them with pilots or small launches, communicate, and monitor. Three habits make them work:

1. **Test the signal before designing the response.** Andy Grove, in _Only the Paranoid Survive_ (1996), offered the silver bullet test: if you had one bullet, which competitor would you use it on? When the answer changes, or your team disagrees, something has shifted. If the diagnosis from the strategy kernel lesson still holds, the news is noise.
2. **Validate the response at the smallest size that answers the question**, then re-plan around the result.
3. **Say what changed and what did not**: the new diagnosis, what stops, what stays, and the result that would reverse the call.

## Predict, then verify

Last season, bookings from first-years at four large Pune colleges fell from 1,600 to 880 after those colleges put lists of approved PGs on their admission pages. Bookings from every other college fell 3%, from 7,000 to 6,790, and admissions at the four were flat. Six more colleges, which brought Roost 2,100 bookings last year, plan lists for this June. The founder calls it noise and wants the roadmap untouched: instalments on June 1, then an owner dashboard redesign. The head of operations wants to pause instalments and build for colleges. What do you do?

Answer: neither. The drop is a signal. The four kept 880 / 1,600 = 55% of their bookings, a 45% fall, and the other colleges, down 3%, rule out the city and the market. The 1,600 - 880 = 720 lost bookings cost ₹518,400 in commission. If the six follow at the same 45%, 2,100 × 45% = 945 more, or ₹680,400. But the response is untested: will colleges take Roost's listings, and will students book through them? So move one engineer off the dashboard for four weeks to offer two of the four colleges a free feed of Roost-verified rooms, keeping the other two as a comparison. Instalments ships on June 1 and the dashboard slips a month. Bookings happen only in season, so waiting for more evidence costs a year. Tell the team what changed, what slips, and the number that decides next year: if the pilot colleges win back half their lost bookings, colleges become next season's main bet. The principle: when customer behavior shifts against a comparison group, act at once, at the size of a test.
