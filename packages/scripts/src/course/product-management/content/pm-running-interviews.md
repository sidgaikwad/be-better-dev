Roost's trio ran eight student interviews in a week, unrecorded, with the PM's notebook as the only record. At planning ten days later, the PM remembers "students want trust", the designer remembers a student who could not find the deposit amount, and the tech lead missed half the calls. Eight interviews have shrunk to three opinions.

The conversation is the middle third of the work. Who you talk to decides what you can hear, and what you write down that day decides what survives.

Talk to people who did the thing recently: for Roost, students who sent a booking request in the last 30 days, including some who gave up, who often explain more. Screen on behavior ("When did you last look for a room?"), not demographics. Keep friends, family and colleagues out; they are the mothers of the Mom Test. Owners get their own recruiting and script, and so do parents when the question is who pays.

## A loose script, two roles

A script is a guide, not a questionnaire; only its opening, story question and close are fixed:

```text
Open (2 min)
  "We're learning how students find rooms. There are no right
   answers, and we have nothing to sell you today."
  Ask to record. Say who will hear it and when it is deleted.
Context (3 min)
  College, year, where you live now.
Story (15-20 min)
  "Tell me about the last time you looked for a room."
  Probes: what happened next, what did it cost, what did you try.
Close (5 min)
  "Is there anything I should have asked?"
  "Who else should we talk to?"
```

Two people run it. The interviewer holds the conversation and nothing else. The note-taker writes what was said, in the student's words, and marks interpretations separately. In a trio, rotate the roles and let the third person listen: an engineer who hears about a ₹4,000 surprise charge argues differently at planning.

Never pitch. If asked what you are building, offer to show it at the end. Once the idea is on the table, the answers turn into compliments.

## The snapshot, the same day

Teresa Torres's answer to the notebook problem is the interview snapshot: one page made right after each interview, while the story is fresh, which practiced teams finish in 15 to 20 minutes. One for Roost:

```text
Ananya, first-year B.Com, moved from Nagpur to Pune in July
Quote:          "The listing said ₹8,500. The first bill said ₹12,500."
Story:          Cousin visited 3 PGs. Father paid the deposit by UPI.
                Owner asked for ₹4,000 "maintenance" on day one.
Opportunities:  I can't tell what I'll really pay each month.
                My father won't pay until someone has seen the room.
Insight:        The family, not the student, decides on the visit.
```

Her template adds a photo, quick facts and a small drawing of the story. Anyone on the team can read one in a minute, weeks later.

## How many before a pattern is a pattern

Two studies give an order of magnitude. Abbie Griffin and John Hauser (1993) estimated that 20 to 30 interviews surfaced 90 to 95% of customer needs in a relatively homogeneous segment, and a single interview about a third. Greg Guest and colleagues (2006) found that almost all the themes in their interviews had appeared by the twelfth, and the basic ones by the sixth.

Both numbers are per segment, and Roost has at least three: students, owners, and the parents who pay. A workable rule: 5 or 6 matching stories in one segment are a pattern worth testing, and a segment is covered when new interviews stop adding opportunities.

## Predict, then verify

The founder has read 9 snapshots: 6 from Pune first-years, 3 from owners. Five of the 6 students describe an unlisted first-month charge. He wants 30 more interviews "to be sure". Your designer wants to redesign how every listing shows its price. What do you do?

Answer: Neither. Five matching stories in one segment make hidden charges a real opportunity for Pune first-years, enough to start testing solutions cheaply but not to redesign every listing. Thirty more in that segment would mostly repeat it; Griffin and Hauser's curve flattens for that reason. Spend the next interviews where you are blind: owners, where 3 conversations cannot say why charges go unlisted, and students in Bengaluru and Hyderabad. The principle: count evidence per segment, act once stories converge, and interview where you know least.
