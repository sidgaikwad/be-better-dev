A slide in Roost's investor deck says Roost moved ₹432,000,000 of rent last year. It is true, and anyone reading it as revenue would think Roost 12.5 times its real size. A marketplace has its own chain of numbers, each answering a different question.

## From GMV to contribution

- **Gross merchandise value (GMV)** is the value of the transactions on the platform: 48,000 bookings x ₹9,000 of first month's rent = ₹432,000,000 a year.
- **Take rate** is the share the platform keeps: 8% for Roost.
- **Net revenue** is GMV x take rate: ₹34,560,000 a year, or ₹720 a booking.
- **Contribution per transaction** is net revenue minus the costs each transaction brings with it.

Roost's average booking:

```text
Commission, 8% of ₹9,000                            ₹720
Payment fees, about 1% of the ₹9,000 Roost collects  -₹90
Support: 360 disputes a month x ₹1,000 / 4,000       -₹90
Verification: ₹720,000 a year / 48,000 bookings      -₹15
Contribution per booking                            ₹525
```

That is 73% of net revenue and under 6% of GMV. Verification is small here because the ₹720,000 re-verifies properties Roost already has; a new property costs more.

GMV only compares when defined the same way. Count the year's rent a booking produces, about ₹90,000 as the business models lesson did, and Roost's GMV is ten times larger while its take rate falls to 0.8%. Airbnb's gross booking value includes host earnings, service fees, cleaning fees and taxes; for 2025 it reported $91.3 billion of it against $12.2 billion of revenue, about 13.4%. How high a take rate can go is strategy, not arithmetic. Bill Gurley's 2013 essay "A Rake Too Far" argues that a high take rate invites competitors, sends suppliers to other platforms and raises prices for buyers, quoting Jeff Bezos: "Your margin is my opportunity."

## Liquidity and the scarce side

Liquidity is the chance that someone who comes to transact succeeds: for Roost, the chance that a search ends in a booking. In an average month 40,000 students search and 4,000 book, so 10%. The metric tree showed where the rest go, and one line stands out: 8,000 students send a booking request and half are accepted. Half the students who chose a room and asked for it never got one, most often because the bed was already gone. Demand is not what is missing.

Price one more booking from each side:

```text
Demand: the next paid booking (Instagram)              ₹900
Supply: one verified property = ₹600 visit / 75% pass   ₹800
        bookings in its first season                     5
        verification per booking = ₹800 / 5            ₹160
```

A rupee spent on verified supply buys over five times as many bookings as a rupee spent on paid demand, and the property keeps producing. An average one brings 40 bookings a year (48,000 / 1,200), about ₹21,000 of contribution, against ₹903 for a student across her whole degree. Even in its first season, a new property's booking earns ₹720 - ₹90 - ₹90 - ₹160 = ₹380.

Supply is often the constraint in a marketplace: suppliers are fewer, each serves many buyers, and joining takes more work than browsing. For Roost every property must also pass a visit before anyone can book it.

## Predict, then verify

It is April in Pune. The marketing lead wants ₹450,000 to take student ambassadors to more colleges, at the ₹500 a booking they cost last season: 900 bookings, at a measured price. Operations wants ₹120,000 for 200 extra verification visits before June; at a 75% pass rate that is 150 properties at about 5 bookings each, 750 bookings, from an average rather than a measurement. Which do you fund?

Answer: Operations, which wins even on paper. The ambassadors' 900 bookings earn 900 x ₹525 = ₹472,500 against ₹450,000, leaving ₹22,500. The new properties' bookings earn ₹720 - ₹90 - ₹90 = ₹540 each before verification, so 750 x ₹540 = ₹405,000, less ₹120,000 of visits, leaves ₹285,000. And the paper overstates the ambassadors: Pune already turns away half its requests, so more students mostly meet full beds. If only half of their bookings happen, 450 x ₹525 - ₹450,000 is a loss of ₹213,750. The visits need no new demand, because they give beds to requests Roost already receives and refuses; even at 3 bookings per property they leave 450 x ₹540 - ₹120,000 = ₹123,000. The principle: a marketplace grows at the pace of its scarcer side, and money spent on the abundant side mostly buys rejections.
