Roost's investor deck has a competitor slide with three student-housing apps on it. In June, Roost asked 1,000 first-year students in Pune who had searched but not booked where they ended up living:

| Where they went                            | Share |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----- |
| A broker                                   | 38%   |
| The WhatsApp group, or a senior's old room | 27%   |
| Relatives' home, commuting to college      | 14%   |
| A college hostel seat that opened late     | 11%   |
| Walking the streets near campus            | 6%    |
| Another app or website                     | 4%    |

Every app and website combined, the three on the slide included, took 4%. The other 96% went to things the slide does not mention.

## Competition is set by the job

The value proposition lesson named the broker and the WhatsApp group as Roost's real alternatives. Jobs to be done gives the rule behind that: a competitor is anything a customer hires for the same job, in whatever category it sits. The milkshake's rivals were bananas, bagels, doughnuts and boredom. Alan Klement named his 2016 book _When Coffee and Kale Compete_ for the same reason.

Sorted by kind, Roost's competition looks like this:

- **Direct rivals**: other apps and portals, the only ones on the slide.
- **Services**: the broker, hired for the Locate and Confirm steps, because the family sees rooms in person.
- **Workarounds**: the WhatsApp group, a senior's old room, notice boards, a class spreadsheet of PGs.
- **Nonconsumption**: hiring nothing new, such as commuting from an aunt's house or waiting for a hostel seat. The term is Christensen and Raynor's, from _The Innovator's Solution_ (2003).

## Why new products lose to doing nothing

The four forces explain the table. Every alternative except the apps is already familiar, so habit is on its side, and none asks the family to pay an unknown company online, so anxiety stays low. Doing nothing also costs nothing up front. A rival app faces the same uphill climb Roost does. So an early product's likeliest loss is not to a rival but to customers who try it, feel the risk, and go back to what they did before.

The useful question for each alternative is what it does better for the job:

- The broker lets a family see a room before paying.
- The WhatsApp group has seniors who vouch for places.
- The relatives' home is free, and a parent approves of it at once.

Each is something to beat or neutralize, and none would appear on a feature grid of apps.

## Where it breaks

State the job too broadly and everything competes. "Help me have a good first year" competes with a gym, a bicycle and a phone plan, and the list tells you nothing. Keep the job at the level where the customer actually chose among the options: this student, this June, choosing among a broker, the group, an aunt's spare room and Roost. And the table covers one side of the marketplace. Property owners hire Roost for a different job, filling their rooms, and their alternatives need a table of their own.

## Predict, then verify

A rival app launched 360-degree photos, and the founder wants to match them, at six engineer-weeks. The same six weeks could build "see before you pay": families book visit slots with owners through Roost and pay nothing until after the visit, taking on the broker's advantage directly. In June, 5,000 first-years in Pune searched without booking, and the table above says where they went. Assume the photos win back a quarter of those lost to apps, and visits win back a tenth of those lost to brokers. Which do you build?

Answer: visits. Apps took 5,000 × 4% = 200 students, and a quarter of that is 50 bookings. Brokers took 5,000 × 38% = 1,900, and a tenth is 190 bookings, nearly four times as many with the smaller win-back rate. At ₹720 in commission each, that is ₹136,800 against ₹36,000. The rival is the competitor you can see, in app stores and investor questions, so it gets the attention; the broker is the one taking the customers. The principle: size competition by where lost customers actually go, then compete on the thing that alternative does better for the job.
