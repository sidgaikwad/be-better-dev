A hiring manager has 30 portfolios to read. Two cover the same project. The first opens: "Redesigned the owner listing flow in Figma. 12 screens, new design system, dark mode." The second opens: "54 of the 90 owners who started a listing each month abandoned it. Eight owner interviews traced it to a documents step asking for papers owners did not have at hand. We moved the documents to the verification visit. Abandonment fell to 31 of 90 over two months." The first shows output. The second shows a decision and its result, and gets the call.

## What the video asks for

The video's final roadmap step turns your work into a case study, which it calls the thing that turns an aspiring PM into someone with proof of execution. It should tell the whole story: the problem you solved, the research you did, how you built the MVP, the feedback you received and how you iterated, with screenshots, metrics, and even failures and what you learned. It suggests publishing on Notion, Medium or as a Loom walkthrough, for use in interviews, pitch decks and LinkedIn posts.

The list is right. It leaves out order and length, which decide whether anyone finishes reading.

## A structure that survives skimming

```text
Title        The outcome, not the activity
             "Cutting listing abandonment from 60% to 34%"
Summary      Five sentences: problem, number, what you did, result, lesson
1. Context   The product, the users, your role and team size
2. Problem   The number that was wrong, and why it mattered
3. Research  What you did to find the cause; what surprised you
4. Options   Two or three you considered, and why you rejected them
5. What shipped   The smallest version, with one or two screenshots
6. Results   Numbers with a time window and a baseline
7. What failed    The thing that did not work, and what you changed
8. Next      What you would do with another quarter
```

Aim for 800 to 1,500 words, readable in five minutes. The summary carries the most weight: many readers stop there. Section 4 is where judgment shows: a case study without rejected options reads as if the answer was obvious, and interviewers probe exactly there. Section 7 is not optional: a story with no failure reads as untrue or unambitious.

Three rules for honesty. Say "I" for what you did and "we" for what the team did, because the interviewer will ask which was which. Give results as before and after with the window ("60% to 34% over two months"), never as "improved engagement". If your employer's numbers are confidential, use ratios or indexed values and say so.

Write it first, in Notion or Medium: writing forces the argument into order. Then record a five-minute Loom from the written version for interviews. A Loom made without the writing tends to wander.

## Without a PM job yet

Without the title, use work that had real users: a side project, a feature you drove as an engineer, an internal tool. A redesign of a famous app with no users behind it shows taste, not judgment, because nothing could prove it wrong.

This course gives you practice material. Across it you made Roost decisions with numbers behind them: refusing unverified listings in Chennai when supply fell short, finding the map pin behind "farther than shown" cancellations, reading September's drop against last year. Write one up with the structure above as practice, labeled a course exercise, not work experience: the skill transfers, and the label keeps you credible.

## Predict, then verify

A candidate has room for one featured case study. Project A: a hackathon app with nine features, polished screens, and 40 users who never came back. Project B: a notes app for exam prep with 300 signups, 12% of whom were still active in week four; after interviewing 10 of the dropouts, she shut it down after eight weeks because the retention curve never flattened. Which one leads?

Answer: B. It has a problem, research, a measured result, and a hard decision taken on evidence, which are exactly the parts an interviewer can probe. The shutdown shows she can read a retention curve, as the product-market fit lessons taught, and act on it. A shows output, and its only number, 40 users who left, raises a question the case study never answers. The principle: a portfolio sells judgment demonstrated with evidence, and a well-reasoned failure demonstrates more of it than a polished success nobody measured.
