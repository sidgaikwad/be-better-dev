Roost's first persona came from a branding agency: "Riya, 19, from Nagpur. Loves K-pop and cold coffee. Three hours a day on Instagram. Dreams of studying abroad." It had a stock photo and sat on the wiki for a season. Asked which decision it had changed, the team could name none. Every line described Riya. None said what she was trying to do, or what stopped her.

## A fiction built from facts

Alan Cooper began using personas in 1983, while designing a project-management program: he play-acted a user modeled on Kathy, a woman he had interviewed. He published the idea in _The Inmates Are Running the Asylum_ (1999). His personas are defined by goals and behavior: one specific, archetypal user, not an average of everyone.

The video's definition agrees: a fictional but realistic representation of your ideal user, grounded in interviews, behavior and feedback. The name and face are made up; everything else must be traceable. Roost's replacement:

```text
Ananya: first-year, moving to a new city
Evidence: 14 interviews (May 2026); first-year booking data

Context      Admitted to a college in Pune, 700 km from home.
             Searches on her phone in June, three weeks before term.
             Cannot see a room before paying. A parent pays.
Goal         A safe room near campus, booked before she leaves
             home, at a cost her parents agreed.
Frustrations Photos that did not match the room (9 of 14)
             Charges that appeared after the deposit (7 of 14)
             Owners who went quiet at a video-call request (5 of 14)
Quote        "I can't take a train to Pune just to look at a room."
Scenario     Shortlists four PGs, forwards two to her father on
             WhatsApp; they pick the one showing the full cost.
```

## Building one, and what to leave out

1. Research: interviews, plus analytics, support tickets and reviews.
2. Find the few behaviors where people differ: can they visit before paying, who pays, how far ahead they search.
3. Group people who answer those the same way. Each tight group is a candidate persona.
4. Write it with the parts the video lists: name, context, goals, frustrations, a real quote, a short scenario.

The video's rule for step 4 is the one agencies break: no favorite TV shows or pizza unless they change how the product is used. Every line must help make a product decision. Riya's K-pop fails that test. Ananya's "cannot see a room before paying" shapes the whole listing page.

Keep the set small; the video says to start with one or two. Cooper and Robert Reimann's _About Face 2.0_ (2003) is stricter: design each interface for a single primary persona, and give secondary personas only additions that do not break it. And date the evidence: research goes stale, so rerun the counts each season and rewrite the lines they contradict.

## Where personas fail

Nielsen Norman Group separates proto-personas (the team's assumptions), qualitative personas (from interviews) and statistical personas (interview findings tested by a survey at scale). A proto-persona is a fair start in week one if it is labeled as assumptions. It does harm when the label falls off and a guess passes for a finding.

Some jobs-to-be-done advocates go further: personas invite teams to design for who someone is, not what they are trying to get done. That critique lands on Riya, not on Ananya. Write the persona around the circumstance and the job, and the two work together: the job says what progress is wanted, the persona says in what life.

## Predict, then verify

The designer is laying out the listing page. Ananya's group makes 58% of bookings and needs proof the room is real: visit date, dated photos, full cost. Karthik, a second-year moving within the city, stands for the other 42%: he wants rent and distance, and inspects rooms in person. The designer proposes splitting the first screen evenly between them. What do you decide?

Answer: Make Ananya the primary persona for this page and lead with verification and full cost. The reason is not only 58% against 42%. Karthik's needs are met elsewhere: rent and distance appear in search results and filters, and he visits. Ananya's need can be met only here. As a secondary persona, Karthik gets rent and distance just below the proof: a scroll for him, nothing lost for her. An even split answers neither person's question first. The principle: one primary persona per interface, and secondary needs only where they do not break it.
