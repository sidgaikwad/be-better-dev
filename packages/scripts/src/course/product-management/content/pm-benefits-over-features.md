The course's instructor recalls an interview with an Indian advertising writer about a pressure-cooker campaign, probably from the early 1990s. The brief was simple: the new cooker cooks faster and saves time. The ad-maker objected that "saves time" is still the manufacturer talking. The person choosing between two cookers, in that era usually the woman who did the family's cooking, was not buying minutes. She was buying what the minutes turned into: time with her family, or time to rest. That is what the ad should say.

The details are loose, but the principle is older than the story: customers buy an outcome, and a feature is only the mechanism.

## The so-what ladder

The tool is one question, asked repeatedly: start from the feature and ask "so what?" until the answer is something the customer would say in their own words. For Roost:

```text
Feature:  Every property is visited by Roost's operations team before listing.
So what?  The photos, rent and rules on the listing were checked in person.
So what?  You will not travel 600 km to find a different room, or a
          surprise ₹5,000 "maintenance" charge on move-in day.
Parent:   You can send your child to a city you have never seen and
          know where they will sleep.
```

The video's example is smaller: "long battery life" is a feature, and "stay connected all day without worrying about recharging" is the benefit.

The feature does not disappear; it becomes the proof. Apple's press release for the first iPod, on 23 October 2001, led with 1,000 songs in your pocket. The 5 GB hard drive appeared further down, as the reason the claim was true. Lead with the benefit and keep the feature underneath it.

## AIDA: the order of one message

AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action) is usually credited to the advertising man E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1898, though a 2023 study by the marketing scholar Akinori Iwamoto found no evidence for that and credits early 1900s salesmanship writers. Treat it as a checklist for one message, not a model of how people decide. A June Instagram ad for Roost:

1. Attention: "Moving to Pune for college?" It names the situation, not the product.
2. Interest: "Every room on Roost was visited by our team."
3. Desire: "The rent you see is the rent you pay, and your parents can see it too."
4. Action: "See verified PGs near your campus."

Each segment's ladder tops out somewhere different, so one positioning yields several messages:

| Segment            | Channel                          | Lead benefit                                      |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| First-year student | Instagram, college groups        | The room in the photos is the room you get        |
| Parent who pays    | A listing shared on WhatsApp     | See where your child will live, and the full cost |
| Property owner     | A visit from the operations team | Students who arrive already decided               |

## Where it breaks

The rule fails in two directions. A benefit any rival could claim, such as "your home away from home", is a slogan, and it fails the swap test from the positioning statement lesson. And some buyers want the specification: an engineer choosing a database wants latency figures, not a promise of speed. For expert buyers, the feature often is the benefit.

## Predict, then verify

Roost tests two June ads, each shown to 20,000 students for the same ₹4,000. Ad A, a feature: "Every PG checked in person by our team." 1% click (200 clicks) and 10% of those book, so 20 bookings. Ad B, a benefit: "Your home away from home." 2% click (400 clicks) and 4% of those book, so 16 bookings. The campaign lead wants B for its doubled clicks. Which runs?

Answer: A, for now. Bookings pay and clicks do not: at ₹720 commission per booking (8% of ₹9,000), A earns 20 × ₹720 = ₹14,400 and B earns 16 × ₹720 = ₹11,520 on the same spend. B lost not because benefits lose but because "home away from home" names no specific pain, so it pulled clicks from students with no reason to choose Roost. The next test is the version this lesson argues for, a specific benefit with the feature as its proof: "The room in the photos is the room you get. Every PG checked in person." A vague benefit loses to a specific feature; a specific benefit backed by the feature is the one to bet on.
