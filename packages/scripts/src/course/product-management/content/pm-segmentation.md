Roost's first segmentation deck split its 60,000 monthly active students by gender and by city. Six slices, six pie charts, and a booking rate for each. Every rate landed between 6.3% and 7.1% a month, close to the overall 4,000 bookings out of 60,000 students, or 6.7%. The deck was accurate and changed nothing. If every group books at about the average, no slice tells you whom to build for or where to spend. A segmentation is worth something only when the groups behave differently.

## The four standard cuts

The course's instructor lists the four bases textbooks have taught since Wendell Smith introduced market segmentation as a strategy in 1956:

- **Demographic**: age, gender, income, education, occupation, family size.
- **Geographic**: city, region, country, urban or rural.
- **Psychographic**: lifestyle, values, interests, attitudes, personality.
- **Behavioral**: purchase patterns, loyalty, how often people use a product, how they decide.

The video applies all four to a fitness app: age groups, urban and rural, enthusiasts and weight-loss seekers, beginners and advanced users.

A fifth cut matters more to a product team: **needs-based** segmentation, which groups people by the need that brings them to the market and the circumstance around it. Three cuts of the same Roost students, in the same month:

| Cut          | Groups                                     | Monthly booking rate |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------- |
| Gender       | women, men                                 | 6.9%, 6.5%           |
| City         | Pune, Bengaluru, Hyderabad                 | 6.4% to 7.0%         |
| Circumstance | moving to a new city, already living there | 12.9%, 4.0%          |

Only the circumstance cut has a gap worth acting on. First-years moving to a new city are 18,000 students making 2,320 bookings a month. Everyone else is 42,000 students making 1,680. The first group books at more than three times the rate, and the reason has a name: they must choose a room they cannot visit, before term starts, often with a parent paying. That is the need Roost's verification serves.

## Which cuts predict

Daniel Yankelovich pushed marketers past demographics in 1964, in the Harvard Business Review article "New Criteria for Market Segmentation": age, sex, education and income no longer said enough. In 2006 he and David Meer returned in "Rediscovering Market Segmentation" (HBR) with a complaint: psychographic profiling had become a mostly wasteful diversion from segmentation's purpose, which is finding customers whose behavior can be changed or whose needs are not being met. They cite a 2004 survey of 200 senior executives: 59% had run a major segmentation exercise in the previous two years, and only 14% said it produced real value.

The jobs-to-be-done section made the same point from the other side: attributes correlate with purchases, circumstances cause them. A useful cut passes three tests:

1. Behavior differs sharply between the groups, not by a fraction of a point.
2. You can say why, so the difference will still be there next season.
3. You can find the group at the moment you need to act.

Needs-based cuts usually pass the first two and fail the third. No ad platform offers "moving to a new city next month" as a targeting option. So practitioners define the segment by need and reach it through proxies: the admission calendar, first-year college WhatsApp groups, a home city on the signup form that differs from the college's city. Demographics and geography are how you reach a segment, not what defines it.

## Predict, then verify

Marketing notices that 18-year-olds book at 8.1% a month against 5.9% for older students, and wants next season's ad budget aimed at every 18-year-old in the three cities. Roost has 20,000 of them. 12,000 are first-years who moved from another city (two-thirds of the 18,000 movers; the rest are 17 or 19) and book at 12.9%. The other 8,000 live with family in the city and book at 1%. Is age the right target?

Answer: No. Age predicts only because it is a proxy. The movers make 12,000 at 12.9%, or 1,548 bookings, and the students at home make 8,000 at 1%, or 80, which is 1,628 in all and where the 8.1% comes from. Inside the age group, the circumstance splits them 12.9% against 1%, so an age-targeted campaign spends 40% of its reach on students who almost never book. It also misses the 6,000 first-year movers aged 19 or older. Target the circumstance through its proxies, admission season and first-year groups at colleges that draw from other states. The principle: segment on the cause of the behavior, and use demographics only to reach it.
