The week the parent page went live in Bengaluru, it showed every listing's last visit date, as the strategy promised. By Wednesday the Bengaluru city lead had taken 40 calls from owners whose pages said "last visited 14 months ago", and 11 of them asked to be delisted. The operations lead heard from support, not from you, that dozens of re-visits were suddenly urgent. Nobody in the PRD review had objected to the page. The two people it hit hardest had not been in the review.

The page was right. The rollout skipped a step: working out who the change touches and what each of them needs from you.

## The power and interest grid

A stakeholder is anyone who can change the outcome of your work or is changed by it. The standard tool for sorting them crosses two axes. Power is how much a person can help, block or reshape the work. Interest is how much the outcome affects them, and so how closely they will watch. The four quadrants each get a treatment:

1. **High power, high interest: manage closely.** Involve them in the decision, show them drafts, meet often.
2. **High power, low interest: keep satisfied.** Spare them detail, but tell them early about anything touching what they care about.
3. **Low power, high interest: keep informed.** They cannot stop the work, but they live with it. Tell them what is coming, before it arrives.
4. **Low power, low interest: monitor.** Check occasionally whether that has changed.

The grid is usually credited to Aubrey Mendelow and dated 1991, but his paper is from a 1981 conference, and it crossed power with dynamism: how unpredictably a stakeholder's position shifts. Gerry Johnson and Kevan Scholes's textbook _Exploring Corporate Strategy_ swapped dynamism for interest, and that version is the one practitioners use.

Roost's map for the parent page, as it should have been drawn before launch:

| Stakeholder                   | Power | Interest | Treatment      |
| ----------------------------- | ----- | -------- | -------------- |
| Founder                       | High  | High     | Manage closely |
| City leads, operations lead   | High  | High     | Manage closely |
| Small PG owners               | Low   | High     | Keep informed  |
| Support team                  | Low   | High     | Keep informed  |
| Lending partner               | High  | Low      | Keep satisfied |
| College accommodation offices | Low   | Low      | Monitor        |

The city leads and the operations lead see the design two weeks before owners do, and agree on the re-visit schedule the dates will trigger. Owners get a message a week ahead: what the date means and how to request a visit. Support gets a short FAQ on launch morning. The lending partner hears nothing, because nothing in this release touches payments.

## Where the grid breaks

**It is a snapshot.** Positions move when the project changes. Mendelow's dropped axis was about exactly this. Redraw the map at each phase, and place people by what this project does to them, not by their title.

**Power is not the org chart.** One small PG owner has no power over Roost. Eleven owners in one locality delisting in the same week do, because supply is half the marketplace. The support lead has no formal authority but holds 40 call logs the founder will read.

**Monitor is not ignore.** Surprises come from the low-low quadrant. A college accommodation office ignores the parent page until the page starts showing charges its own hostel does not disclose.

Two habits keep the map honest: draw it per project, and end every stakeholder conversation with "who else will this affect?"

## Predict, then verify

Version two of the parent page adds a "Pay in instalments" button, backed by the lending partner. The partner has never approved more than 40 applications a day; the forecast for June is 75 a day. The current map puts the partner in keep satisfied. Where should it go, and what changes?

Answer: Manage closely, starting now. The partner's interest just went from low to high: 75 - 40 = 35 more applications a day than it has ever handled, or 35 x 30 = 1,050 extra in June. Its power was always high, since a partner that cannot keep up turns the button into a queue of stalled bookings at peak season. So it joins design reviews, agrees a surge plan in writing, and gets a weekly check-in through June. The partner did not change; the project changed what it asks of the partner. The principle: map stakeholders by what this project does to them, and redraw the map whenever the project changes.
