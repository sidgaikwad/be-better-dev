Ask four people on Roost's team for a one-sentence description. The designer says "the safest way to find a PG." The operations lead says "rooms we have actually checked." An engineer says "a booking platform for student housing." The owner-acquisition lead says "the easiest way for owners to fill beds before June." Each is true, and together they describe four products, each implying a different competitor, home page and next feature. A positioning statement makes the team pick one.

## Moore's template

Geoffrey Moore popularized the fill-in-the-blanks version in _Crossing the Chasm_ (1991), a book about technology companies winning a first mainstream segment:

```text
For (target customer)
who (statement of need or opportunity),
(product name) is a (product category)
that (key benefit, the compelling reason to buy).
Unlike (primary competitive alternative),
our product (statement of primary differentiation).
```

Each blank is one of Dunford's five components from the previous lesson. Moore's template is older; Dunford's process is how you fill it. For Roost:

```text
For first-year students moving to a new city for college, and the parents paying,
who must choose a place to live without seeing it,
Roost is a verified student housing app
that shows only rooms its own team has inspected, with every charge listed.
Unlike a broker or a WhatsApp group,
every Roost listing was checked in person, so the room and the rent
are the ones you get on move-in day.
```

It is not elegant, and need not be: it is an internal decision record, not copy.

## Three tests

**The swap test.** Replace "Roost" with a competitor's name. If the sentence stays true, it is not differentiation. Try a real one: Stanza Living, founded in 2017, leases buildings and runs them as its own managed residences in cities including Pune, Bengaluru and Hyderabad. "Shows only rooms its own team has inspected" survives the swap, since Stanza runs every room it offers. Against Stanza, the difference is the model: Roost lists independent PGs run by their owners at a range of rents, where Stanza offers its own branded residences. So which alternative goes after "unlike"? The one most of your target customers use today. For first-years that is still the broker and the WhatsApp group, so the statement stands; a page for students comparing managed residences needs a different claim.

**The so-what test.** Read the benefit to someone in the segment. "An innovative housing platform" gets a blank look. "The room and the rent are the ones you get" draws a nod from any student shown a room that did not match its photo.

**The proof test.** Every clause needs evidence the team can show: the visit record, the photo date, the charge list. A clause you cannot prove is a slogan.

## Where drafts go wrong

The video's fitness example targets busy professionals with personalized plans that fit their schedule, "unlike generic workout apps". The difference is only implied, and every fitness app says "personalized", so it fails the swap test. The video's project-management example ends in "empowering small teams with intuitive project management tools", which is a tagline: one of many things you write from a positioning statement, not a substitute for it.

Teams also paste the statement onto the home page, where it reads like a form, and widen the target until it covers everyone. Separate statements for separate sides are fine: in a marketplace, property owners have different alternatives (a "to let" sign, a broker) and need their own.

## Predict, then verify

The founder wants the target to read "students across India looking for accommodation", arguing that a bigger target means a bigger business. Last season, first-year students moving to a new city were 30% of Roost's 60,000 monthly active students but made 58% of its 4,000 monthly bookings. Do you widen the target?

Answer: No. First-years are 0.30 × 60,000 = 18,000 students making 0.58 × 4,000 = 2,320 bookings, a rate of 12.9%. Everyone else is 42,000 students making 1,680 bookings, a rate of 4.0%. The narrow segment books at about three times the rate, because verification solves its particular problem: choosing a room it cannot see. Widen the target and every clause goes generic: the need becomes "find accommodation", the benefit "easy", and the swap test fails against every portal. Moore's target is the segment you win first, not a limit on who may sign up; the other 42,000 can still book. Keep the statement narrow and let the business grow past it.
