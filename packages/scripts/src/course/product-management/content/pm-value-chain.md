Intellipaat's video illustrates Porter's value chain with a retail company: fast, cheap supply from good vendors, inventory technology that prevents stockouts, fast delivery with order tracking, a generous return policy. The mapping is neat because a shop moves goods. Roost has no warehouse and ships nothing. So where does a marketplace create value, and where does its money go?

## Nine activities and a margin

Michael Porter introduced the value chain in _Competitive Advantage_ (1985). A firm is a set of activities, and each one either adds something a buyer will pay for or only adds cost. Porter defined value as what buyers are willing to pay, and margin as the gap between that value and the total cost of performing the activities.

Five primary activities make and deliver the product:

- **Inbound logistics**: receiving and storing inputs.
- **Operations**: turning inputs into the product.
- **Outbound logistics**: getting the product to buyers.
- **Marketing and sales**: giving buyers a reason and a way to buy.
- **Service**: keeping the product's value after the sale.

Four support activities run across all five: firm infrastructure (management, finance, legal), human resource management, technology development, and procurement, meaning the buying of inputs for every activity, not the inputs themselves.

Porter's point was not the list. Advantage comes from how each activity is performed and how activities link, so the chain shows where a cost or differentiation advantage actually lives. The generic strategies lesson placed Roost in differentiation focus; the chain shows which activities pay for it.

## Roost's chain, per booking

The unit economics section found that a booking leaves Roost about ₹525 of its ₹720 commission. Mapped onto the chain, with marketing added:

| Activity            | At Roost                       | Cost per booking    |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------- |
| Inbound logistics   | Owner onboarding, verification | ₹15 re-verification |
| Operations          | Search, booking, payment       | ₹90 payment fees    |
| Outbound logistics  | The move-in handover           | not tracked         |
| Marketing and sales | Channels, selling Featured     | ₹167                |
| Service             | Disputes and support           | ₹90                 |

Marketing is the ₹8,000,000 of yearly acquisition spend spread over all 48,000 bookings: ₹8,000,000 / 48,000 = ₹167. So ₹720 - ₹15 - ₹90 - ₹167 - ₹90 = ₹358 per booking remains, before salaries for engineers, the designer, the PM and verifiers (support activities), and before ₹800 to verify each new property.

Two things stand out. Verification and service are where Roost differs from a free listing portal, which lists without visiting and leaves disputes to the student: buyers pay for them. And the payment fee is a procurement cost; no student chooses Roost for its checkout.

## Where it breaks

Porter drew the chain for firms that transform inputs in sequence, and a marketplace does not. Charles Stabell and Øystein Fjeldstad argued in the Strategic Management Journal (1998) that the chain is one of three ways value gets configured: the chain, for transformation; the value shop, for solving a client's problem, as a hospital does; and the value network, for linking customers to each other, as a bank or phone company does. Roost is mostly a network, valued for the matches it makes, with a shop inside it: each verification visit solves one family's problem, whether this room is real.

The chain still earns its place as a checklist: list every activity with its cost and ask whether a buyer would pay for it. At Roost, outbound logistics is move-in day, which nobody owns or measures, a gap worth finding.

## Predict, then verify

Finance needs ₹1,000,000 a year cut. Option A replaces re-verification visits, ₹15 per booking or ₹720,000 a year across 48,000 bookings, with owner-uploaded videos reviewed remotely at a quarter of the cost. Option B renegotiates the payment contract from 1% to 0.7% of the ₹9,000 Roost collects. Which do you take?

Answer: B. The payment cut saves 0.3% x ₹9,000 = ₹27 a booking, and ₹27 x 48,000 = ₹1,296,000 a year, clearing the target alone. The video option saves 75% of ₹720,000 = ₹540,000, falls short, and weakens the activity Roost's differentiation rests on: a video is filmed by the owner, the party the visit exists to check. Under a differentiation strategy, cut cost in activities buyers never see and protect the ones they pay for. That is Porter's cost and differentiation logic applied one activity at a time.
