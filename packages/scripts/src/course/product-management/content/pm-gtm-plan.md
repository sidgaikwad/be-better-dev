The video's instructor lists twelve components of a go-to-market plan and illustrates each with a different company: Zoom for the value proposition, Tesla's direct showrooms for distribution, Netflix's tiers for pricing, Dropbox's referrals for acquisition, Salesforce's success programs for retention. Each is sensible alone. Stitch them together and you get a product sold like a Tesla, priced like Netflix and spread like Dropbox: a plan for no customer in particular.

That is how most go-to-market plans fail. Twelve boxes get filled by different people, each answer reasonable, none derived from the others.

## Twelve components, five questions

A go-to-market (GTM) plan is how a product reaches the people who will pay for it. The video's twelve components group into five questions:

1. **Who is it for?** Target market, customer segmentation.
2. **Why us?** Value proposition, positioning and messaging.
3. **How do they find and buy it?** Sales and distribution channels, pricing, marketing and demand generation, customer acquisition.
4. **Why do they stay?** Retention and customer success.
5. **How will we know, and what could stop us?** Metrics and KPIs, competitive analysis, risks and contingencies.

The list is common practice, not one author's framework, and the grouping matters more than the count: each question depends on the one above it.

## The ideal customer profile at the center

The answer to "who is it for" must be sharp enough to derive everything else from. Practitioners write it as an ideal customer profile (ICP), a term from B2B sales with no single author: a description specific enough that a stranger could say whether a given person fits. Roost's, for its home market:

```text
Ideal customer profile: Roost, Pune
Who:      first-year undergraduate admitted to a Pune college,
          moving from another city
When:     searches in May and June, moves in July
Budget:   ₹7,000 to ₹11,000 a month, paid by a parent
Trigger:  admission confirmed, no college hostel seat
Today:    asks seniors in WhatsApp groups, tours brokers' rooms
Fear:     losing a deposit on a room that does not match its photos
```

Now read the plan off it. The parent pays, so the message must reassure a parent, as the lesson on buyers and users argued. The student is in college WhatsApp groups before arriving, so channels start there. The trigger is an admission, so the calendar is fixed: a campaign in October reaches nobody. The fear is the deposit, so the value proposition is verification, and the metric that matters is bookings, not installs.

Swap in a young professional moving for a first job and nearly every line moves: the search runs all year, the renter pays, and commute outranks safety checks. Same product, different plan. That is the test: if changing the ICP would not change the other components, they were not derived from it.

The video's hostel-app PM, Priya, makes the same move: she rejects "everyone" and narrows to college students in tier-1 cities, where students arrive from across India.

## Where the checklist breaks

- **No objective.** The video's own step list starts with "define objectives" (its example: a fitness app aiming for 100,000 downloads in six months), yet the twelve components contain no target. Without one, no row can be judged.
- **No order.** Presented flat, the components get filled in parallel. Fill them top down: ICP and objective, then positioning, then channels and budget.
- **The bottom rows get skipped.** Metrics, competitors and risks are where plans run thinnest. The course's instructor adds support: in his experience it is the piece teams overlook most.

## Predict, then verify

Roost is planning next season in Pune with a ₹1,500,000 budget. The marketing lead wants the target market to be "everyone aged 18 to 30 renting in Pune", which Roost estimates at 400,000 people, instead of the roughly 50,000 incoming students who need a room. Bigger market, same budget: which do you plan for?

Answer: the 50,000 students. The larger group is not one market but at least two ICPs with different calendars, buyers, channels and messages, so one budget buys two half-plans. Spread across 400,000 people all year, ₹1,500,000 is ₹3.75 a person; concentrated on 50,000 students in a twelve-week season it is ₹30 each, eight times as much. Roost's supply (verified rooms near campuses) and positioning (trust for parents) also fit students, not commuters. The principle: a GTM plan is derived from one ICP, and widening the target does not enlarge the budget, it dilutes it. Add a second ICP once the first one's channels work.
