A rival app and Roost set the same goal for the season: more bookings. The rival pays ₹500 cashback on every booking. Roost spends its money on verification visits and a page parents can check before they pay. Same goal, opposite actions. What differs is each team's belief about what stops students booking, the part most strategy documents leave out.

## The kernel

Richard Rumelt, in _Good Strategy Bad Strategy_ (2011), treats strategy as a response to a challenge. Its core, which he calls the kernel, has three parts:

1. **Diagnosis**: an explanation of the nature of the challenge. It names the obstacle that matters most.
2. **Guiding policy**: the overall approach chosen to overcome that obstacle. Like a guardrail, it rules out many actions without listing them.
3. **Coherent actions**: steps coordinated with one another to carry out the policy.

Rumelt's own example, from his 2011 McKinsey Quarterly article on the book, is Nvidia. The diagnosis: we are losing the performance race. The guiding policy: release a faster chip at three times the industry's usual pace. The actions: three development teams on overlapping schedules, heavy investment in simulation so chips and drivers were not delayed, and taking driver development back from the board makers.

Roost's kernel, built from discovery's evidence:

```text
Diagnosis
  A family pays ₹27,000 upfront for a room nobody in it has
  seen, and fake listings have taught it that photos lie.
  So it trusts people who have seen the room: 65% of the
  students who searched Roost and booked elsewhere used a
  WhatsApp group or a broker. Inside Roost, half of 8,000
  monthly booking requests fall through, and "my parents
  won't pay until someone has seen the room" came up in 9
  of 14 interviews. The obstacle is trust, not supply.

Guiding policy
  Make Roost's visit stand in for the family's. Compete on
  the truth of each listing, not on listing count or
  discounts, and grow only as fast as verification can.

Coherent actions
  1. Visit every property before it goes live
  2. Record every charge at the visit; show one monthly cost
  3. A parent page: visit date, dated photos, charges, pay
  4. Reviews only from students who booked and moved in
  5. Open a city only after its first properties are visited
```

The actions cohere because one visit feeds three of them, producing the photos, the charge list and the parent's report, and the reviews then confirm or correct it. A cashback would reinforce none of them.

## The video's five misconceptions

The video defines strategy as a deliberate plan of action to reach long-term goals, then lists five misconceptions. Each gets sharper against the kernel:

- **Strategy is not a plan.** A plan schedules actions; the kernel is the argument for choosing them.
- **It is not static.** When the diagnosis changes, the policy changes with it.
- **It is not only for leaders.** Roost's diagnosis came from interviews and verification visits, not the board.
- **It is not the same as goals.** Roost and the rival share a goal; the diagnosis is what differs.
- **More strategies are not better.** One guiding policy concentrates scarce people and money; five spread them thin.

What the video's definition skips is the diagnosis, and it is also where the kernel breaks: a confident but wrong diagnosis produces coherent actions aimed at the wrong obstacle. That is why Roost's diagnosis cites its evidence.

## Predict, then verify

The founder disputes the diagnosis: "1,200 properties for 60,000 students. Our problem is supply." He wants 2,400 properties by June, which means about 1,600 visits (a quarter fail) at ₹600 each, or ₹960,000. In a typical month 40,000 students search, 2,400 of them (6%) find no room in budget near campus, 8,000 send booking requests, and 4,000 book. Which diagnosis do the numbers support?

Answer: trust. Suppose doubling supply gave all 2,400 empty-handed searchers a room. At the usual 20% request rate they send 480 requests, and at the usual 50% conversion that is 240 more bookings a month, a 6% gain. The larger loss is downstream: 4,000 students a month find a room they want enough to request, then do not book. Moving request-to-booking from 50% to 60% adds 800 bookings, more than three times as many. Supply still matters where searches come up empty, so target visits there instead of doubling everywhere. The principle: the diagnosis decides where effort goes, so test it against where the losses actually are before you write a policy.
