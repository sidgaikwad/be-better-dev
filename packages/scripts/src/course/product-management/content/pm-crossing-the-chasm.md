The course's instructor describes a pattern among Indian startups. Launch a pilot in Bengaluru, where people eager to try new technology are concentrated, watch how they react, fix what needs fixing, then widen: to the early majority, people like the instructor, and eventually to laggards, like the instructor's mother. It is a sound order with a trap inside it. The people who make a Bengaluru pilot succeed are the people least like everyone who comes after them. Two theories explain why.

## Rogers' five groups

Everett Rogers' _Diffusion of Innovations_ (1962) drew on hundreds of studies of how new ideas spread, including Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross's 1943 study of Iowa farmers adopting hybrid seed corn. Rogers sorted adopters by when they adopt, measured in standard deviations from the average adoption time. The famous percentages come from the normal curve, not from counting anyone:

```text
Adoption time vs the mean    Normal curve   Rogers' band
More than 2 SD early             2.3%       Innovators, 2.5%
1 to 2 SD early                 13.6%       Early adopters, 13.5%
Up to 1 SD early                34.1%       Early majority, 34%
Up to 1 SD late                 34.1%       Late majority, 34%
More than 1 SD late             15.9%       Laggards, 16%
```

So "16% of any market are enthusiasts" is a convention built into the definition, not a finding. A real product's adopters can be skewed or split into two humps. Measure your own.

Rogers also named five attributes that speed adoption: relative advantage, compatibility, low complexity, trialability and observability. For Roost, observability is high, because juniors see where seniors live, and trialability is low: a student books a room once a year and cannot try one for a week first.

## Moore's chasm

Rogers drew one smooth curve. Geoffrey Moore, in _Crossing the Chasm_ (1991), argued it has a crack, between the early adopters and the early majority, at about the 16% mark. His groups differ in what they buy:

- **Visionaries**, his early adopters, want a breakthrough, accept an incomplete product, and will do work to make it succeed.
- **Pragmatists**, his early majority, want proven results, a complete "whole product", and references from people like themselves.

A visionary's enthusiasm is therefore a poor reference for a pragmatist, and a product can stall at the crack while its first users rave. The video presents the chasm as part of the adoption lifecycle in general; it is Moore's addition, written about technology sold to businesses. The choosing-a-segment lesson already used his cure, the beachhead. What carries over to Roost is the diagnosis. Students may try a new app because a senior mentioned it, but parents, who often pay, behave like pragmatists: in 9 of Roost's 14 student interviews, "parents won't pay until someone has seen the room" came up. For that buyer, the verification visit is part of the whole product.

## What a Bengaluru pilot proves

A pilot in the most eager city shows that visionaries want the product. It says little about pragmatists, because they are not in the sample, and they wait for references the pilot's users are poor at giving. Three habits fix most of the damage. Read pilot results by segment, not as one total. Treat the pilot's conversion as a ceiling. And before scaling, run a second test on ordinary users picked at random, not recruited for their enthusiasm.

## Predict, then verify

Roost pilots a hypothetical roommate-matching feature in Bengaluru with 800 students recruited from college coding clubs, and 40% use it within a month. The founder wants to staff support for 40% of all 60,000 monthly active students: 24,000 users. Roost's records show that 7,200 active students, 12%, opted into at least one beta feature in the past year. What do you plan for?

Answer: About 3,000 users, not 24,000, and a second test before committing more. The pilot measured enthusiasts, and Roost's own beta records, not Rogers' conventional 16%, say how many it has: 7,200. If they behave like the pilot, 40% of 7,200 is 2,880. For the other 52,800 students there is no evidence at all, and pragmatists wait for references from people like them, which coding-club members are not. Staff for about 3,000, then run a month-long test on 800 students picked at random from the active base, and scale support to what that test shows. The principle: early adopters' behavior is a ceiling for the mainstream, not a forecast, so measure the majority directly before spending on it.
