Roost's goal for the season: more booking requests should end in a paid booking. In an average month, 8,000 requests produce 4,000 bookings, so half fall through. The kickoff lists seven things to build: instalments, chat with owners, video tours, a map view, review badges, a price breakdown, faster owner replies. After an hour of argument, nobody can say which problem each one solves.

A flat list hides the reasoning behind each item. Teresa Torres introduced the opportunity solution tree in 2016 to make it visible.

## Four levels, drawn for Roost

Each level answers to the one above:

1. **The outcome**, at the root: a product result the team can move, such as the share of booking requests that end in a booking.
2. **Opportunities**: needs, pain points and desires heard in interviews, phrased the way a customer would say them. Large ones split into smaller ones.
3. **Solutions**: ideas, each addressing one specific opportunity.
4. **Assumption tests**: small experiments on what each solution depends on.

Built from interview snapshots, the top three levels look like this:

- **Outcome:** raise the share of booking requests that end in a booking, now 50%.
  - "I can't tell what I'll really pay each month."
    - An all-inclusive monthly price that owners must fill in.
    - A first-month bill preview on every listing.
  - "The owner never replied, so I booked somewhere else."
    - A WhatsApp alert that reaches the owner within minutes.
  - "My parents won't pay until someone has seen the room."
    - A verification report parents can open from a link.
    - A live video tour with the operations team.
    - A refund if the room does not match the listing.
  - "I can't pay the first month and the deposit at once."
    - Instalments through a lending partner.

Most of the kickoff's ideas now hang under a problem a student described. The map view and review badges do not, so they wait until an interview supports them.

## Choosing where to work

Torres asks you to pick one target opportunity at a time, comparing opportunities with each other, not judging each alone. Her four lenses: sizing (how many people, how often), market factors, company factors, and customer factors (how much the need matters and how badly it is met).

Hidden charges produced the most vivid stories, but the students who told them had booked anyway; the charge hurt on move-in day, so it mainly serves a different outcome, fewer disputes after move-in. "My parents won't pay until someone has seen the room" came up in 9 of 14 student stories, it blocks the payment this outcome counts, and Roost's verification visits give it a head start. It becomes the target.

Only now do solutions compete, and Torres recommends comparing at least three. Side by side, they turn "should we build the video tour?" into "which of these works best?", a better question than yes or no on the first idea anyone had.

## Where the tree goes wrong

The tree is only as honest as its opportunities. Torres lists the usual failures:

- Opportunities invented in a meeting instead of heard in interviews.
- Solutions disguised as opportunities. "I want a map view" is a solution; the opportunity is whatever the map would help with.
- One tree for the whole company, which collapses into a list of everything.
- Overreacting to a single interview by adding a branch for every story.

The worst misuse is drawing the tree backwards from a solution already chosen, so the decision looks researched. The sign: one solution under the target, and it arrived with a sponsor.

## Predict, then verify

The founder wants instalments to be the season's big bet. On your tree, the instalment opportunity came up in 3 of 14 student stories and the parents opportunity in 9. The tech lead estimates instalments at six weeks, and each parent solution at three or less. What goes into this season's plan?

Answer: The parents opportunity, with its three solutions compared through cheap tests. Instalments stays on the tree, visible rather than rejected, but with a third of the evidence at twice the cost it does not get the season. The tree also changes the argument: the founder is no longer defending his idea, but choosing between two customer problems with the evidence beside each. The principle: choose the opportunity first, compare several solutions for it, and never let a solution pick its own problem.
