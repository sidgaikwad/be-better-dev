Roost's April investor update leads with one line: 165,500 registered students, up for the ninth month in a row. But registered students is a running total. It would have risen even in a month where every new student had a terrible first week, so it cannot tell you whether anything is getting better.

## Vanity and actionable

Eric Ries drew this line in a guest post on Tim Ferriss's blog in May 2009, and later in _The Lean Startup_ (2011). Vanity metrics make you feel good but give no guidance on what to do: total hits to a website, or a press release about billions of messages sent. Actionable metrics show cause and effect. His examples are split tests, numbers per customer rather than totals, and cohorts, where each month's new customers are tracked as their own group.

Here is Roost's registered total next to one cohort number, the share of each month's new signups who sent a booking request within 30 days:

| Month    | Registered (total) | New signups | Sent a request in 30 days |
| -------- | ------------------ | ----------- | ------------------------- |
| January  | 140,000            | 9,000       | 810 (9.0%)                |
| February | 149,000            | 9,000       | 720 (8.0%)                |
| March    | 157,500            | 8,500       | 595 (7.0%)                |
| April    | 165,500            | 8,000       | 480 (6.0%)                |

The total grew every month. The cohort column says new students are a third less likely to act than in January: a problem with a date on it, which the team can go find.

The label matters less than a test you can run on any number. A metric is useful if some movement in it would change a decision. Before a number goes on a dashboard, write down what you would do if it rose and if it fell. If both answers are "keep going", it is decoration. The course's instructor lists five families of post-launch metrics (customer, financial, operational, market, usage) and says to pick what suits your business. This test is how you pick.

## Leading and lagging

A lagging indicator reports a result that has already happened: commission this month, bookings this season. A leading indicator moves earlier and predicts it. Amplitude's North Star Playbook makes the point about revenue metrics: they tell you what happened, not what will.

For Roost the lag is severe. The season's bookings are known in September, when nothing can be done about them. What is visible in April sits earlier in the chain:

| Metric                         | Kind            | Decision it can change                     |
| ------------------------------ | --------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Registered students (total)    | Lagging, vanity | None                                       |
| Commission this month          | Lagging         | Next season's budget                       |
| Owner replies within 24 hours  | Leading         | Which owners operations calls this week    |
| New signups who send a request | Leading         | Whether the latest onboarding change stays |

Leading and lagging is a spectrum, and "leading" is a claim that needs evidence. Owner reply rate earns the name only if last season's data shows it moved before bookings did. Recheck it every season: if students start calling owners directly, the reply rate may stop predicting anything.

## Predict, then verify

It is May. After an Instagram campaign, app downloads rose from 18,000 to 25,000, up 39%, and the marketing lead wants to double the budget for June. The same week, as new owners joined, the share of booking requests answered within 24 hours fell from 78% to 61%. You have one operations sprint and one budget conversation. What do you act on?

Answer: the owner reply rate, and hold the campaign budget until it passes the decision test. Downloads is the vanity number here: it would be reported as a win whether or not anyone booked. Put it per student and it looks different. Of the 7,000 extra students, 2% sent a booking request in their first two weeks, against 5% of other May signups over the same two weeks, so the campaign produced 140 requests where 7,000 typical students would produce 350. The reply rate is leading and decision-changing. If June brings 12,000 booking requests, 78% means 9,360 answered within a day and 61% means 7,320, so 2,040 students in peak season wait a day or more. Spend the sprint on reminders and calls to slow owners. The principle: a metric earns attention when its movement would change what you do.
