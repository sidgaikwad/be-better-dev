It is 20 March. A coaching institute that sends Roost students, one of its cheapest channels at about ₹300 a booking, prints 5,000 brochures on 10 April and wants one line in them: "Pay your deposit in instalments this season." The lending partner signed last week. Instalments sits in Next: nobody has tested whether families finish the partner's application, or whether the partner pays owners on time. The founder says print it. The tech lead says no date before discovery. Both are arguing about when a date belongs on a roadmap.

## Why dates cause trouble

The first lesson of this section gave Cagan's two inconvenient truths: at least half of ideas will not work, and those that do usually need several iterations. A date on an untested feature promises both unknowns at once. Two more problems make it worse:

- **Dates travel without their caveats.** The brochure will not print "subject to discovery". Whoever reads the date last reads it as a promise.
- **One slip spends trust on every other item.** After a missed date, readers discount all the dates, including the real ones.

## When a date is real

A date is real when the world sets it and missing it has a cost you can name: a conference, a contract, the day a regulation takes effect, a season. Roost has exactly one. Of about 48,000 bookings a year, about 26,000 land from June to August, 54% in three months. A feature that misses the season does not arrive a little late; it waits nine months for the next peak.

Marty Cagan's answer for a real date is the **high-integrity commitment** (SVPG, December 2012). His diagnosis is that the grief about commitments comes from _when_ they are made: too early. So the team asks for a short, bounded time for discovery, enough to know the solution is valuable, usable, feasible and viable, with engineers judging the timing, and then commits. The commitment goes on the roadmap marked as one, and it should be rare, a few a season. A real deadline sets when discovery must finish; it does not justify promising before discovery starts.

## One roadmap, several readers

_Product Roadmaps Relaunched_ (O'Reilly, 2017) recommends tailoring the roadmap to each audience. The discipline is that every version is cut from one source. A version may leave detail out; it may never add a promise the source does not hold. Instalments on 20 March, four ways:

| Reader                   | Needs                                      | Instalments appears as                                          |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Engineers and design     | Now in detail, dependencies                | Next: partner API and family prototype, two weeks               |
| Founder                  | Objectives, themes, decisions due          | Families who cannot pay ₹27,000 at once; go or no-go on 3 April |
| Partners and owner sales | Themes, Now and Next, committed dates only | Working with a lender so families can pay in parts; no date yet |
| Students and owners      | What is live or committed                  | Nothing yet                                                     |

A PM who gives sales one date and engineering another does not have two views of a roadmap. They have two roadmaps, and one of them will be broken in public.

## Predict, then verify

Discovery takes two weeks, to 3 April. If it passes, three engineers build the thin slice in six weeks, to 15 May, then a two-week pilot in Pune runs to 29 May. The brochure prints on 10 April. What goes in the brochure, and when do you decide?

Answer: decide on 3 April, not on 20 March, and print a date only if discovery passes. The printing deadline leaves a week after discovery ends, so the team loses nothing by waiting for evidence. If families finish the application and the partner pays owners within a working day, commit to "instalments in Pune from 15 June". The pilot ends on 29 May, so that date has 17 days of slack, and it still catches July and August, the bulk of the season. If discovery fails, the brochure says nothing about instalments and no promise breaks. Printing "this season" on 20 March would commit Roost's most seasonal feature to two unknowns in 5,000 copies nobody can recall. The principle: a date is earned by discovery, and a real deadline decides when discovery must end, not what you may promise before it.
