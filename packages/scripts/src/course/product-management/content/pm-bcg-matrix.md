The course's instructor gives the class an exercise: place Reliance's businesses on the BCG matrix. His answers: Jio and Retail are stars; the core oil and petrochemicals business is the cash cow; media is the dog. It is a good exercise with one gap: the matrix is two measurements per business, and nobody measured anything.

## Two numbers per business

Bruce Henderson, founder of the Boston Consulting Group, set out the growth-share matrix in a 1970 essay, "The Product Portfolio". The axes:

- **Relative market share:** your share divided by your largest rival's. At 2.0 you are twice the next player's size; at 0.5, half the leader's. It stood in for cost: the biggest producer has the most experience, so the lowest costs.
- **Market growth:** how fast the whole market grows. It stands for cash needed: holding share in a growing market means adding capacity.

The four cells, in cash terms:

1. **Stars**, high share and high growth, report profits but may not fund themselves. When growth slows, they become cows.
2. **Cash cows**, high share and low growth, throw off more cash than holding their share requires.
3. **Question marks**, low share and high growth, need far more cash than they make. Funded, they can become stars; starved, they fall behind.
4. **Pets**, low share and low growth: Henderson's word, where later textbooks and the video say dogs. He called such a product "essentially worthless, except in liquidation."

The logic is a flow: cows fund question marks into stars, and stars become the next cows. The familiar cutoffs, 10% growth and a relative share of 1.0, are convention; Henderson gave none.

The video pairs the matrix with strategic business units: divisions with their own market, resources and profit, like Procter & Gamble's beauty and grooming units. The matrix works at that level, because a unit needs a market to hold a share in. For Roost, a city is the nearest thing (Roost's estimates):

| City      | Share | Largest rival | Relative share | Growth | Cell          |
| --------- | ----- | ------------- | -------------- | ------ | ------------- |
| Pune      | 30%   | 10%           | 3.0            | 0%     | Cash cow      |
| Bengaluru | 18%   | 12%           | 1.5            | 10%    | Star          |
| Hyderabad | 2%    | 8%            | 0.25           | 12%    | Question mark |

Pune's 30% is 15,000 bookings of the 50,000 students who need a room there each year, a number that has not moved. Hyderabad's 2% is 640 first-season bookings of 30,000.

## Where it breaks

BCG revisited its own matrix in 2014. In 75% of industries, the average time a business stayed in one quadrant had halved, from about four years in 1992 to under two in 2012, and relative share no longer predicted lasting competitiveness. A classification is a snapshot that expires; redo it every year.

The market definition decides the cell. Jio's market could be mobile connections, mobile data, or all of Indian telecom. Each has its own growth rate and its own largest rival, so each can put Jio in a different cell. The instructor's placements were really choices of market.

Labels also act on the business: a unit called a dog gets starved and declines on cue, as the maturity and decline lesson warned. And the matrix treats units as independent, blind to a brand or customers they share.

## Predict, then verify

Roost has ₹3,000,000 of growth money for its three live cities next year, with Chennai budgeted separately. The founder proposes splitting it by bookings: of about 48,000 a year, Pune has 15,000, Hyderabad 640 and Bengaluru 32,360. Roost's Hyderabad plan says catching the 8% leader, 2,400 bookings, needs about ₹1,500,000 of verification and channel spend. What do you propose?

Answer: allocate by position, not size. Split by bookings, Pune gets 31.25%, or ₹937,500; Bengaluru 67.4%, or ₹2,022,500; Hyderabad 1.3%, or ₹40,000, about one verifier's monthly pay. That is the worst outcome for a question mark: enough to stay alive, not to win, which is how question marks become pets. Pune, the cash cow, needs no growth money; what holds its share, verification above all, sits in its operating budget. Give Hyderabad ₹1,500,000 in two halves, the second only if 70% of its owners say they will list again, and Bengaluru the other ₹1,500,000 to hold share as its market grows. If Hyderabad fails the test, exit rather than keep it on a drip. The principle: fund a question mark to win, or not at all.
