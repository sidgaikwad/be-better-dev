Apple sold 231.2 million iPhones in fiscal 2015. Over the next three years it sold 211.9 million, 216.8 million and 217.7 million. In November 2018 it told investors it would stop reporting unit sales, saying a unit of sale was less relevant than it used to be. iPhone revenue still rose 18% in fiscal 2018, on higher average prices. Nothing was failing. The product was mature, and Apple changed what it measured to match.

## Maturity: keep what you have

The course's instructor describes maturity as stable sales, high adoption and a known brand in a saturated market with the fiercest competition. Retention matters more than acquisition, since few new customers are left, and margins shrink as rivals match each other. The PM's work turns to retention, performance, and new use cases or markets. His example is the iPhone, kept relevant by steady upgrades and heavy marketing; the risk he names is complacency.

Levitt's 1965 article gives the working tool. He showed how DuPont kept nylon growing through four levers: more frequent use among current users, more varied use among current users, new users, and new uses for the basic material. Applied to Pune, Roost's first city:

| Levitt's lever    | Roost in Pune                              |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| More frequent use | Second-year students rebook when they move |
| More varied use   | Short stays for summer interns             |
| New users         | Young professionals in their first job     |
| New uses          | Rent reminders for owners' current tenants |

Start at the top: current users are the cheapest to reach, because they already trust you.

## Decline: four ways out

Decline comes when needs shift or a better substitute arrives. The instructor's examples are the analog watch, fading slowly as smartwatches replace it, and the DVD player, which he says almost disappeared overnight with streaming. Not quite: Netflix began streaming in 2007 and kept mailing discs alongside it for sixteen years, until September 29, 2023.

The instructor offers three responses: phase out, sell to a niche, or revamp. The standard list comes from Kathryn Harrigan and Michael Porter's "End-game strategies for declining industries" (HBR, 1983):

1. **Leadership:** stay and win the remaining demand as rivals leave.
2. **Niche:** hold a segment whose demand will last, and defend it.
3. **Harvest:** cut investment and take cash while it lasts.
4. **Divest quickly:** sell or close early, while the business is still worth something.

Their point was that decline does not hurt every competitor equally, so the choice depends on your position. A revamp is really a new product starting its own introduction.

The instructor's exit example is Windows 8.1, whose support ended on January 10, 2023, as Microsoft moved to modern platforms. It was an exit run by calendar: under Microsoft's Fixed Lifecycle Policy the date was published years ahead, and no paid extension was offered. Nobody was surprised.

## A lens, not a route

Not every product follows the curve. The instructor notes that Coca-Cola's original formula has stayed mature for decades, and that Coke Energy skipped from introduction to decline. It reached US shelves in January 2020, and in May 2021 Coca-Cola said it would discontinue it in North America, where it had failed to gain traction: a failed introduction, not a decline. In 1976 Nariman Dhalla and Sonia Yuspeh argued in HBR that the stages lack empirical support, and that treating a brand as declining can make it decline. Use the stages to name the decision in front of you, not to predict the future.

## Predict, then verify

Pune bookings have been flat at 15,000 a season for two seasons. Roost estimates that about 50,000 incoming students need a room there each year, a number that has not moved. The founder calls Pune "in decline" and wants to move half of Pune's verification team to Hyderabad. What do you tell them?

Answer: Pune is mature, not declining, and moving the team would make it decline. Decline means the market shrinks or a substitute takes share. Here both held: 15,000 of 50,000 is a 30% share in each season. Verification is why students trust Roost over brokers, so cutting it is the self-fulfilling label Dhalla and Yuspeh warned about. Run the maturity playbook instead, starting with Levitt's cheapest lever, second-year rebookings. Reconsider if share falls for two seasons or colleges add hostel seats faster than intake grows. The principle: diagnose the stage from the market and your share, not from your own growth rate.
