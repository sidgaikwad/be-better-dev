Roost's team wrote its job on the wall: "Students want to use Roost to find hostels easily." Two weeks later the designer had a faster search and an engineer a comparison view, each sure it served the job. The sentence allowed both, because it names the product, "easily" measures nothing, and no survey could ever show it had been done well.

## Verb, object, context

Tony Ulwick, who founded the consultancy Strategyn in 1991, gave the jobs idea its strictest grammar in Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI): an HBR article, "Turn Customer Input into Innovation" (2002), then _What Customers Want_ (2005) and _Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice_ (2016). He writes two kinds of statement.

A **job statement** is a verb, an object and a contextual clarifier, with no product and no adjectives. Strategyn's example: "listen to music while commuting to work".

A **desired outcome statement** is how the customer measures success at the job: a direction of improvement, a metric, an object of control and a clarifier. Strategyn's example: "minimize the time it takes to identify the correct drill bit size for the material being drilled". Outcomes must be measurable and free of solutions, and Strategyn says a job typically has 50 to 150 of them.

```text
Job:  secure a place to live near campus before term starts

Desired outcomes
  Minimize the time it takes to confirm a room matches its listing
  Minimize the likelihood of paying charges not stated up front
  Minimize the number of rooms to compare before finding one
```

## Scoring the outcomes

ODI then surveys customers on each outcome's importance and their satisfaction with how they meet it today, both on a 0 to 10 scale. The opportunity score is importance + max(importance - satisfaction, 0). Suppose 400 first-year students answer:

| Outcome                  | Importance | Satisfaction | Opportunity      |
| ------------------------ | ---------- | ------------ | ---------------- |
| Confirm the room matches | 9.1        | 3.2          | 9.1 + 5.9 = 15.0 |
| No unstated charges      | 8.4        | 4.0          | 8.4 + 4.4 = 12.8 |
| Fewer rooms to compare   | 5.5        | 7.8          | 5.5 + 0 = 5.5    |

The top row is important and badly served, which is the work Roost's verification does. The bottom row is overserved, with satisfaction above importance, so the comparison view the wall sentence produced went where students were already content.

## Two camps

Jobs to be done has two schools, and they have argued in public for years (Ulwick titled one essay "Alan Klement's War on Jobs-to-be-Done").

- **Progress**: Christensen, Moesta, and Klement (_When Coffee and Kale Compete_, 2016). The unit is the struggling moment and the progress sought; the method is switch interviews. It answers why people switch and what they compare you with.
- **Outcomes**: Ulwick. The unit is the functional job and its measurable outcomes; the method is a large survey. It answers which parts of a known job are underserved.

Use progress first and outcomes second. A young product does not yet know which job it is hired for, or by whom, and a dozen switch interviews answer that cheaply. Once the job is settled and the question is which of 40 outcomes to fund, the survey earns its cost.

Treat ODI's success claims as the company's own. In 2010 Strategyn engaged a researcher who interviewed 43 clients: of 21 launches using ODI, the sponsoring companies rated 18 successful (86%), each by a metric it chose. The 17% "traditional" average beside it blends 12 sources, from 0.3% to 67%, that measure different things. It is a vendor's report, not a controlled comparison.

## Predict, then verify

The founder points at the survey: "distance to campus" scored the highest importance of any outcome, 9.0, so the next quarter goes to a commute-time feature. Its satisfaction is 7.5, because the distance filter already works. Another outcome, "minimize the time it takes to get a parent's approval for a room", has importance 7.2 and satisfaction 2.4. Which gets the quarter?

Answer: parent approval. Its opportunity is 7.2 + (7.2 - 2.4) = 12.0, against 9.0 + (9.0 - 7.5) = 10.5 for distance. Importance says what matters; the gap says where it is not yet delivered. Students already choose rooms by distance well. Nobody helps them get a parent 700 km away to say yes. The principle: fund the outcome that is important and underserved, not the one customers rank first.
