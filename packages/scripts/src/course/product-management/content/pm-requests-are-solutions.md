Roost's June feedback also held five feature requests from students, 59 items in all: add a map view (26), sort by distance (14), show the nearest bus stop (9), search by department building instead of by college (6), and show photos of the street (4). At face value that is five features and about ten weeks of work. Asked why, most of the 59 say the same thing: I cannot tell how long it will take me to get to class from this room.

The video's instructor calls this an evergreen problem in product: people state a problem as a solution ("I wish there was a button here that...") or as an end goal, and a hundred requests often stem from one core problem told a hundred ways. Colleagues and executives do it too. The problem behind the request lesson showed why translating them is the PM's job; this lesson shows how.

## A need is not a solution

Griffin and Hauser drew the line in 1993. A customer need, in their definition, describes the benefit wanted in the customer's words and is not a solution: a buyer wants lines on a monitor to look straight with no stair-step effect, not a particular kind of monitor. Theodore Levitt made the same point in 1969, quoting an advertising man, Leo McGivena: a million quarter-inch drills sold, not because people wanted drills, but because they wanted quarter-inch holes.

To find the hole, ask about what the person was doing, never about the feature:

```text
Request:  "Add a map view."
1. What were you trying to do when you wanted it?
     "Work out if I could walk to class from the PG."
2. What did you do instead?
     "Pasted each address into Google Maps, one by one."
3. If you had it, what would be different?
     "I'd drop the far ones before calling any owner."
Need:     Judge the daily commute from a listing, before
          contacting anyone.
```

The questions work on colleagues too. Under question 1, the founder's "build a chatbot for students" becomes "support gets hundreds of messages a night asking if a room is still free": students need to know availability without waiting.

Not every answer lands on the main need: eleven of the 59 said the distance on the listing was simply wrong, a different problem that a map drawn from the same location would not fix.

## Where the translation goes wrong

- **Sometimes the request is the answer.** Eric von Hippel (1986) described lead users, whose needs run ahead of the market and who often build their own fixes. Owners who already send every student a Google Maps pin on WhatsApp are showing you a tested design.
- **Do not make customers do your job.** Never reply "what is the underlying problem?" Ask about the last time it happened, as in the interview questions lesson.
- **Keep the words.** The verbatim request shows what customers expect, and tells you whom to update when a fix ships, even one they did not ask for.
- **Drop the faster horses.** There is no evidence Henry Ford said customers would have asked for faster horses (Harvard Business Review, 2011), and the line teaches the wrong lesson. A rider asking for a faster horse states a real need, arriving sooner. Listen to the need, not the specification.

## Predict, then verify

It is early July, with about 17,000 of the season's 26,000 bookings still to come. A map view takes four weeks, so it ships in August, when most first-years have booked. A travel time from each listing to the student's chosen college, shown on the listing card, takes one week. The founder says 26 students asked for a map, not for travel times, and they should get what they asked for. What ships?

Answer: The travel time, this month. The need under all five requests is judging the commute, and a travel time answers it on the card, where the student decides. Shipped in mid-July, it reaches half of July's 8,700 bookings plus August's 8,300, about 12,600; the map would reach about 8,300. Fix the eleven wrong distances separately, since a travel time computed from a wrong location is wrong too. Then measure: if commute questions fall and map requests stop, the map was only a means; if not, it stays on the list, judged against the same need. The principle: a request count measures the size of a problem, not a vote for the solution it arrived with.
