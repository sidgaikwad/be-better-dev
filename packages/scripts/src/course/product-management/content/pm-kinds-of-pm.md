Here are four job postings, all titled Product Manager. The companies are hypothetical; the postings are typical.

- A 15-person startup: "Own the product end to end."
- A food delivery app: "Own new-user activation."
- A fintech company: "Own the internal payments API used by 30 engineering teams."
- A business software company: "Own the product for enterprise accounts."

Same title, and almost nothing in common from Monday to Friday.

The video's instructor sorts the job by company size. At a startup, PMs are generalists who do everything from market research to specs to testing. At mid-size tech companies the role splits into slices: growth, the core product, platform. At large enterprises, PMs act more like strategists, aligning business units and asking how a feature fits a three-year vision. Across all sizes there are technical PMs, who work on APIs, platforms and infrastructure, and growth PMs, who work on acquisition, activation, retention and revenue. What stays the same, he says, is solving the right user problem at the right time in the right way.

## A week in each

| Kind               | Their customer                  | Judged on                    | A typical Tuesday                              |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Startup generalist | Students and owners             | Bookings                     | Two interviews, a pricing debate, a bug triage |
| Growth PM          | New users at one funnel stage   | One rate, such as activation | Reading an A/B test, designing the next        |
| Platform PM        | Other engineering teams         | Adoption and reliability     | An API review, a migration plan                |
| Enterprise PM      | Companies, through their buyers | Renewals and expansion       | A customer call, a roadmap review with sales   |

Each kind has its own way to fail.

- **Generalist:** breadth. All four risks land on one desk, and the urgent ones win.
- **Growth:** local optimization. One stage of the funnel can improve while the whole gets worse, such as more signups from a shorter form followed by fewer of those users ever booking.
- **Platform:** quiet feedback. Internal teams often cannot switch away, so they complain less and build workarounds instead, and you hear about it late.
- **Enterprise:** promises. The buyer is not the user, and sales has deals riding on dates, which pulls the roadmap toward commitments made in someone else's meeting.

## B2C and B2B

The video draws the other big line through who buys. A B2C product, sold to individuals, makes you obsess over user growth, onboarding and churn, with enough users to learn from data and experiments. A B2B product, sold to companies, pulls you toward sales enablement, feature depth and long-term retention, with few enough customers to know by name. Later in the course, that split gets a lesson of its own.

Roost is both. Its student side, 60,000 monthly users, is a B2C job run on funnels and behavior data. Its owner side, 1,200 properties that each bring a steady stream of bookings, is closer to B2B: relationships, onboarding, and features like Featured listings that owners pay for.

## Predict, then verify

Roost raises money and plans to grow from 5 engineers to 15, with three PMs. The founder proposes one PM per city: Pune, Bengaluru and Hyderabad. The alternative is to split by customer: one PM for students, one for owners, and one for the payments and verification systems both sides use. Which split works better?

Answer: Split by customer. The three cities run one app on one codebase, so three city PMs would each own the same search, the same booking flow and the same payments, and would compete for the same engineers to fix the same problems three different ways. What differs by city is operations and marketing, not the product. Split by customer and each PM gets a clear customer and outcome: the student PM runs a B2C job on the booking rate, the owner PM runs a near-B2B job on listings and owner retention, and the platform PM serves the other two teams. The principle: divide product ownership along customers and outcomes, not along geography or the org chart. The kinds of PM in this lesson are what that division produces.
