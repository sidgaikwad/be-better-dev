First-years moving to a new city are 30% of Roost's 60,000 monthly active students and 58% of its bookings: 18,000 students making 2,320 bookings a month. The founder wants the next push aimed at young professionals moving for a first job: they rent all year, and there are more of them. The calendar supports the case: about 26,000 of Roost's roughly 48,000 yearly bookings land in June to August, leaving about 2,400 a month for the other nine. Both are true; the choice can still be wrong.

## Questions for every segment

The course's instructor assesses each candidate on size, growth, competition, accessibility, cost to serve, and fit with the company's objectives and resources. Roost's three candidates:

## Four ways to target

Then choose how many segments to serve, and how differently. The video illustrates the four standard strategies from Philip Kotler's textbooks with a hypothetical coffee brand, Brew Master:

- **Undifferentiated**: one blend and one message for everyone. Cheap, and it loses anyone with a different taste.
- **Differentiated**: a classic blend, a premium roast and a decaf for different drinkers. Wider coverage, multiplied costs.
- **Concentrated**: an organic, locally sourced coffee for one mountain town. Deep loyalty, until that segment turns away.
- **Micro**: a subscription where each customer picks roast, blend and flavor. The closest fit, at a heavy cost in data and logistics.

For Roost: "hostels and PGs near campus" for anyone; separate products for first-years, seniors and professionals; first-years moving to a new city only; or rooms matched to each student's budget and timetable.

## The beachhead

A five-engineer team cannot afford differentiated targeting. Geoffrey Moore's _Crossing the Chasm_ (1991) makes the case for concentrating: pick one beachhead niche, deliver everything that segment needs, dominate it, then move to adjacent niches, which he pictures as bowling pins, each one toppling the next. He wrote about business technology, but the reason carries over: customers buy on the word of others like them.

That turns the table into a ranking. Pull and fit come first: an urgent need your strength serves. Word of mouth comes next: first-years ask the first-year WhatsApp group, and a year later they are the seniors answering. Size is a floor, not a score: big enough to pay for the next step, and past that, a bigger segment you cannot win is worth less than a smaller one you can.

The cost is the one the video names for concentrated targeting: you rise and fall with one segment, and for Roost that means the calendar. The remedy is the next pin, chosen for what carries over (the same students a year later, the same parents, the same owners), not for size.

## Predict, then verify

The founder proposes putting three of the five engineers on young professionals this year. An 8-week pilot listed 60 verified flats in Bengaluru: 2,000 professionals used it and 30 booked. Rent averaged ₹22,000, so each booking earned 8%, or ₹1,760, against ₹720 for a student room. Each flat took twice as long to verify as a PG. Do you split the team?

Answer: No. Professionals booked at 30 out of 2,000, or 1.5% over eight weeks, about 0.75% a month, so an active professional is worth 0.0075 times ₹1,760, about ₹13 a month. A first-year mover is worth 0.129 times ₹720, about ₹93: seven times as much, at half the verification cost. The off-season is real, but a segment worth a seventh as much per user will not fix it. Keep the pilot as an operations-only probe and spend the engineering on the beachhead and its nearest pin. The principle: choose the segment where your strength meets the strongest pull, win it, then take the neighbor it helps you win.
