The video's example of a SMART goal is "increase market share by 33% in India in the next 6 months". Apply it to Pune, where Roost has about 30% of the 50,000 students who need a room each year: 15,000 bookings. If 33% is relative, the target is 40%, or 20,000 bookings, 5,000 more. If it means percentage points, the target is 63%, or 31,500 bookings, 16,500 more. One sentence holds two targets, one asking for over three times the growth of the other, and nobody notices until the review.

## Objectives and key results

Andy Grove adapted management by objectives at Intel in the 1970s and described it in _High Output Management_ (1983). John Doerr, who learned it at Intel, introduced OKRs to Google's founders in 1999, and his book _Measure What Matters_ (2018) spread them further.

- The **objective** is what you want to achieve: qualitative, significant, time-bound.
- The **key results**, usually three to five, are how you will know. Grove's test: at the end of the period you can say yes or no without argument.

The previous lesson's goals layer, written as an OKR for the June to August season:

```text
Objective  Families book a room they have not seen, without
           sending someone to check it first

KR1  Booking requests that end in a booking: 50% to 60%
KR2  Median days from request to payment: 5 to 2
KR3  Move-ins that meet a charge not on the listing:
     3% to 1%
```

Each key result scores the share of the distance covered. At season's end:

```text
KR1  actual 56%    (56 - 50) / (60 - 50)  = 6 / 10  = 0.60
KR2  actual 3      (5 - 3) / (5 - 2)      = 2 / 3   = 0.67
KR3  actual 1.4%   (3 - 1.4) / (3 - 1)    = 1.6 / 2 = 0.80
Average            (0.60 + 0.67 + 0.80) / 3 = 2.07 / 3 = 0.69
```

## Committed and aspirational

Google's OKR playbook separates two kinds. A **committed** OKR is one the team will adjust schedules and resources to deliver; the expected score is 1.0, and anything less needs an explanation. An **aspirational** OKR describes how the team would like the world to look without a clear route there; the expected average is about 0.7, with high variance.

The season objective above is aspirational, so 0.69 is on target. Beside it sits a committed one: every live listing verified within the last 12 months, 100%. The guiding policy depends on it, so 0.9 there is a broken promise, not a good score. Confusing the two kinds is the first mistake on Google's list: treat a commitment as a stretch and it slips; treat a stretch as a commitment and the team sandbags.

## Results, not tasks

The playbook says key results must describe outcomes, not activities. "Launch the parent page" is a task: it scores 1.0 the day it ships, whether or not one more parent pays. Tasks belong on the roadmap; the key result is what the task is supposed to change.

SMART is a useful checklist for each key result. George Doran introduced it in _Management Review_ in 1981 as specific, measurable, assignable, realistic and time-related. The video, like most versions today, reads achievable, relevant and time-bound. Keep Doran's "assignable": a key result with no owner has nobody to explain a 0.4. And the opening example is not even specific until its unit and baseline are written in: "Roost's share of Pune student room bookings, from 30% to 40%, by 31 August."

## Predict, then verify

Another team's season: KR1 "ship the parent page by 15 May", done, 1.0. KR2 "re-verify the 240 properties that drew complaints", done, 1.0. KR3 "request-to-booking from 50% to 60%", actual 51%, 0.1. The average is (1.0 + 1.0 + 0.1) / 3 = 0.7, which the founder notes is exactly Google's aspirational target. Are you satisfied?

Answer: no. Two of the three key results are tasks, which score 1.0 on delivery, so the average hides that the only result barely moved: one point of the ten targeted. Read honestly, the team shipped everything it planned and changed almost nothing families do. That is evidence against either the solution or the diagnosis, and the season review should ask which. Rewrite it with results only, and move the tasks to the roadmap. The principle: a key result measures what changed, not what was done, and averaging tasks with results turns a failed season into a passing score.
