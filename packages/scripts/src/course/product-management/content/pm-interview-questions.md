The course's instructor has Priya open her interviews with "What's your biggest headache when looking for a hostel?" The answers are the ones Roost's early research found: fake listings, no proper reviews, hidden charges, safety, and a wish to pay in instalments. It is a list of worries with no weights. Every student names safety when asked about headaches in general. Which of the five cost anyone money or a week?

A generic question gets the student's theory of themselves. Teresa Torres puts it plainly: asked directly and out of context, people answer badly, and describe the habits they wish they had. A student who says "I always read reviews" may have booked on a senior's word in a WhatsApp group.

## The workhorse question

The fix is to ask for a story: "Tell me about the last time you..." Torres calls this story-based interviewing; it is Fitzpatrick's second rule as a sentence. A story has a date, a place, other people and a sequence, and each can be probed. Scope it to your goal: a team working on search asks about the last time the student compared rooms, a team working on move-in asks about the first week.

Once the story is running, four probes measure how big the problem is:

- **Cost.** What did it cost you, in money, days, or trouble at home?
- **Frequency.** How often does this happen: once a year, or every term?
- **Workarounds.** What have you already tried to deal with it?
- **Money spent.** Have you paid anyone, such as a broker, to fix it?

The last two are the strongest signals. Fitzpatrick's rule of thumb: someone who has never looked for a fix will not go looking for yours. A broker's fee already paid is a price someone has proven they will pay.

## A question bank for Roost's hostel search

```text
Bad:  Would you use an app with verified listings?
Good: Tell me about the last time you looked for a room.
      Where did you start?

Bad:  How important is safety to you, from 1 to 10?
Good: When you saw the place you picked, what did you check
      before you felt okay?

Bad:  Do you read reviews before booking?
Good: Which reviews did you read for the room you have now,
      and where?

Bad:  Would you pay in instalments if we offered it?
Good: How was the deposit paid last June? Who paid it, and how?

Bad:  Have you had problems with hidden charges?
Good: What did you pay in your first month, line by line?
      Which of those did you know about before you moved in?
```

Each bad question asks for an opinion, a prediction or a yes; each good one asks for an event. The last pair is subtle: the bad version is about the past, but it names the answer you hope for. Asking for the bill line by line lets a hidden charge surface on its own, with its amount.

## Keeping the student in the story

Stories drift back to generalities. When you hear "usually" or "always", steer back: "And that time, what did you do?" Move time forward with neutral prompts: "What happened next?" "Who else was involved?" Hold back "why", which invites a tidy reason made up on the spot. "What made you pick that one over the other two?" gets the same information as an event.

One limit is specific to Roost: students book once a year, so the last time may be eleven months ago and half remembered. Recruit students who searched this season, and treat last year's details as softer evidence.

## Predict, then verify

Twelve interviews with Pune first-years are done. Ten mentioned fake listings as a worry; one has ever met one. Five told a specific story of an unlisted first-month charge, averaging ₹3,500, and two of them have since paid a broker ₹2,000 to avoid a repeat. Your designer wants to work on fake listings because "it came up the most." Which problem has more evidence?

Answer: Hidden charges. Ten mentions of fake listings are ten generic worries resting on one event. Five hidden-charge stories come with dates and amounts, and two with money already spent on a workaround, the strongest signal an interview produces. How often a worry is mentioned measures how easy it is to recall, not what it costs. The principle: weigh interview evidence by specificity and cost, not by how often a word comes up.
