Roost's Hyderabad launch page read "Hostels and PGs near your campus." In its first July, 124 of 200 students (62%) who left without booking gave the same reason in an exit survey: not enough options. Roost had about 300 properties in the city, while the broker at the college gate claimed to have everything and the first-year WhatsApp group posted rooms daily. Measured by count, Roost lost.

The students who did book said the opposite, usually some version of "I knew the room was real." Same product. What differed was what each student decided Roost was, and so what they compared it to.

## What positioning decides

April Dunford, in _Obviously Awesome_ (2019), defines positioning as how a product is the best at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about. Her central point is that customers need context before they can judge anything. Once they file a product under a category, the category tells them its competitors, the features it should have, who it is for and roughly what it should cost.

Dunford splits positioning into five components and insists on the order, because each depends on the one before. For Roost:

1. **Competitive alternatives**: what customers would do without Roost, such as the WhatsApp group, a broker, a senior's old room or a week in a guesthouse. As the real competition lesson showed, rarely a rival app.
2. **Unique attributes**: the operations team visits every property before it is listed, photographs it, and records every charge.
3. **Value, with proof**: the room and the rent on the listing are the ones on move-in day. In Pune, the team turned away one property in five at the visit.
4. **Target customers**: first-year students moving to a new city, and the parents paying. A second-year already in the city can inspect rooms in person and cares less.
5. **Market category**: the frame that makes the value obvious. Chosen last.

The video's instructor starts from customer needs; Dunford starts from alternatives, because a need has no edge until you know what it is compared with. The category is where the hook went wrong:

| Frame                            | What students compare         | How Roost looks           |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Rental listings                  | Number of rooms, lowest price | Small                     |
| Hostel booking, like a hotel app | Instant, nightly stays        | Wrong fit                 |
| Verified student housing         | Is the room real, full cost   | The only one that checked |

## Picking the pond

Dunford describes three ways to compete. Head-to-head claims leadership of a known category, which works only if you are the leader. Big fish, small pond dominates a subsegment of a known category. Creating a new category means teaching buyers a new kind of thing exists, which is slow and expensive.

Her best-known story is the second style. Janna Systems, about 30 people, sold "enterprise CRM" against Siebel, so every sales call became "why are you better than Siebel?" But investment banks loved that Janna modeled relationships between people rather than between companies. So Janna repositioned, same product, as CRM for investment banks. In Dunford's telling, revenue went from about $1.2 million to nearly $80 million in 18 months, and in 2000 Siebel bought Janna in a stock deal its SEC filings valued at about $1 billion.

Positioning has limits. It cannot add an attribute the product lacks, and it expires: if a large portal began inspecting listings, Roost would need a new answer to "compared with what?"

## Predict, then verify

Roost launches in Chennai with 150 verified properties. Search ads cost about ₹5 a click. Frame A, "rooms for rent in Chennai", can buy 50,000 clicks, and past cities suggest 0.2% book, since those visitors compare listing counts. Frame B, "verified PGs for students moving to Chennai", has only 5,000 clicks available, and 2% book. Which frame do you launch with?

Answer: Frame B, though both book the same: 50,000 × 0.2% = 100 bookings, and 5,000 × 2% = 100 bookings. The cost differs. Frame A spends 50,000 × ₹5 = ₹250,000 to earn 100 × ₹720 = ₹72,000 in commission (8% of ₹9,000), a loss of ₹178,000. Frame B spends ₹25,000 for the same ₹72,000, a gain of ₹47,000. And head-to-head on listing count, Roost could only win by listing unverified rooms, giving up the one attribute nobody else has. Choose the frame in which your unique attribute is the thing being compared.
