The course's instructor lists these as disruptive innovations: Uber, Bitcoin, AI, robotic surgery, UPI, Netflix, Spotify, Southwest, the iPhone, Amazon, PayPal, Tesla, Coursera and AWS. If all qualify, "disruptive" just means new and successful, which cannot guide a decision. The theory's own test can:

```text
1. Foothold: did it start with overserved customers or nonconsumers?
2. Performance: was it worse on the mainstream's measure, better on another?
3. Motive: did incumbents have a sound reason to ignore it?
4. Trajectory: did it improve until the mainstream switched?
```

## Sorting the video's list

- **Netflix: disruptive.** Christensen, Raynor and McDonald's December 2015 Harvard Business Review article uses it as the model case.
- **Uber: sustaining, against taxis.** The same article notes it launched in San Francisco, a well-served taxi market, to people already hiring rides, and was rarely called worse than a taxi. The authors call it an outlier, helped by regulation that kept taxis from responding; only against limousines do they see a disruptive path. The video notes Uber began as a premium service: top down, the reverse of disruption.
- **The iPhone: sustaining, against phones.** The article says the 2007 iPhone targeted the customers phone makers already wanted and won on product superiority, then disrupted the laptop as the way people get online. But in June 2007 Christensen told Businessweek the theory predicted Apple would not succeed. Verdict: a success the theory missed, reclassified afterwards.
- **Tesla: sustaining.** The article places its foothold at the high end, buyers willing to spend $70,000 or more, a segment incumbents care about.
- **Southwest: disruptive, new-market.** The video calls it low-end, cheap seats for price-sensitive flyers. Christensen (Forbes, 2009) said Southwest first competed against bus services in Texas: nonconsumers of flying.
- **AWS: new-market, by this lesson's reading.** It rented servers by the hour to startups that hardware vendors barely served, then moved up to large companies.
- **AI, Bitcoin, robotic surgery: category errors.** These are technologies, and the article says a technology is rarely disruptive or sustaining in itself; the business path decides. A surgical robot sold to hospitals for operations they already perform is sustaining.

## Why the label matters

Per the article, small rivals nibbling at your edges can usually be ignored, unless they are on a disruptive path, in which case they can be fatal. A rival wooing your core customers is different again: fight it head-on, and the incumbent usually wins. A disruptive rival cannot be fought that way: matching it means lower margins in the core. Mislabel the threat and you pick the wrong response: a spin-off to fight a price war, or a shrug at a cheap rival that matters later.

## What the critics got right

Jill Lepore, a Harvard historian, argued in The New Yorker in June 2014 that Christensen chose cases to fit. Take Seagate, which the book cast as a drive maker that stumbled: its sales doubled between 1989 and 1990 to $2.4 billion, more than all its US competitors combined. In 2015 Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh (MIT Sloan Management Review) asked 79 experts about the 77 cases Christensen and Raynor had cited. Only 7, or 9%, fit all four of the theory's elements: incumbents improving on a sustaining path, overshooting customers, able to respond, and foundering anyway.

What survives is a real mechanism with a narrower range than its fame: a diagnostic for one threat, a cheap rival your own margins tell you to ignore, not a law that picks winners.

## Predict, then verify

A new rival launches in Pune with verified listings like Roost's and offers owners 5% commission instead of 8%. Its sales team is calling Roost's 60 largest hostels, which bring about 6,000 of Roost's 48,000 bookings a year. The founder says: "We're being disrupted, and incumbents can't win head-on. Let's launch a separate cut-price brand." What do you recommend?

Answer: Fight it in the core, because this is not disruption. The rival targets Roost's best customers with the same product at a lower price: a sustaining attack, the kind incumbents win when they respond. Matching 5% for the 60 hostels costs 3% of ₹9,000, or ₹270, on 6,000 bookings: ₹1,620,000 a year. Losing them costs 6,000 × ₹720 = ₹4,320,000 in commission. A separate brand would tie up engineers for a season. Offer the match only to hostels the rival approaches, and sell what owners actually buy: 60,000 students. The principle: classify the threat before choosing the response, because the label picks the playbook.
