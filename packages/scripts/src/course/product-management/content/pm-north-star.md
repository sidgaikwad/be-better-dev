The video's instructor names two north star metrics in passing: for Slack, messages sent, and for Notion, perhaps notes created. Yet Eric Ries, from the previous lesson, told founders in 2009 that a company boasting about billions of messages sent is showing a vanity metric. Both cannot be right unless "north star" means more than "the big number".

## One metric, one level out of reach

Sean Ellis, who popularized the term, defined it in a 2017 post as the single metric that best captures the core value your product delivers to customers. His examples include Airbnb's nights booked. Airbnb's own 2020 IPO filing treats "Nights and Experiences Booked" as a key business metric, describing it as the single unit of transaction on its platform.

Amplitude's North Star Playbook (2019), co-written by John Cutler, turned the idea into a method. A north star has three qualities: it expresses the value customers get, product and marketing can influence it, and it leads revenue rather than trailing it. Around it sit a few **inputs**, factors teams move directly. The north star is the result those inputs produce. Cutler's warning: if you can move your north star directly, it is probably not a good one. It should sit one level out of reach.

## Pick the game first

The playbook asks teams to name their product's game before choosing a metric:

1. **Attention**: users are absorbed, and more time means more value. Spotify's own glossary says its north star might be time spent listening.
2. **Transaction**: users want the right item and a smooth purchase. Airbnb's nights booked.
3. **Productivity**: users have a job to get done, so time spent is usually a cost.

Check the video's suggestions. Slack is a productivity tool, and a raw count of messages grows with seats and chatter whether or not teams get value: the total Ries called vanity. The version with a track record is per team: Stewart Butterfield said in 2015 that a team that had exchanged 2,000 messages had really tried Slack. That is a threshold each team crosses, not a running total. "Notes created" is weaker, as the video's own hedge suggests, since blank pages count. A productivity north star should count work that got used, such as pages a second person opens (an illustration, not Notion's).

## Roost's north star

Roost plays the transaction game: a student wants the right room and a booking that holds up. The candidates in an average month:

| Candidate               | Value      | Problem                                                                                 |
| ----------------------- | ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Monthly active students | 60,000     | A cousin of DAU, on the playbook's vanity list; a housing search should end, not repeat |
| Commission earned       | ₹2,880,000 | Revenue is the lagging result, not the value                                            |
| Bookings                | 4,000      | Counts a booking into a fake or overpriced PG as a success                              |
| Happy move-ins          | 3,240      | Chosen                                                                                  |

A happy move-in is a booking where the student moved in and raised no dispute within 30 days. It mirrors the playbook's delivery app, which rejected app opens for "happy deliveries", orders with no issues, because those tracked retention. Its inputs are what Roost's teams can move this week: verified listings near each campus, students who save a shortlist, requests owners answer within a day, and complete cost breakdowns.

The metric has costs: it arrives 30 days late, so the inputs steer week to week, and it is seasonal, so compare June with last June. And it will need to change: Amplitude changed its own north star when its strategy shifted toward teams.

## Predict, then verify

The designer proposes a feed of short videos from hostel life. In a two-week test, average session length rises from 6 minutes to 11, while booking requests per 100 searching students stay at 20. The designer calls it a win. Does it move Roost's north star?

Answer: no, and the extra minutes are a warning. Roost plays the transaction game, where time spent finding a room is a cost to the student. Requests stayed at 20 per 100, so everything downstream is unchanged: the feed added 5 minutes per session and zero value. In the attention game the same 5 minutes would be the goal, which is why you name the game first: the same metric is value in one game and cost in another. Keep the feed out of search and spend the design time on an input.
