Last January, Roost's founder paid for a research project: 40 student interviews across three cities and a 60-page report. By May the report had been opened twice. That month's questions were new: why owners were ignoring booking requests, and whether parents would trust a verification report. The report had not asked them, and the next study was budgeted for the following January.

That is the usual fate of discovery run as a phase: it answers the questions of its own month, while the team decides things every week.

## Torres's definition

Teresa Torres, in _Continuous Discovery Habits_ (2021), defines continuous discovery as, at a minimum, weekly touchpoints with customers, by the team building the product, where they conduct small research activities in pursuit of a desired outcome. Each clause rules something out.

- **Weekly.** Not quarterly: the cadence matches how often the team decides things.
- **By the team building the product.** The product trio from the first part of the course, not a research team handing over a deck. Nuance does not survive a handoff.
- **Small research activities.** One interview, one prototype test, one assumption test, none needing a budget approval.
- **In pursuit of a desired outcome.** Every conversation serves a result the team is chasing, such as more booking requests ending in a booking.

## A week of discovery at Roost

```text
Monday     Two interviews are already on the calendar,
           recruited automatically.
Tuesday    Student interview, 30 min. PM asks, designer takes
           notes, tech lead listens. Snapshot together, 20 min.
Thursday   Owner interview and snapshot, same routine.
Friday     15 min together: add what we heard to the
           list of customer problems.
```

The cost for three people: 2 interviews × 30 minutes × 3 people is 3 hours, 2 snapshots × 20 minutes × 3 people is 2 hours, and the Friday review is 45 minutes. That is 5.75 hours of a 120-hour trio week, under 5%. Over 48 working weeks it adds up to 96 interviews, more than twice the January study, each heard firsthand by the people who decide.

The habit dies in the first busy week if someone has to find each participant by hand. Torres's advice is to automate recruiting, so Monday's interview is booked without anyone lifting a finger. The common way is an in-product intercept: one question asking whether the user has 20 minutes to talk, for a small thank-you, with a scheduling link for those who say yes.

At Roost, the intercept appears after a student sends a booking request and offers a ₹300 voucher. At 8,000 requests in an average month, about 1,850 a week, a 1% yes rate gives 18 volunteers for one student slot. Owners come through the operations team, which already visits their properties.

Seasonality is Roost's twist. From September to May few students search, so the intercept moves to students who have just moved in, asking about their first month, and owners carry more of the load.

## Why a phase goes stale

A research phase goes stale in three ways. The market moves after the report is written. The team's questions change as it learns, because each solution raises assumptions nobody asked about in January. And learning is lost in the handoff, since the people who heard the stories are not the ones choosing what to build.

The weekly habit has its own failure modes. Interviews with no outcome behind them become a ritual. Overreacting to the latest conversation, a pitfall Torres names, turns the plan into whatever the last student said. And where customers are hard to reach, in regulated industries or enterprise sales, the cadence may have to stretch to every two weeks.

## Predict, then verify

The founder proposes an annual study instead: an agency runs 40 interviews each January for ₹600,000, and the trio gets its 5.75 hours a week back. The weekly habit costs 96 × ₹300 in vouchers, ₹28,800 a year, plus the trio's time. Which do you choose?

Answer: The weekly habit, and the money is the smaller reason. It produces more than twice the interviews for under 5% of the trio's time, but its real advantage is timing: evidence arrives in the week a decision is made, heard firsthand by the people making it. Keep an agency for a one-off question the trio cannot cover, such as whether to enter a new city. The principle: discovery has to run at the same cadence as the decisions it informs.
