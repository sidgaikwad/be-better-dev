Intellipaat's video offers a finding that looks decisive: 50% of users mention difficulty finding products with the current search. Its instructor reads it as a sign that search is broken and starts digging, rightly, but the number needs one question first: 50% of whom? If 400 people wrote in and 200 mentioned search, that is half of those who wrote, a group the channel chose, not half the users.

Roost's June pile holds 2,620 items: 2,100 support messages, 340 in-app reports and 180 reviews. The problem behind the request lesson read 60 of 212 messages and found 23 about price and 18 about availability: this method in miniature. Here it runs on a month.

## Tag, count, segment, weight

1. **Sample.** Read a random 300 of the 2,620 items.
2. **Split** each item into single observations. One message can hold two complaints, so 300 items give 342 observations.
3. **Tag the type** with the video's baskets: bug, usability, performance, feature request. The type is a first guess at the cause, so hold it loosely.
4. **Name the theme** in the customer's words, as a problem rather than a feature, and group similar ones. Grouping by affinity is the KJ method, formalized by the Japanese anthropologist Jiro Kawakita in the 1960s.
5. **Count, then segment** by persona and by value to the business.
6. **Weight** by severity and rank.

Severity is 3 if the problem cost the student money or a room, 2 if it derailed a booking they then made elsewhere, 1 for an annoyance. Score is mentions times severity:

| Theme, in students' words           | Type        | Mentions | Severity | Score |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------- | -------- | -------- | ----- |
| "Rent went up after I booked"       | usability   | 58       | 3        | 174   |
| "Farther from college than it said" | bug?        | 36       | 3        | 108   |
| "Room was already taken"            | bug         | 47       | 2        | 94    |
| "Let me pay in instalments"         | request     | 24       | 2        | 48    |
| "Photos take forever"               | performance | 41       | 1        | 41    |

Counted raw, slow photos rank third. Weighted, they fall to last, and the distance complaint climbs from fourth to second. Segmenting sharpens it: first-year students, about 30% of monthly active students but 58% of bookings, wrote 30 of the 36 distance complaints and only 9 of the 41 photo ones.

Then the denominator: 36 in 300 items is 12%, or about 314 of the 2,620. That is 12% of students who wrote in, not of students; to size the problem, count it in behavior, such as bookings cancelled for location.

## Frequency is not importance

Griffin and Hauser tested whether customers mention their most important needs most often, and found that "frequency of mention does not appear to be a good surrogate for importance." Counts measure what a channel hears: support hears about rent disputes because rent disputes need support. Hence the severity weights.

The same paper found that one analyst coding transcripts caught 54% of the needs on average, with the qualitative expert at the low end. Have two people tag the same 50 items and compare before trusting anyone's tags, including yours.

## Predict, then verify

In 22 of June's 260 owner notes, the three hostel chains (150 listings, about 500 bookings a month) ask for an occupancy dashboard across their properties: six weeks of work. Meanwhile 54 of the 90 owners who start a listing each month abandon the form, and their feedback says it asks for documents they do not have at hand. Fixing the form takes two weeks. It is the video's classic case: power users want reports while new users struggle to start. Which ships first?

Answer: The form. Roost averages 4,000 bookings from 1,200 listings, about 3.3 bookings per listing a month. If the fix saves half the 54 abandoned listings and they book at the average, 27 listings add about 90 bookings a month, or 90 × ₹720 = ₹64,800 of commission, and the gain stacks every month the fix is live. The chains already list and get bookings, and nothing in the notes says they will leave without a dashboard: it is a convenience for users Roost has already won. That changes if a power user's request arrives with a churn risk you can measure. The principle: rank feedback by the value at stake for the current goal, not by who asks most often or loudest.
