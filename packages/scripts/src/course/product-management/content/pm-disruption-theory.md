Netflix was founded in 1997 around a service most of Blockbuster's customers did not want. You picked a film online and waited days for the DVD to arrive by post, while Blockbuster's customers went home with a new release that evening. Ignoring Netflix was the right call that year, and for years after. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010. Clayton Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation explains how a company can make the right call every year and still lose.

## Sustaining and disruptive

Christensen, a Harvard Business School professor, set out the theory in _The Innovator's Dilemma_ (1997), built on his doctoral study of disk drives. He separated two kinds of innovation.

- **Sustaining** innovation makes a good product better for an incumbent's existing customers, usually its most demanding and profitable ones: a fifth razor blade, a clearer TV picture. Incumbents usually win these fights: in the disk-drive study, only 6% of entrants with a sustaining strategy succeeded.
- **Disruptive** innovation is worse on the measure mainstream customers care about and better on another: cheaper, simpler, more convenient. It starts where incumbents are not looking, in one of two places.

A **low-end foothold** is customers the incumbent overserves: steel minimills began with rebar, the lowest-margin product, which integrated mills gladly gave up, then climbed to beams and sheet. A **new-market foothold** is nonconsumers: personal copiers reached the school librarians and small offices that Xerox had priced out.

Disk drives repeated the pattern. Each smaller size, from 14-inch to 8, 5.25 and 3.5, held less than the last and sold first to a new market: minicomputers, then desktop PCs, then portables. The leaders asked their best customers about smaller drives, heard no, and were right, until they were not.

## Overshoot

The engine: products improve faster than customers' needs do. With illustrative numbers:

```text
Mainstream customers need    100 units, growing 10% a year
Incumbent supplies           150 units, growing 25% a year
Entrant supplies              40 units, growing 30% a year

Entrant meets the need when:
40 × 1.30^t = 100 × 1.10^t
(1.30 / 1.10)^t = 100 / 40 = 2.5
t = ln 2.5 / ln 1.182 = 0.916 / 0.167 = 5.5 years

Incumbent at 5.5 years: 150 × 1.25^5.5 = about 512
Need at 5.5 years:      100 × 1.10^5.5 = about 169
```

By the time the entrant is good enough, the incumbent supplies three times what mainstream customers can use. Extra performance stops earning a premium, and customers choose on price and convenience, the entrant's ground.

## Why incumbents walk away

None of this needs bad managers. Moving upmarket raises margins; defending the low end lowers them. Budgets go to projects with high margins, large markets and known customers. Each decision is sound. Disruption is their sum, which is why Christensen, Michael Raynor and Rory McDonald (Harvard Business Review, 2015) insist it is a process, not an event: the minimill Nucor took more than 40 years to match the revenue of the largest integrated steelmakers.

Run Netflix through the test. Its foothold was movie buffs, early DVD owners and online shoppers. It was worse on what Blockbuster's customers valued, a film tonight, and better on selection. Streaming, from 2007, made it good enough for the mainstream.

## Predict, then verify

Two rivals appear in Bengaluru. A is well funded and offers video tours and a relocation manager for rooms above ₹12,000, Roost's most valuable bookings. B lists shared rooms around ₹4,000 that Roost skips, checked only from owners' photos, and charges owners 5%. A Roost visit costs ₹600, and a new property brings about 5 bookings in its first season. Which rival is the disruptive threat?

Answer: B. Roost skips cheap rooms for a sound reason: 5 bookings × ₹320 is ₹1,600 against a ₹600 visit, leaving ₹1,000 per property, while an average ₹9,000 room earns 5 × ₹720 = ₹3,600, leaving ₹3,000. Cheap rooms return a third as much per visit, so Roost cedes them, as the mills ceded rebar. B is worse on what parents care about, a room someone has seen, but carries none of Roost's visit costs, and the S-curves lesson showed ₹25 automated photo checks improving every season. If B's checks get good enough for parents, it reaches Roost's core with costs Roost cannot match. A is a sustaining attack, the kind incumbents win when they fight back. The principle: judge a rival by its foothold and trajectory, not its size today, and track B's check quality rather than its booking count.
