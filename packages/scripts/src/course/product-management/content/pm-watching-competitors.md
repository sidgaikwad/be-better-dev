The memo from the responsibility without authority lesson, Ben Horowitz and David Weiden's "Good Product Manager/Bad Product Manager", was written at Netscape in the late 1990s, during its browser fight with Microsoft. One line from it: "Bad product managers focus team on how many features Microsoft is building." Good ones, it says, focus the team on revenue and customers. That is not advice to ignore competitors, but a rule about what to watch them for.

## Sources, and their limits

| Source                     | What it shows                                    | What it does not                       |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Pricing page               | How they charge, and whom they target            | Whether anyone pays it                 |
| Changelog and app updates  | What they are betting on, and how fast they ship | Whether anyone uses it                 |
| Job postings               | Where they will spend in six to twelve months    | Whether the plan survives              |
| Reviews of rivals          | What their customers suffer                      | A fair sample: angry people write more |
| Sales and partner feedback | What rivals offer in the field                   | More than an anecdote                  |

For Roost, partners are owners and the operations team: a verifier who hears three owners mention one rival's offer in a week has a signal no website shows.

The video adds competitive intelligence tools, naming Similarweb, which estimates a site's traffic, and Crayon, which tracks changes to rivals' web pages. The instructor also demos an AI tool that writes a weekly digest of rivals' moves, filtered by pricing, hiring, market entry and user feedback, and says it replaces grunt work such as searching Twitter and hunting for someone who once worked at the rival. Such tools make collection cheap, but see only what a rival publishes online.

The richest source is not about the rival at all: win-loss interviews, as B2B teams call them, with people who chose a rival over you, or chose nothing. They tell you why, which no changelog can.

## A move shows intent, not demand

A rival's launch tells you what it believes and what it can afford. It is the rival's hypothesis, and it may be failing quietly. When a rival ships roommate matching, you learn its team thinks roommates matter. You do not learn that students choose a room on it, because launches are public and usage is not. Read moves for intent; read reviews and your own customers for demand.

Reviews of rivals are free research into your category, including stages your own data never reaches. If a rival's one-star reviews keep describing deposits not returned at move-out, Roost's students probably face the same problem eleven months after booking, long after Roost stops measuring.

## Why copying the leader loses

- You ship a release late, after the rival has learned from its version what you have not.
- The feature fits the rival's strategy and customers, which are not yours.
- Matching buys parity at best: a reason not to leave the rival, never a reason to choose you.
- The leader usually has more engineers, which is the race the memo warns against.

Copy only what has become table stakes, something customers now expect of every option, and copy the outcome cheaply rather than the design. Everything else a rival ships is information, not instruction.

## Predict, then verify

The founder wants a competitive intelligence subscription at ₹45,000 a month tracking the websites, prices and job ads of the five apps in Roost's markets. The PM proposes 20 interviews a month with first-years who searched and did not book, at a ₹500 voucher each plus about 12 hours of the PM's time. The competitor sheet says where lost first-years go: brokers 38%, the WhatsApp group or a senior 27%, relatives, hostel seats and walking around 31%, apps 4%. Which do you fund?

Answer: the interviews. They cost 20 × ₹500 = ₹10,000 a month against ₹45,000, but price is the smaller reason. The tool would watch the apps, which took 4% of lost first-years; brokers and WhatsApp groups have no pricing page, changelog or careers site to track. The interviews reach the other 96%, and they surface the apps too whenever a student mentions one. Keep a free check on the five apps: a monthly look at their app updates and job ads, which is how Roost would see a rival preparing to verify listings. The principle: spend watching effort where your customers go and where a rival could take your base, not where monitoring is easiest.
