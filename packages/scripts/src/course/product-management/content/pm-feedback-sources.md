In the first week of July, three people tell Roost's PM what customers are saying, and they disagree. The founder has been reading the Play Store: 72 of June's 180 reviews give one star, and 41 of those say the photos do not match the room. Support logged 2,100 messages in June, the biggest pile about rent that rose after booking. Analytics says 32,000 of the 40,000 students who search in an average month never send a request, and nothing about why.

None of them is wrong. Each channel lets a different group of customers speak and filters out everyone else. Knowing the filter is most of the skill.

## Asked, volunteered, observed

Abbie Griffin and John Hauser's 1993 paper "The Voice of the Customer" describes a customer need as the benefit a customer wants, in the customer's own words. Intellipaat's video lists where those words come from, and they sort into three families by who chooses the topic:

1. **Asked.** Interviews, surveys and focus groups. You choose the question, so you hear only about what you thought to ask. Interviews explain why; surveys count how many, once you know what to count.
2. **Volunteered.** Support messages, Play Store and Google reviews (the video adds sites such as MouthShut.com), in-app feedback, social media, and what owners tell operations, Roost's version of sales calls. The customer picks the topic, so you hear about problems you never imagined.
3. **Observed.** Product analytics, such as Google Analytics or Mixpanel. It covers everyone, including the silent majority, and shows what they did, never why.

Many companies also send a Net Promoter Score survey, one question, how likely you are to recommend the product, scored 0 to 10 (the video says 1 to 10). The video's instructor says it has never helped him: a low score says people are unhappy, not why.

## Who bothers to speak

Outside analytics, the customer decides whether to speak, and that choice bends what you hear.

- **Reviews** crowd the extremes. Nan Hu, Paul Pavlou and Jie Zhang (Communications of the ACM, 2009) showed that product ratings form a J shape, mostly fives, some ones, few in between: only buyers review, and people with middling experiences rarely bother.
- **Support messages** come from students far enough into a booking to get stuck. The 32,000 who left after searching rarely write to support.
- **Focus groups** hear the loudest person in the room. Griffin and Hauser found that two one-hour interviews surfaced about as many needs as one two-hour focus group (51% against 50%), and the expected group synergy never appeared.
- **Surveys** hear the engaged, and what people say differs from what they do. Jakob Nielsen's rule (2001): watch what people do, and do not believe what they say they do, let alone what they predict.

Write down whom each source speaks for:

```text
Roost, June            Items    Who it speaks for
Support messages       2,100    students with a booking under way
In-app reports           340    students on a listing, right now
Play Store reviews       180    students angry or delighted enough
Owner notes from ops     260    owners, mostly the big chains
Interviews                14    students the trio recruited
Analytics                all    every searcher, behavior only
```

A theme is strong when a volunteered channel raises it, an observed channel shows it in behavior, and an asked channel explains it. A theme found in one channel is a lead, not a finding.

## Predict, then verify

The founder wants to hide the 300 listings whose photos are more than a year old, a quarter of Roost's 1,200, until owners reshoot them. His evidence is the 41 one-star reviews. Support has 38 photo complaints out of 2,100 messages, and in-app reports flag 25. It is July, when roughly 8,700 bookings a month land. Do you hide them?

Answer: No, not on this evidence. If those listings carry a quarter of bookings, hiding them puts about 2,175 bookings at risk, or 2,175 × ₹720 = ₹1,566,000 of commission in a month. The reviews are real but tilted: a one-star reviewer is the student most likely to write. Check the claim where everyone is visible: compare "not as shown" cancellations on the 300 old-photo listings with the other 900. If the rates match, the reviews describe particular listings, and operations can re-check the 29 they name this week. The principle: know who a channel lets speak before deciding what it proves, and confirm a loud signal in a channel that sees everyone before acting at scale.
