Picture a team of eight on Slack's free plan before 2022, with no salesperson involved. Someone searches for a decision made in the spring and cannot find it: the free plan searched only a team's most recent 10,000 messages. The team lead upgrades that afternoon. The course's instructor lists Slack's usage-based upgrade among the video's growth loop examples: the more a team uses the product, the sooner it meets the limit, and its own history makes the sale. Since September 2022 the limit has been 90 days of messages instead, with the same shape.

## The product does the selling

Blake Bartlett at OpenView, a venture firm, coined "product-led growth" in 2016 for companies whose product is the primary driver of acquisition, conversion and expansion. Wes Bush's book _Product-Led Growth_ (2019) turned it into a playbook. The go-to-market motions lesson treated PLG as a way to sell; as a growth engine, it is a loop with the product at every step:

1. A user signs up free and reaches value without help.
2. Using the product brings in others. A Slack workspace is useless alone, so every user invites colleagues.
3. Usage grows until it meets a limit tied to how much value the account gets.
4. The account upgrades, and pays more as it grows.

Step 3 is the design decision. The limit belongs on a value metric: something that grows as the customer succeeds. Slack's limit was on history, which matters only once a team relies on the product, while sending messages stayed unlimited. Its Fair Billing Policy also bills paid workspaces only for members active in the last 28 days.

Expect small percentages. Elena Verna, a growth advisor, puts freemium at about 5% of free users converting to paid, opt-in trials without a card at about 15%, and card-required trials at 70 to 80% of the far fewer people who start one. The skimming, penetration and free lesson compared these models. For growth, the point is that freemium's other 95% are not waste if they invite colleagues, create content, or feed a network effect.

## Product-qualified leads

PLG does not remove sales for large accounts. It changes whom sales calls. Tomasz Tunguz described the product-qualified lead in January 2013: a user who has used the product and reached predefined triggers that signal a strong likelihood of paying. His illustration was an Expensify user who had uploaded 10 receipts and invited a friend.

Roost has a PLG surface on its owner side: listing is free, and Featured listings are paid. Suppose Roost defines an owner PQL like this:

```text
owner PQL, checked weekly in May:
  lists 10 or more beds
  has 2 or more empty beds 4 weeks before the season
  logged in 3 or more times in the last 14 days
```

Suppose that of 1,200 owners, 150 qualify. Operations calls those 150 and 40 buy Featured, a 27% rate. Calling all 1,200 would take eight times as long and mostly reach owners who are already full. As Tunguz said, the triggers should come from data: check which behaviors preceded past purchases, and revise them each season.

## Where it breaks

PLG needs a product that shows its value without a demo, used by someone who can adopt it without permission. It fails for software that takes a month to configure or that a compliance team must approve first. Free users also cost hosting and support, so a free tier nobody converts from or spreads is only an expense.

## Predict, then verify

Roost's growth lead proposes a limit: free owners can receive 10 student enquiries a month, and more require a ₹999 monthly Pro plan. In a typical season month, 400 owners exceed 10 enquiries, averaging 18 each. The lead expects 30% of them to pay. Suppose 20% of enquiries become bookings, at ₹720 commission each. Does the limit make money?

Answer: No. 30% of 400 is 120 owners paying ₹999, about ₹120,000 a month. The other 280 owners each have 8 enquiries blocked, 2,240 in all, which at 20% is 448 lost bookings, and 448 x ₹720 is about ₹320,000 of lost commission. Worse, students turned away learn that Roost listings do not answer, which weakens the network effect on the side that was never asked. In PLG the paywall belongs on something that grows with the customer's success, such as visibility, never on the interaction that feeds the other side of the network.
