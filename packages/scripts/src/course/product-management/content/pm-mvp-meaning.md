Roost's planning meeting has accepted the product mix lesson's pick: owner software, rent reminders and tenant records at ₹299 a month, forecast at 300 of the 1,200 owners. Everyone wants an MVP first, and they mean three different things. The tech lead means the full tool with fewer features: seven weeks. The designer means the first version she would be proud to show: ten weeks. The founder means whatever can ship before June. Nobody has said what the MVP is supposed to find out.

## An experiment, not a smaller version

Eric Ries gave the definition most product teams now use in a 2009 blog post, and built _The Lean Startup_ (2011) around it: the minimum viable product is the version of a new product that lets a team collect the most validated learning about customers for the least effort. The unit is learning, not features. "Minimum" is measured against a question: the least work that answers it.

Ries describes a loop, build, measure, learn, and says to plan it backwards: decide what you need to learn, then what to measure, and only then what to build. The question comes from a leap-of-faith assumption, of two kinds: the value hypothesis (people get enough value to act on it) and the growth hypothesis (how new customers will find it).

The video's MVP module gives a practical version: an MVP answers whether the problem is worth solving, and whether your way of solving it points in the right direction. Both belong to Ries's value hypothesis.

For the rent tool, written down before anyone builds:

```text
Leap of faith   PG owners will pay ₹299 a month to stop
                chasing rent.
Question        Of owners who start, how many pay a second month?
Measure         Second-month payments. Not signups, not taps.
Forecast        300 of 1,200 owners is 25%.
Pass            25 or more of 100 owners pay a second month.
Fail            10 or fewer.
Between         Run another 100 before deciding.
Build           The least work that produces this number.
```

The last line names no features on purpose: the build serves the measure, and the measure serves the question.

## Three meanings of one term

The phrase is older than Ries. Frank Robinson, of the product development firm SyncDev, coined it in 2001 for a right-sized first product: big enough to cause adoption, satisfaction and sales, small enough not to be bloated and risky. Ries kept the words and changed the purpose from selling to learning. Everyday use drifted to a third meaning, the tech lead's: version one minus some features. That is neither. It is sized by a deadline and answers no question in particular.

Ries warned that the name misleads. In the same post he wrote that an MVP, despite the name, is not about creating minimal products. Size is relative: IMVU's first MVP took six months, which he counted as fast, since his previous company had taken nearly five years to launch. And if you already know the answer, an obvious problem with an obvious fix, you do not need an MVP at all. Build it.

The video's opening module calls an MVP the simplest version of your product; its later module quotes Ries. Keep Ries's.

## Predict, then verify

Two plans for the rent tool. Plan A, the tech lead's: five engineers build it in seven weeks and launch to all 1,200 owners; second-month payments are known about nine weeks after launch. Plan B: operations offers the tool to 100 owners during property visits and sends their reminders by hand from a shared spreadsheet, charging ₹299 a month. Setup takes two weeks and no engineers. The founder prefers A: a real product, and twelve times as many owners. Which is the better MVP?

Answer: Plan B. Both answer the same question, but B answers it in 11 weeks (2 plus 9) instead of 16 (7 plus 9), with no engineering time against A's 35 engineer-weeks (7 weeks × 5 engineers). If owners will not pay, A has spent 35 engineer-weeks learning it and B has spent two weeks of operations. The bigger sample buys nothing yet: chance moves a count of 25 in 100 by about four either way, so 100 owners separate 25% from 10% easily. B cannot show that software can do the job, but that was never the risk: reminders are easy to build. The principle: minimum is measured against the question. The least effort that answers it wins, and a smaller product is not automatically less effort.
